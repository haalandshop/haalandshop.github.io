Best Things to Buy Right Now — August 8, 2026

Quick take: There is a comfortable assumption sitting underneath almost every shopping decision, and this is the weekend it stops being true. The assumption is that price is the variable. Costs go up, prices go up; costs go down, prices go down; watch the number, and you know what is happening. That model works fine in a calm market and it fails completely in the one we are in now. A week into a serious freight disruption — East Coast ports still closed, Chinese port capacity knocked around by back-to-back typhoons, European inland water levels low enough to constrain barge loading, and ocean rates on the trans-Pacific lane up sharply — almost nothing has moved on the price tag. That is not because the cost has not arrived. It is because the price tag is the last dial a seller turns, not the first. It is the most visible number on the page, the one that anchors every comparison, the one that triggers price-tracking alerts, the one that determines whether a listing still clears an advertised threshold. Sellers will defend it for as long as they possibly can, and they will pay for that defence somewhere else. So the cost goes into the places nobody screenshots. The free-shipping threshold quietly moves from thirty-five to forty-nine. The twelve-pack becomes a ten-pack at the same price. The dispatch estimate goes from same-day to three business days, because the unit is now being picked from a node further away. The returns window shortens from ninety days to thirty, the restocking fee reappears, and the label that used to be prepaid is now deducted from your refund. Every one of those is a real price increase. None of them shows up as one. And the practical consequence for this weekend is that two listings at an identical price can be twenty dollars apart in what they actually cost you, and the gap is entirely in fields you can read for free before you commit. This is also move-in weekend for a large share of the back-to-school wave, which means a lot of people are buying in a hurry, in bulk, and with a deadline — the exact conditions under which nobody checks the threshold. Read the whole contract. The number is only the headline.

What’s Hot This Week

The headline price is holding and everything around it is moving. This is the single most useful observation available right now. Across the categories most exposed to ocean freight — cookware, furniture, exercise equipment, large-format storage, anything shipped in a big box — sticker prices are broadly where they were a fortnight ago, while shipping thresholds, pack sizes and dispatch estimates have all drifted in the seller’s favour. If you have been waiting for prices to rise before acting, you are watching the wrong field, and by the time that field moves the cheap options will already be gone.

Move-in weekend is the demand peak, and it is not a buyer’s market. The first half of August is when the bulk of the dorm and first-apartment wave actually happens, and this is the heaviest weekend of it. Demand-side pressure is high, intent is high, price sensitivity is temporarily low, and sellers know all of it. Dorm lighting, desk risers, storage bins, mattress toppers, small kitchen appliances, laptop bags and surge protectors are all moving hard. There is no discount coming in the next ten days for anything on that list, and by late August the good sizes and colours will be picked over rather than reduced. Buy what you actually need now and stop waiting for a markdown that the calendar does not support.

Cooling is in genuine clearance, and this is roughly the last honest week. Fans, portable air conditioners, cooling bedding, evaporative units and personal chillers are being cleared properly rather than cosmetically, because sellers are looking at storage costs on a category that has maybe three weeks of natural demand left. The catch is that the clearance is real on the units nobody wants and thin on the ones everybody does. If a specific model in a specific size is discounted and in stock, take it. The pattern from here is that the discounts get deeper and the selection gets worse, and after about the third week of August the selection problem dominates.

Early autumn arrived about a week early and is not discounted at all. Corduroy and waxed-cotton bags, indoor lighting, grooming tools, throws, warm-toned home goods, boots and mid-weight layers are all climbing. New-season product is priced into demand, not out of it. This is the annual moment where people apply summer clearance logic to autumn arrivals and get confused about why nothing is on sale. Nothing is on sale because the season has not started, and the correct move on genuinely new-season goods is to buy for fit and availability rather than price.

Unglamorous everyday goods are still outrunning the aspirational ones. The strongest movement in personal care and household consumables remains at the boring end — cotton swabs, plain ointments, anti-dandruff shampoo, refill packs, storage bags, cleaning basics. This is a price-sensitive market buying utility. It also happens to be exactly the category where the unit-count dial gets turned most aggressively, so it is where reading the price-per-unit rather than the price matters most.

The quiet winner of the whole month is anything made near where it is sold. Domestic manufacturing, made-to-order production and short European supply lines are all insulated from what is happening on the water. Brands in that position are not raising prices, not shrinking packs, not extending dispatch windows and not shortening returns, because none of the pressure applies to them. That does not automatically make them cheap. It makes them predictable, and predictability is worth an unusual amount this month.

The Four Dials: Where a Freight Shock Actually Shows Up

A seller facing a sudden increase in landed cost has five levers and will pull them in a specific order. The fifth and last is the price. Understanding the first four is the whole skill this week.

Dial one: the free-shipping threshold

This is the fastest lever there is, because it requires no change to any product page and triggers no price-tracking alert. A threshold moving from thirty-five to forty-nine dollars is a fourteen-dollar increase on every order that used to sit in between, and it is invisible unless you were paying attention to the old number. Watch for two variants. The first is the straightforward raise. The second is subtler: the threshold stays the same but a growing list of categories becomes ineligible, usually the heavy ones, so the sofa cushion that used to count toward the total no longer does. Both produce the same outcome. Before you add a filler item to reach a threshold, work out whether the filler is cheaper than the shipping — surprisingly often it is not, and you have just spent eleven dollars to save nine.

Dial two: the unit count and the variant lineup

The classic consumable version is the pack that loses two units at a constant price. The durable-goods version is more interesting and less discussed: the variant lineup gets pruned. The cheap colour disappears. The small size goes unavailable. The base configuration is out of stock while the mid and high tiers are fine. This reads as a stock problem and is frequently a margin decision — the low-margin end of the range is the first thing dropped when freight per unit rises, because it is the tier where the surcharge eats the whole spread. When you see a range where only the expensive variants are available, you are usually not looking at a popularity signal. You are looking at a deliberate mix shift, and waiting for the cheap variant to return may mean waiting for a season that never comes.

Dial three: the dispatch and handling window

Handling time is a cost dial disguised as a logistics fact. When a seller can no longer serve you from the nearest node, the item comes from a further one, and the estimate stretches. Same-day becomes next-day becomes three business days. For most purchases this is a mild inconvenience. For a move-in deadline, a trip, or a gift, it is the entire decision. It also carries information: a stretching dispatch window on a listing whose price has not changed is one of the cleanest signals available that the seller is absorbing cost rather than passing it on, which in turn tells you their price is unstable and likely to move within weeks. Screenshot the estimate at the moment of purchase. It is the only version of it you will be able to prove later.

Dial four: the returns contract

The least-read and most expensive of the four. A ninety-day window becoming thirty. A prepaid label becoming a deduction. A restocking fee reappearing on bulky items after a year of not being charged. Free returns narrowing to store-only returns for a marketplace seller with no stores. The reason this dial gets pulled during a freight squeeze is simple: reverse logistics is freight too, and it is the freight nobody budgets for. On a bulky item, the difference between free returns and a fifteen percent restocking fee plus outbound freight is easily the largest single number in the transaction, and it is written in the smallest type on the page. If you are buying anything where fit, size or compatibility is uncertain, the returns contract is not a footnote — it is the product.

Cross-Border News Worth Reading This Weekend

The port situation is now in its second week and has stopped being a news event and started being a planning assumption. Thirty-six East Coast facilities remain closed with no announced restart, one Charleston terminal has closed permanently rather than temporarily, and the knock-on effect is not primarily about the containers sitting still. It is about repositioning. Empty containers are in the wrong places, vessel schedules have slipped enough that the rotation is out of sequence, and carriers are prioritising their highest-yield lanes. Even after the ports reopen, the sequencing problem persists for weeks, which is why the sensible planning horizon for anything crossing an ocean is now September rather than late August.

On the Pacific side, the typhoon interruptions to Chinese port operations have cleared, but the backlog has not. Vessels that lost several days of loading do not simply catch up; they arrive late into an arrival window that was already congested, and the delay compounds rather than absorbs. Trans-Pacific rates to the East Coast are up meaningfully and, more importantly for a shopper, surcharges have reappeared — peak-season surcharges and general rate increases that had been dormant. Surcharges matter more than base rates for retail pricing because they are applied per container regardless of what is inside it, which means they fall hardest, per dollar of product value, on cheap bulky goods. That is the mechanism by which a freight event becomes a discount cull on low-priced homeware rather than a price rise on electronics.

European inland waterway levels are the third leg and the one most people miss. Low water on the major rivers means barges load partially, which pushes volume onto rail and road at higher cost, which lands on any European-manufactured good moving to a coastal port. The practical read for a shopper outside Europe is that European brands with their own manufacturing are facing a domestic cost bump even though they are insulated from ocean freight, so the “buy European direct” hedge is real but not unlimited.

Two smaller items are worth flagging. First, marketplace fee schedules for the autumn selling season are being published now, and several categories are seeing changes to fulfilment fee bands based on dimensional weight. Sellers respond to dimensional weight changes by redesigning packaging, which means the same product may ship in a smaller box with less protection over the next month. If you are buying something fragile, that is a genuine and underrated risk this quarter. Second, the back-to-school window is compressing rather than extending this year, with the demand peak arriving earlier and the tail thinner. A thin tail means less late-August clearance in school categories than the last several years conditioned people to expect.

A Worked Example: Two Carts, Same Headline Price, Nineteen Dollars Apart

Principles are easy to agree with and hard to apply at eleven at night with a deadline. So here is the analysis run concretely on two carts that look identical at the moment of comparison.

Cart A contains a cookware set listed at one hundred and twenty dollars. Free shipping is advertised on orders over thirty-five dollars, and the item qualifies. Dispatch is quoted at one business day. The set is available in all four finishes. Returns are ninety days, prepaid label, no restocking fee. The seller is the brand itself.

Cart B contains a comparable cookware set, also listed at one hundred and twenty dollars. Free shipping is advertised on orders over thirty-five dollars, but a line in the shipping detail notes that oversized items are excluded, and shipping calculates at eleven dollars. Dispatch is quoted at four to six business days. Only two of the four finishes are available, and both are the premium ones. Returns are thirty days, with a fifteen percent restocking fee on opened cookware and return freight deducted from the refund. The seller is a third party.

At a glance these are the same purchase at the same price. They are not close.

Cart A costs one hundred and twenty dollars, arrives inside a week, and carries essentially zero downside risk, because a full refund with a prepaid label means the real cost of being wrong is an afternoon of repackaging. Cart B costs one hundred and thirty-one dollars before anything goes wrong. If it does go wrong — wrong size, damaged in transit, simply not what you wanted — the fifteen percent restocking fee is eighteen dollars, the return freight is plausibly another twelve, and the outbound eleven is gone regardless. The failure case on Cart B is roughly forty-one dollars against essentially zero on Cart A, on an item where a meaningful share of buyers do return. Multiply that by the probability you assign to returning it and the “same price” carts differ by nineteen dollars in expected cost even before the four-to-six day dispatch collides with a move-in date.

Now the diagnostic layer, which matters more than the arithmetic. Look at what Cart B is telling you about itself. Oversized exclusion from the shipping threshold, a stretched dispatch window, a pruned variant lineup with only the premium finishes left, and a hardened returns contract — that is all four dials turned in the same direction at once. A seller who has turned all four is a seller under real cost pressure who has not yet touched the price. Which means one of two things is about to happen: the price goes up, or the listing goes quiet and the item is not restocked. Either way, the absence of a price change on Cart B is not evidence of stability. It is evidence that the adjustment is still pending.

The general rule: count the dials. One turned dial is noise — thresholds move, warehouses have bad weeks. Two is a signal worth pricing in. Three or four turned simultaneously on a single listing is a seller in trouble, and the correct response is either to buy immediately if the total landed cost still works, or to walk away entirely. The one thing that is never correct is to treat it as the same offer as Cart A because the big number matches.

Platform by Platform: Where Each One Actually Stands

Amazon is where threshold and dispatch changes are most visible if you know to look, because the fulfilment network makes handling estimates unusually informative. A same-day or next-day estimate means local stock. Anything beyond three days on a common item means the local node is empty. The category to watch closely this month is anything oversized, where the fee band changes discussed above are already producing packaging redesigns. The returns contract is generally the strongest in this comparison for first-party sales and highly variable for third-party ones, which is why the seller field deserves the same attention as the price field.

Shopify-hosted direct brands are, collectively, the most interesting place to shop this month, and the reason is structural rather than promotional. These brands control their own inventory and their own shipping terms, so there is no platform-wide threshold change to absorb. What they do instead is adjust shipping tiers and bundle mathematics, which is a much more legible form of adjustment than a threshold exclusion buried in a shipping detail. The practical advice is to always check the brand’s own site before buying its product on a marketplace, and to check specifically what shipping costs at the final step rather than what the banner claims.

TikTok Shop is at the peak of its back-to-school moment and is the platform where demand is doing the most to prop up pricing. Discovery-driven buying is inherently deadline-free — nobody needed the thing five minutes before they saw it — which means the platform has the least price pressure of any on this list right now. The correct posture here for the next fortnight is scepticism about urgency messaging, because the urgency is real for the seller and mostly manufactured for the buyer.

Walmart and Target are the two places where the bulky-goods surcharge story will surface first and most visibly, because both carry deep assortments of exactly the low-value-density homeware that per-container surcharges punish. Both also have physical return paths, which is worth a genuine premium on bulky items this month for the reasons the worked example makes clear. If you are buying something big and you are not certain, buying it where you can return it without freight is worth paying for.

eBay, AliExpress and the cross-border channels are where the dispatch dial has moved furthest. On the second-hand and open-box side, the contract fields dominate completely — condition grade, seller history and returns terms are the product. On the direct-from-origin side, delivery estimates have drifted and will drift further before they recover. Neither is a reason to avoid these channels; both are reasons to buy only things you can wait for.

Specialist retail — photography, audio, professional tools — remains the most stable corner of the market and the most underrated. These channels run genuine warranty and returns contracts, they carry authorised stock, and they compete on service rather than on being three dollars cheaper. In a month where the contract around the price is the whole story, that is worth more than usual.

Building a Landed-Cost Number in Ninety Seconds

Here is the process, compressed to something you can actually run in a cart rather than a spreadsheet.

Start with the price. Then add four numbers to it. First, shipping as calculated at the final checkout step, not as advertised on the banner — these differ more often than they should and the difference is always in the same direction. Second, any filler you added to reach a threshold, counted at full price, because a filler item you did not want is a cost and not a saving. Third, the expected return cost, which is the restocking fee plus the return freight, multiplied by your honest probability of returning it. For clothing and footwear that probability is not small and pretending otherwise is how people lose money. Fourth, the cost of the dispatch window, which is zero if you have no deadline and can be very large if you do.

That gives you a landed number. Compare landed numbers, never sticker prices.

Then run the reversibility check, which is the part most people skip. Ask what happens if this is wrong. If the answer is “a prepaid label and an afternoon,” you can buy on thin information and correct later, and you should be willing to pay a small premium for that option. If the answer is “a restocking fee, freight both ways, and a thirty-day clock,” then you need to be right the first time, which means measuring the space, checking the compatibility, reading the sizing chart properly, and generally spending the twenty minutes that a reversible purchase does not require.

Finally, timestamp the terms. Screenshot the shipping threshold, the dispatch estimate and the returns window at the moment you buy. This takes four seconds and it is the only leverage you will have if the terms differ when something goes wrong. Terms pages change without notice and without version history, and “it said ninety days when I ordered” is an unwinnable argument without an image.

One refinement for this specific month. When you find yourself comparing a domestic or made-to-order option against an imported one, add the freight-environment adjustment explicitly rather than intuitively. The imported option’s dispatch estimate is more likely to slip, its returns terms are more likely to tighten between now and when you would use them, and its restock is more likely to be delayed if the first unit is faulty. None of that is a certainty and all of it is a real expected cost. It does not always flip the decision. It flips it more often than the sticker gap suggests.

The one exception is glassware and ceramics, where the packaging-redesign risk argues for buying somewhere with a real returns path rather than somewhere cheap.

Desks, chairs and home office. Buy the chair now; the desk can wait if you can tolerate a lead time.

Laptops and accessories. Components are on a different cycle to freight, so storage and memory upgrades are good value and unaffected by everything else in this piece. Docks, hubs and cables are demand-inflated for two more weeks. Sleeves and bags are the clearest wait on the entire list.

Air quality and small appliances. Purifiers transition straight from smoke season to allergy season without a clearance gap, so there is no better moment coming. Price the filters over three years rather than the unit over one — the subscription cost is where the quiet increases have been landing and it dwarfs the hardware difference.

Cooling and fans. Genuine clearance, thin selection, roughly three weeks of natural demand left. Buy if the specific model and size you want is available and discounted. Do not wait for deeper cuts, because the deeper cuts arrive attached to the units nobody wanted.

Early-autumn apparel. New season, no discounts, priced into a demand ramp. Buy on fit and availability. Denim, mid-weight layers, boots and outerwear all belong here. The one genuinely discounted corner is outgoing colourways on direct brand sites, which is a real and reliable value pocket every year at this exact moment.

Footwear. The highest return rate of any category on this list, which makes the returns contract the dominant variable rather than a footnote. A pair twenty dollars cheaper with a thirty-day window and paid return freight is usually the more expensive pair. Buy where you can exchange.

Audio. Small, light, freight-insensitive and on long model cycles, which makes it one of the few categories where the current environment simply does not apply. No timing argument in either direction. Buy on need and on the specific product rather than on the calendar.

Photography and video. Entering the strongest demand quarter of the year, priced accordingly, and served by channels with the best warranty and returns terms in this whole comparison. Buying ahead of the ramp is correct. Buying from a marketplace third party to save a small percentage is not.

Beauty and personal care. The unit-count dial is pulled harder here than anywhere else. Always read price per unit rather than price per pack, and be specifically suspicious of a pack that has been “redesigned.” Sunscreen in stick format continues to sell through fastest; the practical reason is reapplication over makeup, which is the only reason reapplication happens at all.

Outdoor and camping. Autumn season demand is beginning and made-to-order brands are the story. Lead times lengthen with demand, so the cost of hesitation here is weeks rather than dollars. Order now for anything you intend to use this season.

Consumables and household basics. The strongest-moving corner of the market and the one where the price-per-unit discipline pays most reliably. Refills over new units, bulk over convenience packs, and no brand loyalty whatsoever.

Where This Read Breaks Down

Intellectual honesty demands the counter-cases, and there are several.

A stretched dispatch window is not always a cost signal. Warehouses have bad weeks, staffing gaps happen, and a single-node outage produces exactly the same symptom as structural pressure. This is why the count-the-dials rule exists: one dial is noise. If dispatch has stretched but the threshold, the variant lineup and the returns terms are all unchanged, the most likely explanation is boring and temporary.

A pruned variant lineup is sometimes just popularity. The cheap colour disappearing because everyone bought the cheap colour is a genuinely common and entirely benign explanation, particularly during a demand peak like this weekend. The way to distinguish: if the cheap variant is out of stock and the listing shows a restock date, it is popularity. If it has quietly vanished from the selector entirely, it is a mix decision.

Domestic manufacturing is not a magic shield. Domestic assembly frequently uses imported components, and a domestically made product can carry as much exposure as an imported one if its bill of materials crosses the same water. The insulation is real for genuinely vertically integrated makers and partial for everyone else. Treating “made here” as a binary is a mistake.

Tightened returns terms are sometimes about abuse, not freight. Categories with high fraudulent-return rates tighten periodically for reasons that have nothing to do with container rates, and the timing can coincide misleadingly. This matters mostly because it means a tightened returns window on a single listing in a high-abuse category is weaker evidence than the same change on a listing for a bulky appliance.

And the largest caveat: none of this predicts prices. The four dials tell you about a seller’s current cost position and what they are likely to do next. They do not tell you what a competitor will do, whether a promotion is already scheduled, or whether the freight situation resolves faster than expected. The framework improves the quality of a decision under uncertainty. It does not remove the uncertainty, and anyone claiming otherwise is selling something.

Five Mistakes That Will Cost You Money This Month

Comparing sticker prices across sellers with different contracts. This is the central error and the one the worked example exists to demonstrate. Two identical numbers can be nineteen dollars apart. If you take one thing from this piece, take this one.

Adding filler to reach a shipping threshold without checking the arithmetic. If shipping is nine dollars and the cheapest thing you actually want is fourteen, buying the filler costs you five dollars and a thing you did not want. Thresholds are designed to make this feel like winning. Do the subtraction.

Waiting for a late-August markdown in back-to-school categories. The demand tail is thinner this year and the peak arrived earlier. What is left in the last week of August will be picked-over sizes and unpopular colours rather than the same goods at lower prices. If you need it for term, the next ten days are the window.

Applying clearance logic to new-season goods. Autumn arrivals are priced into demand. Waiting for a discount on a product that launched three weeks ago is waiting for something the calendar does not produce until the season ends. Two different logics are operating in the same shop simultaneously, and mixing them up is the most common autumn error.

Buying bulky items from a seller with no physical return path. The freight environment has made reverse logistics expensive, and sellers have responded by making you pay for it. On anything large, the ability to return in person is worth real money this month, and frequently more money than the price gap that tempted you elsewhere.

Policy, Shipping and Returns

A short practical section, because this is the month where these details are load-bearing rather than decorative.

Read the shipping threshold and its exclusions together. A threshold with an oversized-item exclusion is not the threshold that is advertised, and the exclusion is where all of the interesting behaviour is happening right now. The exclusion list is usually one click deeper than the banner and it changes without announcement.

Distinguish the dispatch estimate from the delivery estimate. These are separate numbers and sellers are not consistent about which one they show prominently. A one-day dispatch with a seven-day delivery and a five-day dispatch with a three-day delivery land on the same date, and only one of them is under the seller’s control. If you have a deadline, the number that matters is the delivery date, and if the page only shows dispatch, add transit yourself.

Check who pays for the return before you check the return window. A generous ninety-day window with deducted return freight is worse, on a heavy item, than a thirty-day window with a prepaid label. The window is the number people compare and the freight term is the number that costs money.

Understand restocking fees by category, not by seller. They are reappearing selectively, concentrated in bulky goods, opened consumables, and configured or customised items. A seller with no restocking fee on small goods may absolutely have one on a mattress or a desk, and the terms page will usually say so in a single line under a heading you would not think to open.

On cross-border purchases, confirm who is the importer of record. This determines who handles duties and whether a return can even physically be made, and it is frequently the single most important line on a cross-border listing. A return that must be shipped back across a border at your expense is, for most goods under a couple of hundred dollars, not a return at all.

What’s Coming Next

The next two weeks are the tail of the back-to-school peak. Expect continued firm pricing across dorm, desk and laptop-accessory categories, continued genuine clearance in cooling, and continued absence of discounts on autumn arrivals. The dials keep turning quietly. Nothing dramatic happens on the price tag.

Late August into early September is where the freight situation actually reaches shelves. The goods being sold today were already in warehouses before any of this started; the goods that will be sold in five weeks are the ones currently sitting on repositioned vessels. This is where visible price movement becomes likely, and it will be concentrated exactly where the surcharge mechanics predict: cheap bulky goods, homeware, low-value-density furniture, and large-format storage. High-value-density categories — electronics, optics, jewellery, apparel — will barely notice.

September brings the autumn product cycle in earnest, the first real clearance on summer categories that failed to move, and the beginning of the pre-holiday inventory conversation. It also brings the resolution or continuation of the port situation, which is the single largest branch point. If it resolves cleanly, the dispatch dial reverses first and the price dial mostly never turns. If it drags into a third and fourth week, the repositioning problem compounds and the price dial turns in October instead, ahead of the holiday season, which is the genuinely bad scenario for anyone planning to buy anything bulky before December.

The strategic read from here: front-load anything heavy, imported and non-urgent that you know you will buy before the end of the year. That is an unusual piece of advice and it is specific to this moment. The normal advice is to wait, because normally the discount calendar rewards patience. This quarter, the freight calendar and the discount calendar point in opposite directions on bulky imported goods, and the freight calendar is the larger number.

Bottom line

The price tag is the last thing a seller changes, which makes it the last thing you should look at. This weekend, in the middle of a genuine freight disruption and the peak of the move-in wave, the useful information is in four fields that nobody screenshots: what the free-shipping threshold actually is and what it excludes, whether the pack or the variant lineup has quietly shrunk, how far the dispatch window has stretched, and what the returns contract really costs if you use it. Count how many of those four have turned on a listing. One is noise, two is a signal, three or four is a seller who has not raised the price yet but is going to.

For the next ten days, the concrete plan is simple. Buy the heavy imported thing now, because the surcharge mechanics hit low-value-density goods hardest and they have not reached shelves yet. Buy the back-to-school thing you actually need now, because the demand tail is thinner this year and there is no late-August rescue coming. Wait on bags, on direct-from-origin audio, and on anything fragile shipping in a redesigned box, because the dial that moved on those is dispatch, and dispatch reverses on its own. Ignore the discount status of autumn arrivals entirely, because that season has not started and its prices are still going up.

And when two options come out at the same number, do not flip a coin. Add the shipping you will actually pay, the filler you would have to buy, the return you might have to make, and the days you cannot afford to lose. Then compare. The number on the tag is the headline. The four fields around it are the story.
