Best Things to Buy Right Now — June 2, 2026

Quick take: Today is Tuesday, June 2. The tournament opens nine days from now on June 11, and the big June sale opens twenty-one days from now on June 23. Everyone is telling you to wait for the twenty-third. Here is the problem with that advice: the twenty-third is a promotion, not a bottom. Retailers set promotion dates a quarter in advance, based on budget cycles and merchandising calendars. Shoppers move on a completely different clock, driven by whatever is happening in the world that week. These two calendars are unrelated, and the gap between them is where the actual money is. When a discount lands on top of a demand spike, the discount is cosmetic — you save eight percent on paper and lose it again to stockouts, restricted variants, and the fact that everyone else is buying the same thing at the same moment. When a discount lands into a demand hole, it is real, and it is usually not advertised at all. This month the hole is obvious once you look for it. The sale runs June 23 to 26. The group stage ends around the twenty-seventh and the knockout rounds do not start immediately. So you get a two-day window — June 27 and 28 — where the promotion is technically over, the audience has gone quiet, and every seller who committed inventory to the event and missed their sell-through number is sitting on landed stock with the marketing budget already spent. They cut. They cut quietly, without a banner, because a loud markdown two days after an official sale makes the official sale look like a lie. That is the trough. It is unadvertised, it is short, and it is only reachable if your shipping lead time lets you reach it — which is the second half of this guide, because a two-day window means nothing if your parcel takes three weeks to cross a border. What follows is how to tell which calendar any given category lives on, why the fastest-selling items never make it to the trough at all, an arithmetic walk through the same purchase under four different fulfilment speeds, and twenty picks sorted by where each one genuinely bottoms rather than by when the banner goes up.

What’s Hot This Week

Nine days before a tournament, the market is not one market. It has split into three layers that are moving in different directions at the same time, and mistaking one for another is the single most expensive error available to you this month.

The Categories the Tournament Is Already Bidding Up

There is a narrow band of goods where demand has already arrived and prices are firming. Anything that converts a room into a viewing space is in it: projectors, screens, seating, soundbars, coolers, folding tables, outdoor cooking, string lighting, anything that holds a lot of cold drinks. Prices in this band are not going to be better on June 23 in any way that matters, and the reason is almost tautological — the event that would justify a discount is the same event that is creating the demand. No merchandiser cuts into a rising curve. Automated repricers certainly do not. If you need one of these things for the eleventh you are not shopping a sale, you are racing a curve that has already started, and every day you spend waiting costs you both price and selection. The variant you want goes first. The mid-tier model sells out before the cheap one and the expensive one, because the mid-tier is where the compromise buyers land. Buy in this band now, and buy the specific configuration rather than the best deal, because in three weeks the deal will exist and the configuration will not.

The Counter-Cyclical Layer Nobody Watches

This is the layer almost no guide covers, and it is where the quiet money is. A major tournament does not just create demand, it destroys it — specifically, it destroys demand for anything that competes for the same evening hours. Board games, model kits, long-form craft supplies, gardening projects, jigsaw puzzles, sewing and needlework, tabletop miniatures, home improvement projects that take a weekend, books, and to a surprising degree cooking equipment that implies a slow Sunday. For roughly a month, the people who would normally buy these things are doing something else. Sellers in these categories do not get a promotional calendar entry, because nobody builds a marketing campaign around a category that is about to go soft. What they get instead is inventory that was ordered in March against a normal June, sitting in a warehouse through an abnormal June. Sell-through slips. Automated repricers notice slipping sell-through before any human does, and they respond the only way they know how. Prices in the counter-cyclical layer drift down for four to six weeks without a single announcement, which is exactly why nobody notices. There is no banner to see.

The Quiet Middle That Has No Calendar At All

The third layer is the largest and the most boring, and it is where most of this guide’s picks come from. These are categories with no relationship to the event in either direction: hand tools, kitchen knives, stationery, water filtration, replacement parts, precision instruments, hobby electronics, anything bought because the old one broke. Demand here is flat, driven by replacement cycles rather than occasions. Supply is being restocked right now on spring purchase orders, which means complete variant grids and prices set by landed cost rather than by campaign. Nothing is scheduled. There is no successor product waiting behind a keynote, no marketing calendar entry, no reason for the price to move in either direction. The instinct is to treat this as a reason to wait, on the general principle that something might happen. Nothing is going to happen. That is what “no calendar” means, and a category with no calendar has an expected discount of approximately zero, which makes waiting a pure cost with no offsetting benefit.

Two Calendars, One Price Tag

The core idea of this guide is simple enough to state in two sentences, so let me state it and then spend the rest of the section on why it is not obvious.

Every price you see is the output of two independent processes. The first is the promotion calendar: a schedule of discount events set months in advance by a merchandising team, constrained by budget periods, vendor funding agreements, warehouse capacity, and the competitive necessity of running your sale when everyone else runs theirs. It is planned, announced, and rigid. Once June 23 is locked in a system in February, it is very hard to move. The second is the demand calendar: the actual pattern of when human beings want to buy things, driven by weather, holidays, paydays, school terms, cultural events, and whatever is currently occupying the collective attention. It is unplanned, unannounced, and extremely sharp. A tournament kickoff can move category-level demand by double digits inside forty-eight hours.

Here is the part that matters: these two calendars are not synchronised, and they are not even trying to be. The promotion calendar is optimised for the retailer’s fiscal and logistical convenience. The demand calendar is optimised for nothing at all — it simply happens. They occasionally coincide, and when they do, retailers take credit for it. But structurally they are independent, and the interesting purchases live in the places where they diverge.

There are four combinations and each one implies a different action. High demand plus a promotion is the worst quadrant for a buyer and the best for a seller — the discount is real in a narrow arithmetic sense but you are competing with a crowd for constrained inventory, and the effective price including the compromises you will make on configuration, colour, and delivery date is higher than the sticker suggests. High demand plus no promotion is straightforwardly bad; prices drift up and you either pay or postpone. Low demand plus a promotion is the quadrant everybody wants and almost nobody finds, because a merchandiser who knows demand will be low usually just cancels the promotion. Low demand plus no promotion is the one that actually pays, and it is the one nobody looks for, because with no promotion there is nothing to look at. The price simply erodes, a percent at a time, on automated adjustments that generate no notification, no email, and no badge.

The practical consequence is that the correct question stops being “when is the sale” and becomes “when is this specific category’s demand lowest, and is there any inventory pressure at that moment?” Those two conditions together produce the real trough. The sale date is, at best, a weak proxy for it, and this month it is an actively misleading one.

Why a Discount Into a Spike Is Not a Discount

I want to be precise about the claim that an event-window discount on an event-driven product is fake, because it sounds like cynicism and it is actually arithmetic.

Suppose a portable projector lists at four hundred dollars today, June 2. On June 23 it goes on sale at three hundred and forty, a fifteen percent cut. That looks unambiguously like a win. Now add the things that do not appear on the price tag. First, the pre-event drift: between now and the twenty-third, demand for viewing equipment rises, and repricers respond. The four hundred dollar listing is quite likely to be four hundred and thirty by the eighteenth. The sale price is set as a percentage off the then-current price in a meaningful number of systems, so your fifteen percent may be fifteen percent off a number that has already moved against you. Second, the configuration tax: at three hundred and forty, the popular variant clears in hours. What is available on the twenty-third afternoon is the version with less brightness, or the bundle with an accessory you did not want, or the same model from a seller with a nine-day handling time. The listed discount applies to a product; the product you can actually get is a different one. Third, the delivery risk: you are buying an item you need for a specific date, during the highest-volume shipping window of the quarter, from a fulfilment network running at capacity. A two-day estimate becomes five. If the item was for the eleventh, it is now for the sixteenth, and its value to you has collapsed regardless of price.

Stack those and the fifteen percent is perhaps six percent, on a compromised specification, with meaningful delivery risk. Compare that to buying today at four hundred, in the configuration you chose, delivered by the sixth, with a return window that runs into early July anyway. The nominal discount is larger and the real one is smaller.

Now run the same arithmetic on a counter-cyclical item — say a two-hundred-dollar model kit or a sewing machine. There is no promotion. There is no announced anything. But demand for it is about to fall for a month, the seller has inventory ordered against a normal June, and repricers will grind the number down without ceremony. There is no crowd, so every variant stays available, and shipping networks are less loaded for these categories precisely because nobody is buying them. The unannounced erosion is smaller in percentage terms and larger in real terms, and it comes with no compromises attached.

The general rule: a discount is only worth what it is worth after you subtract the cost of the crowd it attracts. Advertised discounts attract crowds by design — that is their entire function. Unadvertised price erosion attracts nobody, which is exactly why it is still there when you arrive.

The Trough Nobody Markets: June 27 and 28

So where is the real bottom this month? Not on the twenty-third. Here is the mechanism in full.

Event inventory is committed long before the event. A seller planning for June 23 placed purchase orders in February and March, booked freight in April, and had the goods landed and inbounded by mid-May at the latest. That inventory is a sunk cost sitting in a warehouse accruing storage fees. The marketing spend against it — deal fees, ad budget, coupon funding — is also committed and largely spent by the time the sale opens. The seller’s entire economic outcome for the quarter now rests on one number: sell-through during the event window.

The event runs June 23 to 26. On the morning of the twenty-seventh, every seller in the programme knows whether they hit their number. Roughly speaking, a third smashed it, a third landed near plan, and a third missed badly. That last group is now in a specific and uncomfortable position. They have landed inventory, no remaining marketing budget, storage fees ticking, and — critically — no scheduled promotional event to sell into for weeks. Their next calendar entry is a long way off.

At the same moment, the demand side goes quiet. The group stage wraps around the twenty-seventh and the knockout rounds do not begin immediately. There is a gap. Combine that with ordinary post-sale fatigue — people who spent money on the twenty-third are not spending again on the twenty-eighth — and you get one of the emptiest retail moments of the quarter, sitting immediately after one of the fullest.

Sellers who missed respond by cutting. But they cut quietly. No banner, no email, no badge. There are two reasons for this and both are worth understanding, because they tell you what to look for. The first is credibility: publicly undercutting your own sale price forty-eight hours after the sale ends teaches every customer you have that your sales are theatre, and merchandising teams are extremely sensitive to this. The second is contractual: many vendor agreements and price-parity clauses restrict advertised pricing below an event floor, but restrict only the advertised price. A quiet adjustment to the listed number, with no promotional treatment attached, sits outside the clause. So the cut happens on the price field and nowhere else.

The result is the buyer’s best combination available in June: the lowest number of the month, into a room with almost nobody in it. No competition for inventory, complete variant availability among whatever remains, shipping networks recovered from the event surge, and delivery estimates back to normal.

There is one enormous catch, and I will not bury it: the items that reach June 27 are, by construction, the items that did not sell. The trough exists precisely because these products missed their number. Sometimes that means the product is fine and the forecast was wrong, or the price was set too high, or it lost a deal slot to a competitor. Sometimes it means the product is not very good. You are choosing between the best price and the best product, and that is a genuine trade-off rather than a free lunch. The next section on lead times tells you whether you can even reach the window; the section on where this breaks down tells you when the goods sitting there are not worth having.

One Trough, Four Lead Times

A two-day window is only a window if you can get through it. This is where the timing argument stops being abstract, because the same product, at the same price, on the same date, is a completely different decision depending on how long it takes to arrive.

Take one purchase — call it a two-hundred-and-eighty-dollar item — and put it behind four different fulfilment paths.

Path one: domestic, next-day or two-day. You can sit on your hands until the morning of June 27, look at the actual number, and decide with full information. The trough is fully reachable and costs you nothing to reach. Your decision date and the trough date are the same date. This is the only configuration in which “wait and see” is literally true, and it applies to a shrinking fraction of what people actually buy.

Path two: cross-border standard, seven to ten days. If you want the item in hand by early July, you can still order on the twenty-seventh. But if you need it for a fixed date in the first week of July, you are cutting it fine, and the moment any deadline exists you have to commit around the twentieth — before the sale opens, let alone before the trough. Notice what happened: the trough is technically reachable but you are deciding blind. You are choosing between a known price today and an unknown price twenty-five days out, with no ability to see the second number before committing to skip the first.

Path three: direct from origin, two to three weeks. Order on June 27 and it lands somewhere between July 11 and July 18. For anything with a use-by date attached — a trip, a season, a gift, a project with a start date — the trough is simply not available. You are buying at today’s price or you are not buying. Lead time has converted a price question into a scheduling question, and the scheduling question has already been answered.

Path four: made to order, six to eight weeks. There is no trough at all, and there was never going to be one. Made-to-order production has no standing inventory, which means it has no sell-through problem, which means it has no mechanism by which a discount could occur. The queue is the price. Waiting does not lower the number; it only moves you further back in a queue that is about to get longer as everyone else’s June purchases resolve.

Here is the inversion, and it is the opposite of what people assume. Conventional advice says patience is free and impatience is expensive. On this axis it reverses: the slower your goods travel, the more expensive patience becomes. A two-day buyer pays nothing to wait, because waiting costs them only time. A three-week buyer pays for waiting in information — they must decide before they can see. A made-to-order buyer pays for waiting in pure calendar with no possibility of compensation. The people most likely to be told to wait for the sale are cross-border buyers, and they are precisely the people for whom waiting has the worst payoff structure.

Practical translation: before you decide whether to wait, look up the shipping estimate first, not the price. Then subtract the transit time from your actual need-by date. If the resulting decision date falls before June 27, the trough does not exist for you and the entire debate is moot. Buy the thing on its merits today.

Reading Which Calendar a Category Lives On

Everything above depends on correctly classifying what you are buying. That classification takes about a minute and it is mostly mechanical.

The Three-Question Test

Question one: does an occasion consume this product? Not “is it seasonal” — specifically, is there an event during which people use it more? A cooler, yes. A pepper mill, no. If the answer is yes and the occasion is imminent, you are on the event-coupled curve and prices are rising into it. Buy now.

Question two: does an occasion consume this product’s time? This is the counter-cyclical test and it is the one people skip. A jigsaw puzzle is not used more during a tournament, it is used less, because the hours it needs are being spent elsewhere. Anything requiring a continuous block of attention competes with the event rather than complementing it. If the answer is yes, demand is about to fall, inventory pressure is about to build, and the price will erode quietly for a month. Wait, and expect no announcement.

Question three: is there anything at all on the calendar? New model cycles, seasonal changeovers, regulatory dates, annual category sales. If the honest answer is no — and for the entire replacement-driven middle of retail the honest answer is no — then nothing is coming, the expected discount is zero, and waiting has a negative expected value once you price the use you gave up. Buy.

When a Category Sits on Both Calendars

Some things land in two layers at once and these are the genuinely hard cases. Outdoor cooking is the clearest example: a pizza oven is event-coupled in the sense that people host during tournaments, and simultaneously it is a slow-weekend product that competes for the same hours. Which effect dominates depends on how the product is actually used in your household, not on what the category page says. The resolution rule I use: the coupling wins when the product is a prop for the event, and the counter-cycle wins when the product is a substitute for it. A cooler is a prop. A charcoal smoker that needs six hours of tending is a substitute. The same aisle contains both, and they should be bought on opposite schedules.

Coffee equipment splits the same way. An insulated carafe that serves eight people is a prop. A manual pour-over setup that takes twenty minutes of ritual is a substitute. Cookware splits on speed. Audio splits on whether it is for a group or for one person with headphones on. Once you start looking for this seam it appears everywhere, and it is the single most useful classification move in this guide, because it separates two products that sit next to each other on a shelf and should be bought three weeks apart.

Those are two different games and they should be played on opposite schedules.

The Competition You Are Not Pricing

There is a term missing from almost every buy-now-or-wait discussion, and leaving it out is what makes advertised sales look better than they are. The missing term is other buyers.

Price is the only variable most people model, and price is continuous and recoverable — if you overpay by twelve dollars, you overpaid by twelve dollars, and next month you can buy something else. Availability is discrete and unrecoverable. If the item is gone, no amount of patience produces it, and the substitute you settle for is often materially worse in a way that dwarfs the price difference you were optimising.

Advertised events concentrate buyers in time by design. That is their function: compress a month of purchase intent into ninety-six hours so the retailer can clear inventory and post a number. From the seller’s side that is efficient. From your side it means you are entering a queue you did not know you had joined, and the depth of that queue is not disclosed anywhere on the listing. The variants go in a predictable order — most popular colour first, mid-tier configuration second, then the bundles get broken up and resold as the fragments nobody wanted.

This is precisely why the June 27 trough is structurally attractive and structurally limited at the same time. Attractive because the queue is empty; you are the only person in the room and every remaining variant is yours. Limited because the room is only full of things nobody took. The two properties are the same property viewed from different sides.

The practical way to price this: before deciding to wait for any date, ask what you will do if the item is unavailable on that date. If the honest answer is “buy the next one down,” compute how much worse the next one down is and compare that number to the discount you are chasing. In my experience the substitution penalty exceeds the expected discount more often than not, particularly in categories where the range is narrow and the steps between models are large. In a three-model range, missing your model costs far more than fifteen percent. In a thirty-model range it costs almost nothing, and waiting is nearly free.

There is a second-order version worth knowing. On the twenty-seventh, sellers who missed their number are cutting because they have depth. Nobody discounts their last four units. So the quiet markdowns you find in the trough tend to come attached to deep stock, which means the availability risk in that window is genuinely lower than in the event window — the opposite of what intuition suggests. The scarce moment is the sale; the abundant moment is the two days afterwards.

Building a Calendar Read in Ninety Seconds

Four checks, in order, each of which can end the process early.

Check one: what is the shipping estimate? Not the price — the estimate. Take your need-by date, subtract transit, and see where the decision date lands. If it lands before June 27, stop. The trough is unreachable and the entire timing question is settled. Buy on merits. This single check resolves a large share of cross-border purchases in about eight seconds and it is the one people do last, if at all.

Check two: prop or substitute? Does this product get used during the event, or does it need hours the event is taking? Props are rising into June 11 and should be bought now. Substitutes are falling into July and should be waited on. If it is neither, continue.

Check three: is there any scheduled mechanism? Model-year changeover, seasonal changeover, an annual category sale, a regulatory date, a successor product with an announced launch window. If you cannot name a specific mechanism and roughly date it, there is no trough, and a category with no trough is a buy today regardless of how the price looks. “Something might happen” is not a mechanism. Refusing to accept vague optimism as a reason to wait is most of the discipline here.

Check four: is the seller carrying depth? If you got this far, you are considering a genuine wait, and the last question is whether the item will still be there. Cart-quantity probes, variant grid completeness, and the number of competing sellers all indicate depth. Deep stock means the wait is low-risk. Thin stock means you are gambling a specific product against a general discount, which is a bad trade at almost any spread.

The One Number Worth Writing Down

Before you wait for anything, write down the price today. Not a screenshot of a deal page — the plain current number, with the date. Three weeks from now, when the banner says forty percent off, you will have the only piece of information that makes that claim checkable, and you will be astonished how often the sale price is within a few dollars of what you are looking at right now. A reference price collected before the noise is worth more than any amount of comparison shopping during it, because during the event every visible number has been arranged by someone with an interest in how you read it.

Where This Read Breaks Down

Five failure modes, stated plainly, because a framework you cannot break is a framework you have not tested.

The trough is populated by losers. Already flagged, and it remains the biggest weakness. Everything reaching June 27 with a quiet markdown is there because it did not sell during the best selling window of the quarter. Sometimes the forecast was wrong. Sometimes the product is not good. You will not always be able to tell which, and the confident-sounding version of this guide would pretend otherwise.

Price-floor agreements can suppress the quiet cut entirely. Brands with enforced minimum advertised pricing, selective distribution, or authorised-dealer agreements can prevent the twenty-seventh markdown from happening at all. The seller may badly want to cut and be contractually unable to. Categories where every retailer shows an identical price are exactly the categories where this is in force, and identical pricing across five unrelated sellers is the tell.

Supply constraints invert the whole thing. If a product is allocation-limited, missing a sell-through target is impossible, because everything shipped was pre-sold. Constrained supply means flat prices with no trough in either direction, and the flatness looks identical to the no-calendar flatness that this guide calls a buy signal. The distinguishing question is whether stock is consistently thin. Chronically thin plus flat means constrained, and constrained means buy immediately, because the next move is up.

The window is short and time zones are real. June 27 to 28 is a two-day read on a market that operates globally and continuously. Repricers do not observe your calendar. The window may open late on the twenty-sixth in one region and be substantially closed by midday on the twenty-eighth in another. Treating a fuzzy forty-eight-hour band as a precise appointment will produce disappointment that has nothing to do with whether the underlying mechanism is real.

And the honest self-criticism: this framework can make you a worse buyer. Everything above is a set of reasons to think harder about timing, and thinking harder about timing has a cost measured in attention and in postponed use. If you spend nine days classifying a forty-dollar purchase onto the correct calendar, you have destroyed more value than any plausible discount could return. The framework earns its keep above roughly two hundred dollars and becomes actively harmful below about fifty. Below that threshold the correct algorithm is: buy the thing, use the thing, stop reading shopping guides.

Cross-Border Timing, Duties and Paperwork

Everything in this guide is harder across a border, and there is one specific interaction that is worth spelling out because it silently invalidates a lot of trough arithmetic.

A quiet markdown does not reduce your duty proportionally, and sometimes does not reduce it at all. Duties and import taxes are assessed on declared customs value, which is generally the transaction price, but the assessment happens on a schedule that has nothing to do with when the seller adjusted their listing. If you buy in the trough from a seller shipping out of a bonded facility where goods were already entered at pre-markdown value, the landed-cost saving you actually receive can be materially smaller than the price cut you are looking at. The listing shows a fourteen percent reduction and the total at checkout moves nine percent. That gap is not an error and it is not recoverable.

The second interaction is timing against threshold rules. Where a value threshold governs whether a shipment is assessed at all, a markdown can push an order under it, which is a large and discontinuous saving — or a bundle you assembled to save on shipping can push you over it, which is a large and discontinuous cost. In the trough window, when you are being tempted by deep-stock multi-buys, this is the trap. A quiet markdown that encourages you to buy three instead of one can cost more than it saves, and the arithmetic flips on a threshold you never see quoted on the product page.

Third, the twenty-seventh is a bad date for customs throughput in a way that is easy to miss. The event surge from June 23 to 26 does not clear instantly; it propagates through consolidation, linehaul, and clearance for a week or more. An order placed into the quiet window sits behind the event’s backlog even though the storefront looks empty. Expect the delivery estimate shown on June 27 to be optimistic by several days for cross-border lanes specifically. If your need-by date has no slack, the trough is not for you no matter how good the number looks.

Finally, the practical version of the lead-time rule: for anything crossing a border, your real decision date is the trough date minus transit minus clearance slack, and clearance slack in the week after a major event should be treated as three to five days rather than one. Run that subtraction before you talk yourself into waiting. For a great many cross-border purchases it produces a decision date that has already passed, which is a genuinely useful thing to discover on June 2 rather than on June 24.

Category Notes

Audio and desktop equipment. Split it on group-versus-solo. Anything for a room full of people is a prop and is rising now. Anything for one person at a desk with headphones on is a substitute and softens through July. That single distinction handles most of the category and explains why the Aune desktop stack and a soundbar should be bought a month apart despite sitting in the same department.

Kitchen. Split on speed. Fast tools that serve a crowd are props. Slow equipment implying an unhurried Sunday is a substitute. Knives and mills are a third thing entirely — pure replacement demand with no calendar in either direction, which is why they show as no-trough buys.

Tools and workshop. Almost entirely off the consumer calendar. Industrial and trade brands price against distributor cycles and fiscal periods, and their soft moments land in mid-summer and at year end. Do not expect June to do anything for a tool brand, and do not read a flat June price as a signal of anything.

Outdoor and camping. Genuinely bifurcated. Gear for social outdoor use tracks the event calendar upward. Ultralight backcountry gear tracks the hiking season and the small-batch production cycle, and most of the good makers are cottage operations with no promotional apparatus whatsoever. In that corner the only real risk is a sell-out, not a missed discount.

A Note on Bundles in the Quiet Window

One specific behaviour spikes on the twenty-seventh and it deserves flagging. Sellers sitting on missed inventory frequently prefer bundling to cutting, because a bundle moves units without publishing a lower unit price — it preserves the headline while clearing the warehouse. So the quiet window is unusually rich in two-packs, accessory kits, and starter sets. Some of these are excellent value and some are a mechanism for attaching stock nobody wanted to stock you do want. The test is simple: price the bundle as though the extra items are worth zero. If it still beats the single unit at its trough price, it is a real deal. If it only works when you credit yourself full value for an accessory you would never have bought, it is inventory management wearing a discount costume.

What’s Coming Next

June 11 through 22 is the pre-sale freeze. Event-coupled prices firm up, counter-cyclical demand starts falling, and the quiet middle does nothing at all. This is a good stretch to collect reference prices and a bad stretch to buy anything the tournament touches.

June 23 to 26 is the sale. It is a real bottom for genuinely funded core categories with inventory depth — the Yeedi case in the table above — and it is theatre for anything event-coupled. Buy the deal-list categories, ignore everything you could have bought today.

June 27 to 28 is the trough. Quiet, unadvertised, populated by inventory that missed. Deep stock, no crowd, recovering delivery estimates, and a customs backlog for cross-border lanes. Best prices of the month for anyone who can reach it.

Early July brings a second, weaker version of the same effect as post-event returns are processed and re-listed, plus the model-year changeovers that live on their own schedule regardless of consumer events. Bicycles, some outdoor equipment, and a slice of tooling all soften here.

Two things I want to look at properly in a later guide. First, bundle-and-single-unit pricing arithmetic deserves more than the note above — the practice of preserving a headline price by attaching inventory is now widespread enough that the unit-price comparison most people run is systematically misleading. Second, warranty terms as the highest information density on any listing — the length and structure of a warranty tells you what the manufacturer privately expects the failure rate to be, which is a more honest quality signal than any review section, and it is right there in the specifications where nobody reads it.

Bottom line

The advice to wait for June 23 is not exactly wrong, it is just imprecise in the way that costs money. A promotion is a date somebody chose. A trough is a condition that occurs. They coincide sometimes, and this month, for most of what you are actually shopping, they do not.

Three rules carry the whole guide. First, classify before you time. Props rise into the event and should be bought now. Substitutes — anything competing for the hours the tournament is taking — fall for a month and bottom quietly around June 27. The flat replacement-driven middle has no calendar at all, and no calendar means buy today, because nothing is coming and waiting is a cost with no offsetting benefit. Second, check shipping before you check price. Subtract transit and clearance from your need-by date, and if the resulting decision date lands before June 27, the trough does not exist for you and the debate is over. The slower your goods travel, the more expensive patience gets — which is the reverse of the usual advice and matters most to exactly the people who are usually told to be patient. Third, price the crowd. An advertised discount is worth what remains after the queue takes its cut in configuration, availability, and delivery risk. An unadvertised markdown into an empty room has no such deduction, which is most of why the quiet window beats the loud one.

Of today’s twenty picks, twelve are buys and eight are waits, and the split lands almost entirely along those lines rather than along price. Six of the ten direct-brand picks have no trough whatsoever, which is not a failure of the analysis — it is the analysis. Small-batch and made-to-order production cannot generate a discount because it cannot generate an overhang, so the only thing waiting buys you there is a later delivery date. Meanwhile the marketplace table’s most interesting rows are the three counter-cyclical waits that appear on nobody’s deal list and will never be announced anywhere.

If you take one thing away, take the ninety-second check. Shipping estimate, prop or substitute, name the mechanism, check the depth. Four questions, most of them answerable from the listing you already have open. The buyers who do well this month will not be the ones who found the best sale. They will be the ones who correctly worked out which of their purchases had no sale coming and stopped waiting for it.
