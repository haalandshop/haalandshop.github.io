Best Things to Buy Right Now — May 25, 2026

Quick take: It is the last Monday in May, a holiday in the United States, seventeen days before the tournament opens and twenty-nine before the June sale. Every guide published this week will rank products by how far the price has fallen. Yesterday’s argument was that a published spare parts list is the strongest disclosure a manufacturer makes about expected working life, because a parts diagram is expensive to maintain and impossible to fake. That signal has one hard limit: it tells you whether an object can survive year three, not whether anyone will still want it. This piece takes up the signal that answers the second question, and it is the only one no company publishes, controls, or can quietly edit before a sale. It is the second-hand price. What a three-year-old example sells for, as a fraction of what it cost new, is a verdict delivered by people who actually owned the thing, priced in a market with no marketing budget in it. Reviews are written after two weeks by people who are still excited. Resale prices are set after three years by people who are done. What follows is why the resale curve functions as an ownership review, why its shape matters more than its depth, which categories hold value and why, how to run the check in three minutes, and twenty picks sorted by what the secondary market has already concluded.

What’s Hot This Week

Three things are moving, and all three concern the used market rather than the new one. The holiday weekend has pulled a wave of listings onto the secondary platforms, which makes this an unusually good week to read resale data before the June discount cycle distorts it.

Holiday weekend listing volume is spiking on the secondary market

The pattern repeats every year and almost nobody trades on it. A long weekend is when households clean out garages, closets and spare rooms, and the result is a surge of second-hand listings that hits the platforms between Saturday and the following Wednesday. For a buyer researching a new purchase, this is the best available week to sample a resale curve, because the sample size is temporarily large enough to be meaningful. In a quiet week you might find four listings for a model, which tells you nothing. This week you may find forty, and forty listings with visible prices, conditions and time-on-market is a genuine dataset. The window closes fast: by mid-June the same searches will be polluted by sellers front-running the sale, and by July by people who bought during it and changed their minds.

Trade-in offers are climbing ahead of the June event

Retailers and brands raise trade-in credits in the weeks before a major sale, and the increase is usually presented as generosity. It is inventory strategy. A trade-in program does two things at once: it converts a hesitant customer into a buyer by lowering the effective new price, and it removes a used unit from the open market, which supports the price of the new one. The number to check is not how large the credit is, but how it compares to what the same item is actually selling for between private parties this week. When the trade-in credit is meaningfully below the private sale price, the program is buying your inconvenience at a discount, which is a fair trade if you value the convenience and a bad one if you do not. When the credit is above the private price, the brand is subsidising a sale, which is worth taking. Either way the comparison takes two minutes and almost nobody runs it.

Condition grading language is quietly diverging between platforms

The words used to describe used goods are not standardised, and the drift has accelerated. “Excellent” on one platform means functionally new with original packaging; on another it means working with visible wear. “Refurbished” can mean a factory rebuild with new wear components, a third-party clean and test, or a returned unit that was never opened and simply cannot be sold as new. The consequence is that you must compare like with like, and the labels will not do it for you. Ignore condition adjectives entirely and read the photographs and the described faults instead. A listing that says “excellent” with three shots of the same angle tells you less than one saying “good, small dent on the base, everything works” with eight. Specificity is the honest signal; adjectives are not.

The Resale Price Is an Ownership Review

Consider what has to happen for a used listing to exist at a particular price. Somebody bought the product, lived with it for a period of years, decided to part with it, and then had to find a number that a stranger would voluntarily pay. That number is constrained on both sides by people with real money at stake. The seller wants more and the buyer wants less, and the price that clears is the market’s best estimate of what the object is worth to somebody who knows exactly what it is like to own one.

Compare that to the instruments buyers normally use. A review score is produced by people who have owned the product for somewhere between a day and a month, who are unusually motivated to write either because they are delighted or because something failed immediately, and who are answering the question “do I like this?” rather than “what is this worth?” A warranty is a company’s own estimate of failure rates, priced to be profitable, and disclosed in language written by people whose job is to limit the disclosure. A brand reputation is an aggregate of decades-old impressions that may no longer describe anything the company currently manufactures. The resale price is the only widely available signal produced by people who have finished the ownership experience and are being asked to put a number on it.

There is a second property that makes it unusually trustworthy: it is adversarial to the seller of the new product. Every other signal in the buying process is either produced by the manufacturer, solicited by the manufacturer, or hosted on a platform that earns a commission when you buy. The used market is the one place where the manufacturer’s interests and the data’s direction are actively opposed. A company would prefer that its three-year-old products were worthless, because worthless old units mean the only way to have one is to buy a new one. When a used market values a product highly anyway, it has done so against the manufacturer’s commercial preference, which is exactly what makes it credible.

The third property is the one most buyers miss. A resale price prices in things nobody writes about: whether parts are still findable, whether software still updates, whether consumables are still made, whether repairs are economically sensible, whether the thing is pleasant to live with after the novelty wears off. No review can cover those, because when reviews are written none of them are knowable. The used market knows them because it is made of people who found out.

Depreciation Shape Beats Depreciation Depth

The mistake most people make when they start looking at resale data is to reduce it to one number: what percentage of the original price does it retain? That number is nearly useless on its own, because two products can retain forty percent after three years by completely different paths, and the paths mean opposite things.

The first path is a cliff. The product loses fifty-five percent in the first year, drifts down another five over the following two, and then flattens. This shape is the signature of a new-price premium rather than a quality problem. Something about the purchase was worth paying for at the moment of sale and is worth nothing afterwards: the packaging, the warranty period, the certainty of being first owner, or simply the retail markup. Once that premium is gone the object’s remaining value is its function, and the flat portion of the curve is the market saying the function is durable. A cliff followed by a plateau is a good sign, not a bad one, and it is an argument for buying that product used rather than avoiding it.

The second path is a slope. The product loses fifteen percent in year one, another fifteen in year two, another fifteen in year three, and it keeps going at that rate until it is worth nothing. This shape looks gentler and is much worse. A constant rate of decline means the market is continuously revising its estimate of remaining life downward, which is what happens when a product is visibly consumed by use, when the category is improving quickly enough that last year’s version is genuinely inferior, or when a known failure grows more likely with each passing year. A steady slope with no floor is the market telling you the object has a lifespan rather than a life.

The third path is a shelf that collapses. The product holds value for two or three years and then falls off a cliff late. This is almost always a support cliff: a software service ends, a subscription becomes mandatory, a battery family goes out of production, or a consumable is discontinued. It is the most dangerous shape for a buyer of a new unit, because the early data looks excellent. You see strong two-year retention, buy with confidence, and discover in year four that the shelf was held up by something with an expiry date.

The fourth path is the flat line from the beginning, and it is rare. The product loses ten or fifteen percent immediately, then essentially stops depreciating. This happens when the object’s function is unchanging, when it is not being improved in any meaningful way, when it is repairable indefinitely, and when there is a steady stream of buyers who want one and cannot get it new for much less. Products with flat curves are usually boring, usually simple, and usually the best purchases in their category by total cost.

The practical instruction from all four shapes is the same. Do not ask what percentage the product retains. Ask what the curve is doing at the point where you would sell it, and ask what is holding it up. A number tells you where the market is. The shape tells you where it is going and why.

Rimowa and Mammut both show deep used markets with flat curves, and they split on what is holding the price up. An aluminium case is a shell of soft alloy that dents on the first trip and then never gets meaningfully worse, which means the visible condition of a ten-year-old example and a two-year-old example converge. The shell jacket has an equally deep market and an equally flat price, but the flatness is brand demand rather than durability. A flat curve is only good news when the thing holding it flat is the object rather than the label on it.

Vaxee and Titan Army both have thin listings and steep curves, and the verdicts diverge on what the steepness costs you. A steep curve on an inexpensive object is close to meaningless in absolute terms. If a mouse loses seventy percent of its value over three years, the loss is a small number of dollars, and the thin listing count reflects the fact that owners who like theirs simply keep using it until the switches wear out rather than bothering to sell. Low residual on a low base is an accounting curiosity, not a cost. The monitor is the same two readings with a much larger denominator and a real mechanism behind it. Panels dim and develop uniformity problems, specifications advance every season so that a two-year-old panel is genuinely behind, and thin listings mean that when you do want to exit there may be no buyer at any price. Steepness matters in proportion to the sum at risk, and a percentage without a dollar figure attached is not a decision input.

Thermalright and Growatt both have used prices pinned to the new-price war, and they split on whether the object underneath is stable. An air cooler is a stack of aluminium fins, a set of sealed heat pipes and a fan. Nothing in that assembly wears out except the fan, the fan is a standard size available from a dozen makers, and the thermal performance of a five-year-old unit is identical to a new one. The used price tracks the new price because the new price is already very low, not because the product deteriorates, so buying either new or used is defensible and the downside is bounded. The inverter looks similar on the price chart and is a completely different risk. Its used price tracks the new price because the category is in a continuous price war driven by rapid iteration, and behind that are electrolytic capacitors with finite service lives, firmware tied to a manufacturer cloud, and warranty terms that generally do not survive a change of owner or installation. When the used price and the new price fall together, find out whether that is because the product is cheap or because the product is aging.

Onkyo and Vari both show listings dominated by faulty and partial units, for reasons that point in opposite directions. An AV receiver of a certain vintage is notorious for a specific board failure, and the second-hand market reflects it precisely: a large share of listings are described as no-output, intermittent, or sold for parts. That is the market documenting a known defect more honestly than any review round-up ever did, and the correct response is to treat the faulty-listing ratio as a defect report. The desk frame produces a superficially identical pattern for an entirely benign reason. Used desk listings are overwhelmingly “frame only, no top”, because the top is a large awkward slab that is not worth shipping and is trivially replaced with any flat surface. A market full of partial units is bad news when the missing part is the expensive one and good news when the missing part is the cheap one. Here the frame is the engineered component, the motors and controller are the value, and the absent top is a commodity.

Aquasonic and Azmaya both have essentially no secondary market, and the absence means completely different things. A rechargeable toothbrush has no used market because hygiene rules it out and because the sealed cell that fails in year three cannot be replaced, so a three-year-old unit is not merely unsellable but genuinely worthless. The absence is a verdict. A hand-finished copper kitchen tool has no used market because the people who buy them do not sell them, and the ones that do change hands do so within households rather than on platforms. Nothing about the object degrades: copper takes a patina and keeps working, the tool has no assemblies, and a fifty-year-old example is a functioning example. Absence of listings is ambiguous on its face and resolves the moment you ask whether the missing listings reflect retention or rejection. The test: if a used one existed in good condition, would you want it? For the toothbrush, no at any price. For the copper tool, yes at nearly any price — which is precisely why none are available.

Best Direct-to-Consumer and Brand Picks

Ten origins, ten categories, none overlapping the table above. Before reading it, note the structural distinction that makes this a genuinely separate measurement rather than the same one with different brands.

Every value in the marketplace table describes the observable residue of transactions between strangers. “Deep used market, flat curve”, “thin listings, steep curve”, “used price tracks new discounts”, “parts-only and faulty units listed” and “no secondary market exists” are all statements about what happens to be visible in a public listing database at a given moment. Each is produced by the aggregate behaviour of thousands of private sellers, none of whom coordinate, none of whom are accountable to the manufacturer, and none of whom are trying to send a signal. The data is honest precisely because nobody is authoring it.

The values in the table below are the opposite kind of claim. “Factory refurbished sold in-house”, “warranty transfers to second owner”, “discontinued models kept in the archive”, “warranty void on resale” and “trade-in credit against new” all describe a manufacturer’s chosen posture toward the second owner — a deliberate, revocable, published policy decision made by the party that controls the brand. A marketplace reseller cannot decide whether a warranty transfers, cannot maintain a model archive for products it did not design, cannot operate a factory refurbishment line, and cannot offer trade-in credit against a catalogue it does not own. Run the logic in the other direction and it holds equally: “thin listings, steep curve” is a meaningless thing to say about a brand’s own website, because a brand’s site has no listings and no curve. It has one price, set by one party.

These are two halves of the same question asked of two different witnesses. The marketplace tells you what the used market has concluded about the product. The brand tells you whether it intends to help or hinder that market. A product with a strong resale market and a brand that supports second owners is compounding in your favour. A product with a strong resale market and a brand actively suppressing it is a value that exists despite the maker and may not survive the next policy revision.

Freitag and Cecotec both operate refurbishment channels, and the split is on why the channel exists. A bag made from used truck tarpaulin begins its life already weathered, which means the concept of condition degradation barely applies and a refurbished unit is not a compromised version of a new one. The brand’s willingness to take bags back, recondition them and resell them is coherent with the product rather than bolted onto it, and the effect on resale is structural: a manufacturer that participates in its own secondary market puts a floor under the price. The appliance refurbishment channel looks like the same policy and is generated by a different force. High-volume countertop appliances sold at aggressive prices produce high return rates, and a refurbishment channel is the economically rational way to process returns that cannot legally be sold as new. A refurbishment programme born of a return problem tells you about the return rate, not about the durability, and the tell is whether the refurbished catalogue is deep in recent models. A refurb channel full of last month’s product is a returns pipeline.

Hultafors and Rabbit Air both offer guarantees that follow the object rather than the buyer, and the value of that differs enormously. A hand tool guarantee that requires no registration and no proof of original purchase effectively transfers on resale, and for a product whose failure modes are handle cracks and edge damage this materially supports second-hand prices, because a used buyer inherits the same protection as a new one. The transferability is cheap for the manufacturer and valuable to the market, which is the ideal shape for a policy. The air purifier presents a transferable multi-year warranty on a unit whose real economics are elsewhere: the filter stack is proprietary, must be replaced on a schedule, and over five years costs a substantial fraction of the machine. A warranty that transfers is worth exactly as much as the repairs it would cover, and on a product whose dominant cost is a consumable the coverage misses the expense entirely. A used buyer is inheriting a subscription with a warranty attached.

Persol and Untouched World both depend on archival continuity, and only one of them maintains it. Eyewear built on long-running model families with persistent model numbers produces a legible used market, because a buyer can identify exactly what is being sold, match lens and part references, and price against known variants. That legibility is a manufacturer decision — keeping discontinued models documented, keeping numbering stable across decades, keeping lens fitment public — and it is the reason forty-year-old frames trade actively while visually similar frames from brands that renumber every season do not. Seasonal knitwear inverts this. The garments may be excellent, the fibre may be genuinely rare, and the construction may outlast the buyer, but collections are keyed to seasons, product pages vanish when the season closes, and a used listing two years later has nothing to reference. A used market cannot price what it cannot identify, and an archive is the cheapest thing a brand can do to support its own resale value.

Nordisk and Smartmi both void coverage on transfer, and the consequence depends on whether the warranty was doing any work. A cotton canvas tent is a set of panels, seams, poles and guy points. Everything on it is field-repairable with materials sold in any outdoor shop, canvas is patchable indefinitely, and a competent repair is invisible within a season of weathering. A non-transferable warranty on that object costs a second owner almost nothing, because the warranty was never the mechanism keeping the tent alive. The bladeless fan is the opposite: a sealed motor assembly, a control board, and an app dependency, with no user-serviceable path and no independent repair trade. Strip the warranty and the second owner holds an object with no recourse of any kind. Non-transferable coverage is a rounding error on a repairable product and the whole ballgame on a sealed one, which is why the same policy line produces opposite verdicts.

Reima and Tatonka both offer credit against new purchases in exchange for old items, and the split is on how the credit compares to the open market. Children’s outerwear is the strongest possible case for a structured trade-in, because the garment is outgrown long before it is worn out, the supply of nearly-new items is enormous, and private resale of children’s clothing is tedious relative to its value. A credit that saves the parent the listing, the photographs and the shipping is buying genuine inconvenience, and the object is being recirculated at the point where it still has most of its life left. An expedition backpack is the weak case. A well-made pack has a deep, active private market with knowledgeable buyers who pay real money for a decade-old model in good condition, and a trade-in credit will almost always sit below that price because the brand must cover handling and resale risk. Take the credit when private resale is not worth your time; refuse it when the open market pays more and finding a buyer takes an afternoon.

Which Categories Hold Value, and Why

Retention is not random across categories, and the pattern is explicable from a small number of structural properties. Knowing which properties apply lets you predict a resale curve for a product you have never researched.

Products hold value when their function is not improving. A cast iron pan, a hand plane, a mechanical timepiece, a canvas tent and a steel bicycle frame all perform the same task today that they performed decades ago. There is no annual improvement cycle for a buyer to wait out, so a used example is not an inferior version of anything, it is the same version. Categories under rapid technical improvement have the opposite property built in: last year’s model is genuinely worse, and the used price must fall to reflect it. Ask whether next year’s version will be meaningfully better. If the honest answer is no, the curve will flatten.

Products hold value when the failure modes are serviceable and the parts exist. This is where yesterday’s argument connects directly to this one. A used buyer is pricing remaining life, and remaining life is a function of what can be fixed. When wear items are replaceable and available, a worn example is a repairable example and the price reflects the cost of repair rather than the cost of replacement. When the wear item is sealed, the used price must collapse to near zero the moment failure becomes likely, because a buyer is purchasing an unknown remaining fraction of a non-renewable resource.

Products hold value when there is a supply constraint. Discontinued models, limited runs and small-volume makers produce markets where demand exceeds supply. This is the least reliable property for a buyer of a new item, because scarcity can evaporate and paying a premium in anticipation of it is speculation rather than shopping. Treat supply constraint as a bonus you may discover, never as a reason to buy.

Products lose value when ownership carries an ongoing obligation. Anything requiring a subscription, a proprietary consumable, a cloud service or an account transfer depresses its own resale price, because the second owner inherits a cost stream alongside the object. Ask what a new owner must sign up for before the thing works. Every answer beyond “nothing” reduces the price a rational buyer will pay.

Products lose value when hygiene, fit or personalisation limit the buyer pool. Mattresses, personal care devices, earphones and custom-sized items face a structurally small market regardless of build quality. That is not a criticism of them. It is a reason to price them as pure consumption and stop expecting a residual the category cannot provide.

Products lose value when the brand suppresses the used market. Serial blocking, refusing service on second-hand units, voiding entitlements on transfer and account-locking hardware all reduce resale value by design. The effect on you as first buyer is direct: your exit price is lower because the manufacturer wants it lower.

The Three-Minute Resale Check

This is the whole method, and it fits in the time it takes to make coffee. Run it before you buy anything expensive enough that you would notice losing the money.

Find the three-year-old equivalent, not the current model

Search where the category actually trades — general marketplaces for most things, specialist forums for tools and audio, dedicated apps for clothing — and search the model from about three years ago rather than the one you are considering. Three years is the useful horizon: past the initial cliff, past most warranties, far enough in that early failures have surfaced. If you cannot identify what the three-year-old model was called, that is itself a result: a product line whose history is untraceable will produce a used market that cannot price it.

Read sold prices, not asking prices, and count the listings

Asking prices are aspirations and include listings that will never sell. Where the platform exposes completed sales, filter to those immediately, because the gap is often forty percent and always in the same direction. Then count. Under five results is an anecdote, not data, and you should widen to the model family. Above twenty you have a real distribution, and its shape matters as much as its centre: a tight cluster means the market agrees on value, a wide scatter means condition dominates the model name.

Ask what is holding the floor, or what removed it

This is the step that turns data into a decision, and it is the one people skip. Having established that the three-year-old unit sells for some fraction of new, ask the single question why that number and not a different one. If the answer is that the object still works perfectly and buyers know it, the curve will stay flat and the product is a sound purchase. If the answer is that a known failure is approaching, that support is ending, or that the current model is substantially better, you have found the mechanism and you can decide whether it will apply to you. If you cannot construct an answer at all, do not treat the number as informative. A resale price you cannot explain is a coincidence, not a signal, and the discipline of demanding an explanation is what separates this from horoscope reading with spreadsheets.

Why a Strong Used Market Makes New Cheaper

There is a counterintuitive conclusion buried in all of this, and it is the most financially useful part of the piece. A product with a strong second-hand market is cheaper to own than an equivalent product without one, even if you never sell it. Four separate mechanisms produce this, and they compound.

The first is the obvious one. The true cost of owning something is the purchase price minus what you recover, and an item that costs more but retains value can easily cost less over the ownership period than a cheaper item that retains nothing. Buyers compare purchase prices because those appear on the label, and ignore recovery because it happens years later to a version of themselves that feels hypothetical.

The second operates even if you never sell, and it is repair economics. A functioning used market means parts donors exist, independent repairers know the model, and a repair quote can be judged against a real replacement price rather than a retail one. Often the cheapest fix is buying a second-hand unit for parts. Products with no used market have no donors and no repair trade, so every fault is priced against a new one at retail.

The third is optionality, worth more than people think. A liquid resale market means you can change your mind: buy the more expensive option, discover it is wrong, and exit for most of your money. That changes which purchases are sensible, because trying the better version becomes a small bet rather than a commitment. The ability to reverse a decision has value even in the years you never use it.

The fourth is information, and it is why this method works at all. A product with an active used market is one about which a great deal is publicly known, because thousands of people have described condition, faults and quirks in listings and the conversations around them. You are buying into accumulated knowledge. A product with no used market has none, and every problem you meet you meet alone.

Put the four together and the conclusion is not “buy things that hold value so you can sell them”. It is buy things that hold value because the same properties that produce resale value also produce a better ownership experience. Resale value is not a separate feature. It is a measurement of durability, repairability, support and design coherence, taken by a market that has no incentive to flatter.

Thin Listings: Retention or Rejection

The commonest error in reading resale data is misreading the absence of listings, and it deserves isolating because it flips the conclusion completely. Two products can show almost no second-hand supply for opposite reasons.

Retention scarcity happens when owners do not sell because they do not want to. The object works, replacing it would cost more than keeping it, and there is no upgrade worth having. Hand tools, good cookware and simple mechanical goods all show this. The market is thin because nothing is leaving households, and the few examples that appear sell fast at strong prices. The tell is speed and price: listings that vanish within days near retail.

Rejection scarcity happens when owners cannot sell because nobody will buy. The object has failed, become obsolete, or sits in a category hygiene or transfer rules have closed. The few listings that exist sit unsold for months and drift downward. The tell is the mirror image: listings that age and reprice.

Distinguishing them takes thirty seconds once you know to look. Sort by recently sold and check time-on-market. Fast sales at high prices mean people are holding on. Slow sales at falling prices mean people are staying away. The same empty search page supports both a strong buy and a hard avoid, and the only thing separating them is whether the missing listings were never created or never cleared.

What the Spread Between Asking and Sold Tells You

One more reading worth learning, because it is quick and it exposes something no other signal does. Compare what sellers ask to what buyers actually pay, and pay attention to the size of the gap.

A narrow spread means the market is confident. Buyers and sellers agree on value, which happens when the product is well understood, condition is easy to assess, and volume is sufficient for a consensus. That is good news for a new buyer, because your eventual exit price is predictable.

A wide spread means condition dominates the model name. When the same model sells anywhere from a quarter to three quarters of retail, individual history matters enormously — these products are frequently abused, badly repaired, or sold in states the listing does not disclose. That is a warning about the category rather than the brand, and it argues for buying new if you buy at all.

A spread that is wide and persistently unresolved — many listings, few sales, prices anchored high for months — means sellers are anchored to what they paid and the market has moved without them. This is the signature of a category in decline, and the sparse sold prices are the honest number.

Where This Read Breaks Down

The resale signal is the strongest one available, and it has four failure modes that a careful buyer should hold in mind.

Thin categories give unreliable data. Niche products and regional brands do not generate enough transactions to produce a meaningful curve, and reading a price from four listings is not analysis. Widen to the category rather than the model, accept the read is weaker, and lean on parts and repair signals instead.

Brand demand can prop up a curve durability does not deserve. Some products retain value because a badge is desirable, not because the object endures — the shell jacket above is exactly this. A curve held up by fashion is genuinely flat right up until the fashion moves, at which point it becomes the third shape and collapses late. Ask what fraction of retained value is function and what fraction is name.

Recent launches have no history by definition. Anything under about two years old cannot be read this way, which is the most common practical limitation, since new products are exactly what people research. The workaround is to read the predecessor. Makers rarely change their approach to durability and support between generations, and a brand whose last three lines all collapsed in year three is telling you what this one will do.

Regional markets diverge, sometimes wildly. Resale value is local. Duty, voltage, plug types, warranty geography, repair availability and attitudes toward second-hand goods all vary, and a product with a strong used market in one country may have none in another. Check the market you would actually sell in.

Category Notes

Luggage and travel goods. The most reliable category for this read. Wear is visible, models persist for years, failure points are mechanical and replaceable, and the buyer pool is knowledgeable. Metal hard-shells outperform polycarbonate for a simple reason: dents are cosmetic while cracks are terminal.

Outdoor and technical clothing. Deeply split. Mechanical items — packs, tents, hardware, wool layers — hold value on function and repairability. Anything depending on a laminate has a hidden clock, and the used price sits systematically too high relative to remaining life because the degradation is invisible in photographs. Buy the mechanical layer used and the membrane layer new.

Computer components. Anything with no wear parts and a stable standard holds up in real terms: coolers, cases, fans, keyboards, cabling. Anything with a panel, a battery or a rapid specification cycle does not. The dividing line is not price but whether the part is subject to an improvement race.

Home appliances. Governed almost entirely by parts availability and consumable lock-in. Machines with open consumables retain value steadily. Machines with proprietary cartridges, filters or pods depreciate faster than their build quality suggests, because the second owner is buying an obligation.

Audio equipment. The clearest flat-line curve in consumer goods. Amplification and speakers built to unchanging physics trade actively for decades. Anything with an integrated video standard or a board with a known failure follows the late-collapse shape instead, and the collapse arrives when the standard moves.

Kitchen tools and cookware. Retention scarcity in its purest form. Monolithic objects in iron, copper and carbon steel have almost no listings and very high prices when they appear. The absence of a market here is the strongest possible endorsement — exactly the reading that trips up buyers who treat listing count as demand.

Children’s goods. Structurally the best category for trade-in programmes, because items are outgrown rather than worn out. Resale values are high relative to remaining life and supply is constant. Buy well here; you will recover more than in any other category.

Policy, Logistics and Cross-Border Notes

Warranty geography survives resale badly. Even where a warranty transfers between owners, it frequently does not transfer between countries. A unit bought in one market and resold into another commonly lands with a buyer holding coverage on paper and no service centre willing to honour it. Check where the service obligation actually sits before treating a warranty as part of the value.

Grey imports depress resale predictably. Units brought in outside official distribution sell for less second-hand, because buyers know service will be refused. The discount you take today from an unofficial channel is partly borrowed from your exit price later. That can still be the right trade, but it should be a decision rather than a surprise.

Shipping economics decide which categories trade internationally. Heavy or low-value-density goods have local markets only, because shipping exceeds residual value. Small, dense, high-value items — lenses, watches, tools, audio components — trade globally and therefore have deeper and more informative used markets. Adjust your expectations for bulky categories rather than concluding the product is unwanted.

Platform fees are part of the exit price. Selling costs commission, payment processing and shipping. A gross resale price is not what you receive. Subtract a realistic fee load from any recovery figure, because on cheap items it can consume the entire advantage.

Holiday timing affects both ends. Used supply spikes around long weekends and thins before major sales as sellers wait for the retail price to reset. If you are buying used, this week is favourable. If you are selling before the June event, sell now: a large retail discount drags every used price down with it within days.

What’s Coming Next

The resale curve tells you what the market concluded after the fact. It is retrospective by nature, which means it works beautifully for established products and not at all for anything launched recently — the fourth failure mode above, and the one that bites most often, because new products are exactly what people are trying to research. Bottom line

It is the last Monday in May, a holiday weekend, seventeen days before a tournament and twenty-nine before a sale. Used listings are unusually plentiful this week and the discount noise has not yet started. That combination will not repeat before July.

Three things follow. First, check the three-year-old price before you check the review score. One of those numbers was set by people who owned the product long enough to be finished with it, in a market where being wrong costs them money. The other was written by people two weeks in. Both are free to look up and they take about the same amount of time, but only one of them has been tested by anyone’s wallet.

Second, read the shape and not the percentage. A product that drops hard and then holds is a good product with a retail premium attached, and the sensible move is often to buy it used. A product that declines steadily forever is being consumed, whatever the badge says. A product that holds beautifully for three years and then falls off a cliff has an expiry date you have not found yet, and finding it is the most valuable ten minutes you will spend.

Third, when there are no listings, ask which kind of nothing you are looking at. An empty search page means either that nobody will sell or that nobody will buy, and those are opposite conclusions reached through identical evidence. Sort by sold, look at how fast things moved, and the ambiguity resolves immediately. Get that distinction right and you avoid the two largest errors at once: dismissing an excellent product because nobody parts with it, and buying a poor one because the listings looked reassuringly scarce.

The parts list told you whether the object can survive. The resale curve tells you whether it deserved to.
