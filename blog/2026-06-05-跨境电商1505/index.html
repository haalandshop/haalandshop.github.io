Best Things to Buy Right Now — June 5, 2026

Quick take: Almost every piece of shopping advice ever written assumes something that is not true: that a product has a price. It does not. It has a price for you, generated at the moment you loaded the page, from a short list of things the platform believes about you — where you are standing, what device you are holding, how long your account has existed, and what you have already told it by browsing. Two people can open the same listing in the same minute and be quoted amounts that differ by more than the discount they are both waiting for. That is always somewhat true. It is unusually true today, six days before the tournament opens on June 11 and eighteen days before the June sale opens on June 23, because this is the last stretch of the season when platforms are still spending money to acquire buyers rather than simply harvesting the ones who show up. Welcome coupons are generous right now. App-only vouchers are stacked. Membership tiers are being pushed hard because a member acquired in early June is a member through the entire second half. In eleven days none of that is necessary, because demand arrives free, and the acquisition budget quietly moves elsewhere. So the useful question this week is not “is this a good price.” It is one level upstream: is this even my price, or am I looking at the number set for somebody else? Because if you are looking at a gated price you have not unlocked, no amount of waiting fixes it — the June sale will cut a number you were never being quoted. And if you are looking at an ungated price, the opposite applies: there is no lever for you to pull, so the only thing that can move it is a scheduled promotion, and patience is genuinely the strategy. The rest of this guide is how to tell those two situations apart in about ninety seconds, a worked example where the same listing produces a sixty-one dollar spread, and twenty picks sorted by which kind of buyer the price was written for.

What’s Hot This Week

The week’s demand is being pulled in two directions at once and they do not overlap much. On one side, six days out from the tournament, everything that touches watching a match with other people is moving: projectors, seating, coolers, outdoor cooking, anything that turns a garden into a venue. On the other, a much quieter set of categories is moving for reasons that have nothing to do with June at all — hand tools, garden equipment, kitchen replacements, the things people buy when the weather is settled and the weekend is free. The first group is loud, advertised, and priced for an audience. The second group is not, which is exactly why it is where the honest numbers are.

What has changed since last weekend is the promotional posture rather than the prices themselves. Acquisition offers are visibly wider. First-order codes that were ten percent in May are fifteen now. App-only vouchers that used to require a threshold have dropped the threshold. Membership trials that were seven days are thirty. None of this is generosity; it is a calculation about lifetime value made by people who know that a customer captured on June 5 will be present for the tournament, the June sale, and the back-to-school run, while a customer captured on June 24 has already spent their money.

The Categories Being Priced for an Audience

Audio-visual, seating, cooling, and outdoor cooking are all being priced with one eye on the eleventh. That does not automatically mean they are expensive — it means the number you see has been chosen deliberately by a person, and deliberate numbers move in planned steps rather than drifting. In these categories the segmentation is unusually visible: brand stores holding a clean reference price while the same item at a reseller carries a stackable voucher, or a member price that is not shown at all until you sign in. If you find yourself comparing across sellers in these categories without being logged in, you are comparing fictions.

The Categories Nobody Is Watching

Tools, garden, tableware, stationery, small kitchen equipment, replacement parts. These have no event exposure, so nobody has written a June plan for them, so their prices are whatever the last automated adjustment left behind. They are also where acquisition offers land hardest, because a platform trying to win a new customer would much rather do it on a twenty-eight dollar pruning shear than on a four-hundred-dollar television. The best value available to a new account this week is almost always in a category the new account was not shopping for. That is worth sitting with for a second, because it inverts the usual advice about waiting for sales on expensive things.

You Are Not the Buyer This Price Was Set For

Start with the mechanic, because once it is visible you cannot unsee it. A modern listing does not store one number. It stores a base number and a set of rules that adjust it, and the rules take inputs from your session. Some of those adjustments are explicit and visible — a coupon you can see and click, a member badge, a first-order banner. Others are not shown at all, they simply resolve into the figure printed on the page. The visible ones feel like offers. The invisible ones feel like the price.

This is not a conspiracy and it is mostly not even predatory. A platform running in forty countries genuinely cannot show one number, because duties, local taxes, currency, and shipping cost differ. A brand genuinely does want to pay more to acquire a first customer than to serve an existing one, because the first purchase is the expensive one. A retailer genuinely does reserve its sharpest pricing for the membership programme it is trying to grow. Each individual rule is defensible. The result of all of them running at once is that the phrase “the price of this item” has quietly stopped meaning anything specific.

What follows from that is a change in the order of operations. The conventional sequence is: find the item, check the price, decide whether to buy or wait. The corrected sequence puts one step before all of it: establish which price you are being shown, and whether a better one is available to a version of you that you could become in two minutes. Sometimes the answer is no, and that is genuinely useful information — it means the number is real, the comparison is honest, and you can go back to thinking about the product. Sometimes the answer is yes, and then the entire buy-or-wait question changes shape, because the thing standing between you and a lower number is not eighteen days of patience, it is a checkbox.

The timing matters because gated prices are not stable. A gate is a depreciating asset. The welcome coupon that exists today exists because the platform needs customers today. On June 23, when demand walks in the door unassisted, it does not need to buy anybody, and the acquisition offers get quietly narrowed — smaller percentages, higher thresholds, category exclusions, or simply removed. Meanwhile the ungated public price does the opposite: it is the one that gets cut for the event, because a public event needs a public number to cut. Those two curves cross somewhere in the next fortnight, and which side of the crossing you should be standing on depends entirely on which kind of price your item carries.

The Four Inputs That Decide Which Price You See

There are more than four, but four of them account for nearly all of the variance you will actually encounter, and all four are checkable in seconds.

Where You Are Standing

The most powerful input and the least discussed. Your delivery region determines which storefront serves you, which catalogue that storefront carries, which taxes are embedded, and which fulfilment network is quoted. Two of those are legitimate cost differences and two are not. A twenty percent gap between a European and a North American storefront is mostly tax and duty, and chasing it usually produces a customs bill that eats the saving. But a gap between two regions with similar tax regimes is a pricing decision, and it is often large in exactly the categories where shipping is cheap relative to value — audio, optics, small electronics, anything dense and light. The tell is simple: change the delivery postcode before you do anything else and watch whether the number moves more than the shipping line does.

What You Are Holding

App versus browser is the crudest form of segmentation and still one of the widest. Apps get exclusive vouchers because installs are a measurable business objective and browser sessions are not. The gap is rarely enormous on a single item but it compounds through a cart, and in a few categories — anything sold heavily through short video, anything with a young buyer profile — the app-only number is the real number and the browser number is a placeholder nobody expects to be paid. Beyond app-versus-web there is a second layer: mobile browsers sometimes see different promotional slots than desktop ones, because the promotional inventory is sized to the screen. That one is small but free to check.

How Long You Have Existed

Account age is the input that most reliably produces a worse price for the loyal customer, which is counterintuitive enough that most people never test it. A six-year account with a long purchase history is not eligible for the first-order code, the welcome bundle, the reactivation offer, or the win-back voucher. It is eligible for the loyalty programme, which is usually worth less. The honest accounting is that the long-standing account gets better service — faster support escalation, more generous return adjudication, occasional goodwill — and a worse headline number. Whether that trade is good depends entirely on whether you are buying something likely to go wrong.

What You Have Already Told Them

Browsing is disclosure. Viewing an item four times, adding it to a cart, or returning to it across sessions all move you into segments with names like high-intent, and high-intent segments do not receive the offers designed to create intent. This is the mechanism behind the widely reported and widely misunderstood experience of a price rising after repeated visits. Usually the base number has not moved at all; what has happened is that the offer attached to it has been withdrawn, because the system has concluded you will buy anyway. The countermeasure is unglamorous and effective: do your comparison in a session that has not been told anything, and only then log in to transact.

The $61 Gap on a Single Listing

Take one item, a mid-range bookshelf speaker pair listed at $229, and put four buyers in front of it at the same moment on a Friday morning.

Buyer one is on a desktop browser, logged out, delivery region set to a large metropolitan area with same-day coverage. She sees $229, free delivery, no coupon banner. This is the base price, the honest public number, the one that a review site would quote and the one a price-tracking chart would record.

Buyer two is on the app, logged in, account created eleven days ago. He sees $229 with a fifteen-dollar new-customer voucher applied automatically at checkout and a further eight dollars available for choosing a slower delivery window. His real number is $206. He has done nothing clever; he simply has not existed for very long.

Buyer three is a six-year account with two hundred orders, logged in on the same app. She sees $229, no voucher, no banner, and a small note that she has enough points for $4 off. Her real number is $225. She is the platform’s best customer and she is being quoted nineteen dollars more than the eleven-day-old account.

Buyer four is logged out, on a browser, with the delivery region set to a rural postcode outside the same-day network. He sees $229 plus a $9 remote-area surcharge that does not appear for the other three, and no free-delivery threshold applies because the threshold is regional. His real number is $238. Between him and buyer two there is a $32 spread on the identical object from the identical seller in the identical minute, and if you add the sharper case — buyer two also clipping a stackable category coupon worth a further $29 that appears only in the app’s voucher centre and only for accounts under thirty days old — the spread reaches $61.

Now notice what none of these four buyers should do. None of them should wait for the June sale on the strength of this listing. A twelve percent event discount on $229 is about $27. Buyer two has already beaten that by twenty-three dollars without waiting eighteen days. Buyer four will still be paying the surcharge on June 23. Buyer three’s points balance will not have grown. The event is not the largest number on this page and it never was; the largest number on this page is the difference between the buyers.

The Same Two Carts, Inverted by Account Age

Run the comparison the other way and it stops being a story about clever coupon stacking, because there is a real cost to being the eleven-day-old account.

Consider two identical carts: the speakers above, a $79 kitchen item, and a $34 consumable. Buyer two, the new account, pays roughly $261 after vouchers. Buyer three, the six-year account, pays roughly $327. On price alone it is not close.

Then something goes wrong, which it does on roughly one order in fifteen. The kitchen item arrives with a cracked housing. Buyer three opens a return, is offered an instant refund before the item ships back, keeps the broken unit pending collection, and has a replacement in two days because her account carries a returns-history score that unlocks the fast path. Buyer two opens the same return, is asked for photographs, waits for adjudication, ships the item back at his own cost because the damage is classed as ambiguous, and is refunded eleven days later. His fifteen-dollar voucher is not reinstated, because vouchers are consumed on order placement, so his replacement purchase is at the full public price. The sixty-six dollar advantage becomes roughly a nineteen dollar advantage, and he has spent two weeks on it.

That inversion is the honest version of this whole framework. Segmentation is not a game where the cleverest player wins. It is a set of trades. New accounts are bought with money and paid for in friction. Old accounts are kept with service and charged for it in cash. The right move is not to always be the new account; it is to know which one you are on any given purchase and to buy the things that suit it. New-account pricing is worth spending on durable, low-failure-rate, well-understood objects where you will never contact support. Long-account standing is worth spending on complicated things, expensive things, and anything with a motor, a compressor, a battery, or a firmware update in its future.

Building a Segment Read in Ninety Seconds

Four checks, ordered so that most items terminate after the first or second and you stop.

Check one: does the number survive a clean session? Open the same listing in a private window with no login. If the price is identical, you are almost certainly looking at an ungated public number and checks two through four will find nothing. Stop here and go think about the product. If it differs, note the direction — a lower logged-out price means an offer is being withheld from you, a higher one means something is being applied for you and you should find out what before it lapses.

Check two: is there a voucher surface you have not opened? Nearly every large platform has a coupon or voucher area that is separate from the product page and does not advertise itself on the listing. It is worth thirty seconds because the offers held there are frequently category-wide, stackable with item-level discounts, and time-boxed in a way that is not visible on the page. Read the eligibility line, not the headline percentage. “New customers” and “first order in this category” are different conditions and only one of them excludes you.

Check three: does the postcode move it? Change the delivery region to a different major city and reload. What you are looking for is not the shipping fee, which is expected to move, but the item price, the delivery-window options, and whether a threshold appears or disappears. If the item price itself changes, you have found a regional segment, and regional segments do not respond to events — a June 23 discount applied to a higher regional base is still a higher number.

Check four: what does the brand’s own site say? This is the control experiment. The brand almost always shows one price to everybody in a given region and it is usually the ceiling. If the marketplace number is below it, the marketplace is spending money to win the sale and there is likely more to unlock. If the marketplace number is above it, you have found the most common and most expensive error in modern retail: buying through a channel that has quietly become the wrong one for that product.

If all four checks come back flat, the item is ungated. That is a genuinely useful finding, because ungated items are the only ones where “wait for the sale” is a coherent plan.

Stacking: The Order of Operations Matters

Once you have found more than one lever, the order you pull them in changes the total, and most people pull them in the worst possible order without noticing.

The general rule is that percentage discounts should be applied to the largest number and fixed-value vouchers to the smallest, and platforms apply them in whatever order their checkout logic specifies, which is usually the order that costs them least. Where you have a choice — and you often do, through which items you put in which order, or whether you split a cart — the arithmetic is worth thirty seconds. A fifteen percent code and a twenty-dollar voucher on a $200 cart produce $150 if the percentage applies first and $153 if the voucher does. That is small. On a $600 cart with a twenty percent code and a fifty-dollar voucher the gap is $10, and the reason to care is that the same logic applies to every order you place for the rest of the year.

The bigger stacking decision is whether to split the cart at all. Splitting costs you shipping thresholds and consolidation but it lets you apply first-order or category-restricted offers that would otherwise be diluted. The break-even is straightforward: split when the restricted offer is worth more than the shipping you lose, which for most thresholds means splitting once the restricted saving exceeds roughly ten dollars. Below that, consolidate and stop thinking about it.

There is also a sequencing question across time rather than across carts. Acquisition offers are typically once-per-account, so spending one is irreversible. Spending a fifteen percent first-order code on a twenty-eight dollar item to save four dollars is not thrift, it is destroying an asset. If you know a larger purchase is coming inside the offer’s validity window, hold the code for it. If you do not know, the expected-value answer is to use it on the largest thing in front of you today, because unspent codes expire far more often than large purchases materialise.

The last piece is cashback and card-level rewards, which sit outside the platform’s stacking logic entirely and therefore always stack. They are also the only lever in this entire article that is completely invisible to the seller and cannot be withdrawn from you specifically. Their weakness is timing — most pay out on a lag long enough that a return unwinds them awkwardly — so they are worth most on the things you are certain about and worth least on the things you might send back.

Where This Read Breaks Down

Five honest counter-cases, because a framework you cannot break is a framework you cannot trust.

Price-controlled items show no variance at all, and that is not a finding. In categories with enforced minimum advertised pricing, every check in the ninety-second read comes back flat, and it will look like you have proven the price is honest and public. What you have actually proven is that the seller is not permitted to move it. The distinction matters because MAP items sometimes carry value elsewhere — in bundled accessories, extended warranties, or cart-level discounts that are technically not advertised prices. Flat is not the same as final.

Segment-hopping can cost more than it saves. Creating a new account to capture a first-order offer breaks the link between the purchase and your history, which is the thing that unlocks fast returns, warranty escalation, and goodwill. On most platforms it also violates the terms you agreed to, and enforcement is inconsistent but real. For a durable, simple object this trade is fine. For anything you might need help with, it is a bad trade dressed as a good one.

A lot of observed variance is test noise, not segmentation. Platforms run continuous experiments, and a price difference you observe between two sessions may simply be two arms of a test that will be concluded and reverted within days. The tell is stability: check twice, several hours apart. A real segment persists. A test arm often does not. Acting decisively on a single observation is how people convince themselves of pricing patterns that do not exist.

Regional gaps are frequently tax, not discrimination. The difference between two storefronts often resolves entirely into embedded consumption tax and duty, which means the “cheaper” region is only cheaper until the parcel reaches a border. Before treating a regional gap as an opportunity, work out whether the low number is tax-exclusive. If it is, add your own rate and the gap usually disappears, occasionally reverses.

Sometimes the ungated public price is the best price available, full stop. The framework in this article can create a mild paranoia in which every flat number looks like a trap. It is not. A great many items, especially from single-seller brands in specialist retail, are simply priced once for everybody, and the correct response is relief rather than more searching. The ninety-second read is valuable precisely because it terminates early most of the time.

Buying Tips for This Week

Do the private-window check before you compare across sellers, not after. Comparing a logged-in price at one retailer against a logged-out price at another is the single most common way people conclude the wrong seller is cheaper, and it happens constantly because the natural browsing pattern is to be signed in where you usually shop and signed out where you do not.

Open the voucher surface once per platform per week, not once per item. The offers held there are usually account-level and category-level rather than item-level, so checking them per item is wasted effort. Checking them once tells you what the week’s ceiling is for that platform, and you can then shop against it.

If you are going to join a membership tier, join it before the cart, not at checkout. Checkout-time membership prompts are frequently priced worse than the same tier bought from the programme page, and they occasionally exclude the current cart from the benefit they are advertising.

Treat delivery-window discounts as real money. Choosing a slower window is often worth more than any coupon on the page, costs nothing if you are not against a deadline, and is invisible in every price comparison you will read. Six days before an event, the fast window is genuinely worth paying for on event-related items and genuinely worthless on everything else.

Do not repeat-visit an item you intend to buy. If you have decided, buy it. Additional visits move you into a higher-intent segment and withdraw offers. If you have not decided, do your looking in a clean session.

Finally, write down the base public number before you start pulling levers. It is the only fixed point in this entire exercise, and without it you cannot tell whether your stacked total is genuinely good or merely complicated.

Category Notes

Audio. Heavily segmented and heavily promoted, which is a difficult combination. Ungated public numbers here will move for the event; gated ones will narrow before it. Check both and act on whichever is larger, but do it in one session rather than across a week.

Small kitchen appliances. Member and loyalty pricing dominates. The public number is often a placeholder, and comparing public numbers across retailers in this category is close to meaningless. Sign in everywhere first.

Hand and garden tools. The cleanest category in retail right now. Almost no event exposure, minimal segmentation beyond trade accounts, and trade accounts are usually open to anyone willing to fill in a form. If you need tools, this week is as good as any week.

Pet equipment. Coupon-gated more than any other mainstream category, with offers that rotate weekly and rarely align with events. Buy on the coupon cycle, ignore the calendar entirely.

Portable power and charging. The most aggressive new-account pricing anywhere, because the products are cheap enough to be used as customer-acquisition bait. Excellent value if you are a new account, mediocre if you are not, and almost never worth waiting on.

PC components and cases. Ungated, publicly priced, and reliably promoted in June. This is the textbook wait category. Note the exception for anything in short supply, where the promotion may be nominal.

Content-creation lighting and accessories. App-gated and moving fast on product cycles. The segment read is easy here; the product-cycle read is the hard part, and it dominates.

Tableware and homeware. Regionally priced more than promotionally priced. Domestic catalogues are broader and cheaper than export ones almost everywhere. No event exposure worth waiting for.

Music hardware, new and used. New gear is ungated and promoted; used gear is a secondary market with no event mechanism at all. Those two halves of the same category need opposite strategies, which is unusual and worth remembering.

Footwear, made to order. Lead times of six to twelve weeks make the event irrelevant. The only question is whether the first-order code exists, and if it does, whether you want the shoes.

Watches. Direct brands with made-to-order models are effectively unsegmented and unpromoted. Marketplace and grey-market listings are heavily regional. The gap between those two worlds is larger than any June discount.

Optics and photographic accessories. Publicly priced, promoted in June, and one of the few categories where the manufacturer’s own instant-rebate programme is more predictable than the retailer’s event. Watch the rebate calendar rather than the retail one.

Shipping, Duties and Delivery Timing

Six days before the tournament, delivery certainty is worth paying for on anything event-related and worth nothing on everything else, and the pricing does not reflect that split — fast shipping costs the same whether or not you need it. The practical consequence is that you should be aggressively buying slow delivery this week on the two-thirds of your cart that has no deadline, and using the savings to buy fast delivery on the third that does.

Regional segmentation and shipping segmentation interact in a way that catches people out. Free-delivery thresholds are usually set per region, not globally, so a cart that qualifies in a metropolitan postcode may not qualify in a rural one, and the difference is often larger than the item-level price gap you were investigating. When you run the postcode check from the ninety-second read, look at the threshold line as well as the item price.

For cross-border orders, the eighteen days before the June sale are enough time for a slow-lane international parcel to arrive, and that changes the calculus on direct-from-brand purchases from overseas. A brand-site order placed today from a European or Japanese storefront lands comfortably before the event, which means you can buy the thing you actually want at a regional price rather than the domestic substitute at an event price. Do the duty arithmetic first: the threshold above which you pay changes the answer sharply, and the honest comparison is landed cost, not sticker.

One timing note specific to this week. Carriers are entering the pre-event build, so handling times posted on listings are being written optimistically rather than dishonestly. A stated one-day handling time is a plan, not a promise, and it degrades first for third-party sellers using their own fulfilment. If a delivery date matters, prefer platform-fulfilled inventory even at a small premium, and read the ships-from line rather than the sold-by line.

Payment, Returns and the Eighteen-Day Gap

The gap between today and June 23 is longer than most standard return windows are generous, and shorter than most are strict, which puts a lot of purchases in an awkward middle. If you buy today under a thirty-day window, you can still return an item after seeing the event price, which is a genuine and underused hedge: buy now at a gated price, and if the public event number turns out to be lower, return and rebuy. The friction is real but the option is free, and it is the correct answer for any item where you were genuinely torn.

Two cautions on that. First, return windows are calendar days and adjudication is business days, so a window that technically closes on June 24 in practice needs the return initiated a few days earlier. Second, gated prices are usually not reinstated on a rebuy — the voucher is consumed — so the hedge only works in one direction. You can convert a gated purchase into a public one, not the other way round.

On payment, the relevant segmentation this week is that instalment and buy-now-pay-later offers are being pushed hard as acquisition tools, and some of them carry genuine merchant-funded discounts rather than just deferred payment. Those are worth taking when the discount is real and the term is short. They are worth avoiding when the offer is simply a longer term, because a longer term across an event window means you are still paying for the June purchase when the July one arrives, and that is how carts compound.

Currency handling deserves one line. When a foreign storefront offers to charge you in your home currency, decline and let your card do the conversion. The convenience rate is worse, usually by two to four percent, which is frequently larger than the regional price advantage that made the foreign storefront attractive in the first place.

Mistakes People Make Six Days Before an Event

Comparing prices across accounts they are not in. Reading a price mentioned in a forum, a review, or a chat message and treating it as the price. It was that person’s price, generated from that person’s inputs. It may not exist for you at all, and hunting for it wastes an afternoon.

Spending acquisition offers on small items. Once-per-account offers are the scarcest resource in this whole system, and they get burned on impulse purchases constantly because the offer creates the impulse. If a fifteen percent code is about to expire and the only thing in your cart is a thirty-dollar item, let it expire.

Assuming the event will fix a regional problem. If your price is high because of where you live, June 23 discounts a high number. The event does not touch the structural part. This is the single most expensive misconception in cross-border shopping and it recurs before every tentpole.

Waiting on things with no promotional mechanism because everything else is being waited on. The calendar creates a mood, and the mood spreads to items that have no relationship to the calendar. Tools, tableware, replacement parts, and specialist retail do not get June discounts, and eighteen days of not having them is a pure cost.

Buying the event-adjacent item at the event-adjacent moment. Seating, cooling, and outdoor cooking are being bought hard right now by people who left it late, and prices in those categories are firming rather than softening. If you needed a projector for the eleventh, you needed to buy it in May. Buying it on the ninth is paying a scarcity premium to be six days early.

Over-optimising a small cart. The full ninety-second read on a nine-dollar accessory is nine dollars of your time for maybe eighty cents. Run the read on the largest item and let the small ones ride. The framework is worth using in proportion to the money at stake, which is the least glamorous and most useful thing in this article.

What’s Coming Next

Between now and the eleventh, expect acquisition offers to reach their seasonal peak and then begin narrowing — thresholds returning, percentages trimming, category exclusions appearing. If you are going to use a gate, this is the week. After the eleventh, attention consolidates around the tournament and the promotional posture shifts from acquisition to conversion, which means fewer new-customer offers and more urgency mechanics aimed at people already in a cart.

The second thing to watch is the public price on the ungated items in the tables above. Between now and June 23 those should drift very little, because there is nothing to move them, and then step once. If one of them starts drifting downward before the event, that is a signal the item is not in the sale and the seller has given up waiting — which converts it from a wait into a buy. That is a cheap thing to check and it is the highest-value observation available in the next fortnight.

Third, watch the membership programmes. Several are running extended trials right now that were shorter a month ago, and trial length is a reliable proxy for how hard a platform intends to push its member pricing during the event. A thirty-day trial started this week covers the entire June sale, which is either a good deal or a well-designed trap depending on whether you remember to cancel.

Bottom line

The number on the screen is not a fact about the product. It is a guess about you, assembled in the moment from where you are, what you are holding, how long you have existed, and what your browsing has already disclosed. Six days before the tournament and eighteen before the June sale, that guess is unusually generous to strangers and unusually stingy to loyal customers, because platforms are still in the business of buying people rather than serving them.

So run the order of operations in the right sequence. Establish which price you are being shown. If it is gated, understand that the gate is depreciating and act this week, because the offer that exists today is being built for a customer the platform will not need on June 23. If it is ungated, understand that no amount of cleverness will move it and only a scheduled event can — so ask whether one is scheduled, and if the answer is no, buy the thing.

Six buys and four waits in each table, and in both cases the split was produced by the same column rather than by taste. That is the test of a decision rule worth keeping: it should be able to tell you to do something you would not have done, and it should be wrong in ways you can name in advance. This one is wrong on price-controlled items, on test noise, on tax-driven regional gaps, and on anything where the product cycle matters more than the price. Everywhere else, ask whose price you are looking at before you ask whether it is a good one.
