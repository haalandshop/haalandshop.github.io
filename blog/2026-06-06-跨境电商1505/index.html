Best Things to Buy Right Now — June 6, 2026

Quick take: There are two dates on everybody’s mind this morning. Thursday, June 11, when the tournament opens. Monday, June 23, when the June sale opens. Those two dates are so loud, so heavily advertised, and so relentlessly referenced that they have quietly taken over the way people think about every purchase they are considering — including the overwhelming majority of purchases that have absolutely nothing to do with either date. That is the expensive mistake of early June, and it is almost invisible because it does not feel like a mistake. It feels like patience. You add something to a cart, you think “the sale is in seventeen days,” and you close the tab. For maybe a quarter of what you buy, that is genuinely the right call and it will save you real money. For the rest, you have just committed to a seventeen-day wait for an event that, as far as that specific item is concerned, is not going to happen. Nobody has scheduled a discount on it. Nobody is planning to. The price on June 23 will be the price today, except that you will have spent seventeen days without the thing, and in a few categories you will be paying more because the clock that item actually runs on moves in the opposite direction. So the question this guide is built around is not “should I buy now or wait.” It is one level upstream of that: what clock is this item on, and is the calendar in my head the same clock? There are four of them, they behave completely differently, and almost every item you will look at this weekend is on exactly one. Sort that out first and the buy-or-wait decision answers itself, usually in under two minutes. The rest of this guide is the sorting method, a worked example on a real cart, and twenty picks that show the method producing verdicts you would not have reached by instinct.

What’s Hot This Week

The demand picture is about as legible as it ever gets, and that legibility is itself worth something, because it means you can see the boundary between what the calendar touches and what it does not. Five days out from Thursday evening, everything that helps a group of people watch a screen together is moving: large-format televisions, soundbars, portable and short-throw projectors, folding chairs, coolers, ice makers, outdoor fans, canopies, and every category of food and drink that gets consumed in front of a match. Add to that the second ring of hosting demand, which is less obvious and larger than people expect — indoor grills, air fryers in the family sizes, warming trays, serving platters, disposable and reusable drinkware, and the small kitchen hardware that reduces the number of times a host has to leave the room.

Then there is the third ring, which is where it gets interesting: categories that are surging purely because they share shelf space or browse nodes with the first two rings, without any real underlying event demand. Home audio that nobody is buying for football still gets swept into football merchandising. Outdoor furniture gets merchandised next to viewing setups. Air purifiers end up in the same “get your living room ready” collections as televisions. These items are experiencing promotional attention without experiencing demand, and that combination produces some of the least reliable pricing on the internet right now.

Underneath all of it, the largest part of the catalogue is completely untouched. Skincare and personal care are on replenishment cycles that do not know what month it is. Tools are on their own trade-driven rhythm. Kitchen cutlery, pens, specialist photography gear, ergonomic seating, coffee equipment, pet supplies, and the whole enthusiast long tail are running exactly as they were in April and will be running the same way in July. This is the majority of what most people actually buy over a given year, and it is the part of the catalogue where importing the June calendar does the most damage.

The supply side is quiet and that quiet is informative. Ocean rates are in a flat stretch, air freight has not yet been pulled into peak-season contention, and every unit that will be sold in the June event has already been received, counted, and allocated. Nothing landing in the next fortnight changes what is available on June 23. Practically, that means today’s prices reflect purchasing decisions made in the first quarter rather than current replacement cost. Where you see a good number this weekend it is usually a legacy number rather than a concession — and legacy numbers get corrected by attention, not by cost.

One last thing that costs nothing to exploit and that almost nobody bothers with: advertising budgets in event-adjacent categories step up hard on Monday and run flat out through Thursday. That does not change what anything costs, but it substantially changes what you are shown. A search run this morning and the same search run Wednesday afternoon return different worlds, with different brands in the first eight results. For most buyers the choice set matters more than the negotiation, so the cheapest optimisation available this week is simply doing your browsing before Monday.

Most of What You Buy Is Not on the Calendar

Here is the claim, stated as plainly as possible. A retail calendar is a schedule of decisions that merchandising teams have already made. It is real, it is knowable, and on the items it covers it is worth a great deal of money. But it covers a minority of the catalogue. The reason this is not obvious is that the calendar’s advertising covers everything — the banner at the top of the homepage does not say “this event applies to roughly a quarter of the items below.” It says the sale is coming, and your brain generalises.

So when you are deciding whether to buy something tonight or wait, the useful first move is not to estimate a discount. It is to identify what mechanism could possibly change this price, and then ask whether that mechanism is scheduled, unscheduled, or nonexistent. There are exactly four answers in practice, and they map to four clocks.

The Promotional Clock

This is the one everybody knows and the only one most people use. An item on the promotional clock has its price changed by a human being on a date chosen in advance for commercial reasons. The change is planned, it is funded, it is usually co-funded by the brand, and the date is generally guessable within a week because these calendars repeat annually with minor drift.

The tells are consistent. The item has a visible history of participating in past events, which means a price chart with sharp, temporary step-downs clustered around the same weeks each year. It carries full merchandising furniture — strikethroughs, badges, bundle offers, coupon toggles — because a brand willing to fund a promotion is also willing to fund the presentation of that promotion. It sits in a category with high advertising spend and broad appeal, because promotional budgets go where volume is. And critically, its price right now is often suspiciously flat, because merchandising teams stop touching a price in the fortnight before they cut it, in order to make the cut look bigger.

If an item is on the promotional clock and you have seventeen days of patience, waiting is a real strategy with a real expected payoff. This is the only one of the four clocks where “wait for the sale” is coherent advice.

The Product Clock

An item on the product clock changes price when a successor exists. Not on a retail date — on an engineering and inventory date. Prices in these categories are a slow staircase downward through a generation’s life, followed by a cliff when the replacement is announced and the remaining stock has to clear.

The tells are structural rather than visual. The product name carries a generation marker, whether that is a number, a year, or a letter suffix. The category has a knowable refresh cadence — annual for phones and cameras and gaming peripherals, eighteen months to three years for audio and computing hardware, longer for appliances. And there is usually chatter, in the form of certification filings, retailer placeholder listings, or a conspicuous stock thinning in specific colourways.

The product clock is the most misread of the four because its cliff can coincide with a promotional date and look identical to a promotional cut. It is not the same thing. A promotional cut is temporary and the price comes back. A product-clock cliff is permanent and gets deeper for months. Buying an end-of-generation item at a modest event discount is one of the reliable ways to lose money while feeling clever.

The Supply Clock

An item on the supply clock is priced off landed cost, and landed cost moves with currency, freight, materials, and duty treatment. There is no calendar at all. The price changes when the underlying inputs change, and it can move in either direction, which is the entire reason this clock matters.

The tells are easy once you look for them. The brand has no meaningful domestic distribution and no local subsidiary, so the seller is functionally an importer. The price sits at an odd number that does not look like it was chosen by a marketer, because it was derived from a conversion rather than selected. It moves in small irregular increments rather than in clean promotional steps. There is no minimum advertised price policy holding it flat, because there is no domestic brand office to enforce one. And the same item is priced quite differently across countries in ways that track exchange rates rather than local purchasing power.

Supply-clock items are the ones where waiting is not neutral. On the promotional clock, waiting costs you the use of the item. On the supply clock, waiting can also cost you money, because the number can be higher in seventeen days for reasons that have nothing to do with anyone’s merchandising plan.

The Items With No Clock at All

Then there is the largest group, and the one that gets the least attention: things whose price simply does not change. Made-to-order goods, where there is no inventory to clear and therefore no clearance. Consumables bought on replenishment cycles, where demand is flat and the manufacturer has no reason to discount. Specialist retail with a single distributor and tight margins. Categories under strict minimum advertised pricing where every retailer shows an identical number, forever. Small-batch and workshop production where the constraint is capacity, not demand.

The tells: the item is the same price at every retailer that carries it, to the cent. There is no promotional history to find. The product page mentions lead times rather than stock counts. There is a subscription or auto-replenishment option, which is a manufacturer telling you it expects steady demand and has no plans to manipulate it. Reviews span years, because the product has not been revised.

For a no-clock item, “wait for the sale” is not a cautious decision. It is a decision to not have the thing, in exchange for nothing. The correct action is to buy it when you need it and stop thinking about it, which is genuinely liberating once you can identify the category with confidence.

The Four Ways the Calendar Misleads You

Knowing the four clocks is not the same as using them. The failure modes below are what happens when the calendar in your head overrides the clock the item is actually on, and each one has a distinct signature.

Waiting for a Discount That Was Never Scheduled

This is the common one and by a wide margin the most expensive in aggregate, precisely because it feels responsible. You want a chef’s knife, or a bike pump, or a pair of specialist tools. You know the June sale is coming. You wait. On June 23 the item is the same price, because German cutlery and Italian cycling tools and Japanese precision hand tools do not participate in general consumer events, have never participated, and have structural reasons not to — tight distribution, single importers, MAP enforcement, and brand positioning that treats discounting as damage.

The cost is not zero and it is not just inconvenience. You have spent seventeen days without a thing you had already decided to buy, you will very likely buy it anyway at the end of the wait, and you have also spent seventeen days of low-grade cognitive overhead maintaining an open decision. The fix is one search: look for whether this exact item has ever been discounted. If the answer is no, the calendar does not apply, and the decision is simply whether you want it.

Buying at the End of a Product Cycle Because the Retail Date Looked Right

The mirror image, and much rarer but much sharper per instance. You see a solid discount on something in an active-refresh category during an event week. The discount is real. What you have not checked is whether the discount exists because the retailer is participating in an event, or because the retailer is clearing a generation that is about to be superseded.

Those look identical on the page and they are opposite in meaning. In the first case you have caught a temporary low and the price will revert. In the second you have caught the first, shallowest step of a decline that will run for months, on hardware that is about to become the old one. The check is thirty seconds of work: how old is this generation, what is the category’s refresh cadence, and is the item’s own retailer still stocking every configuration or only some of them. Thinning colourways and missing capacities are the loudest signal available.

Forgetting That One of the Clocks Runs Upward

Three of the four clocks either lower prices or hold them flat. The supply clock is the exception, and it is the one people never model, because the entire cultural framing of shopping advice is about discounts. When your assumption is “prices go down or stay the same, so waiting is free,” you have implicitly declared that no item you buy is import-priced. That is not true for anyone in 2026, and it is spectacularly untrue for enthusiast categories, where a large share of the interesting products come from a single manufacturer in a single country with a single importer.

For those items the honest framing is that you are holding a position in a currency you did not choose. The price can improve, it can worsen, and it does not care about June 23. If you want the thing and the current landed number is reasonable against its own recent history, taking it off the table is a legitimate decision rather than an impatient one.

Borrowing a Clock From the Item Next to It

The subtlest of the four and the one that survives the most scrutiny, because it looks like good contextual reasoning. You correctly identify that televisions are on the promotional clock. You are also shopping for a soundbar, which appeared in the same collection, on the same page, under the same banner, with the same badge treatment. So you assume it is on the same clock.

It might be. It also might be a third-party-sold import running on the supply clock, or a two-year-old model in the last quarter of its product cycle, or a MAP-locked audio brand that will show the same number in December. Category adjacency is not clock adjacency, and merchandising deliberately blurs the line because grouping high-intent items with lower-intent ones is how collections do their job. The discipline is boring: read the clock per item, not per page. Two items in the same collection can require opposite decisions, and frequently do.

Now change one variable, and only one: the same six items, the same $340, but the buyer is ordering cross-border into the EU rather than domestically. Almost every instruction inverts.

The projector loses its wait. The June event is a domestic-calendar event; the local storefront runs a different promotional calendar with different dates and typically shallower depth, so the mechanism the wait depended on does not exist at this address. Buy it if you want it. The grinder stays a buy but should be ordered as its own shipment rather than bundled, because consolidating it with other goods changes which duty and VAT treatment applies and can cost more than the item saves. The filters flip from buy to buy-locally — a $31 consumable does not survive cross-border shipping economics, and the correct action is to source an equivalent domestically rather than to optimise the timing. The camp chair should not be bought cross-border at all; bulky low-value goods are where dimensional weight quietly doubles the effective price. The hub is still deleted, for the same reason as before.

And the earbuds are the only item of the six whose instruction survives the change of address unchanged, because the product clock is the one clock that is genuinely global. A successor ships worldwide on the same date. That is the point of the exercise: five of six decisions were not really about the product at all, they were about which clock applied at that address, and the sixth was about the product.

Reading an Item’s Clock in Ninety Seconds

Four checks, in this order, because each one can terminate the process early. Most items resolve on the first or second.

Check one: has this exact item ever been discounted? Search the product name alongside a past event month, look at whatever price history is available to you, and look for step-downs clustered in the same weeks of prior years. A regular annual pattern is a promotional clock and you can stop here. A completely flat multi-year line is a no-clock item and you can also stop here. Ambiguity — one cut eighteen months ago, nothing since — means keep going.

Check two: is there a successor, and how close is it? Read the model designation. If it carries a generation marker, work out the cadence from the previous generations’ release dates, then check where you are in that cycle. Then look at the retailer’s own configuration list: a full grid of colours and capacities means mid-cycle, a thinning grid means end-of-cycle. If you are in the last quarter of a cycle, the product clock dominates whatever else is true and the discount you are being offered is probably the beginning of a decline rather than a temporary low.

Check three: who is actually setting this number? Look at the seller name and the ship-from location. Look at whether the brand has a domestic website with a local currency and local support. Look at whether the price is a marketer’s number or a converter’s number — $199 was chosen by a person, $186.40 was calculated by a spreadsheet. If the answer points at an importer, you are on the supply clock, waiting is not free, and the direction is not guaranteed.

Check four: is the same number showing everywhere? Open three other retailers. If all four show an identical price to the cent, you are looking at an enforced floor and there is no clock at all — no retailer can cut it, so no retailer will, and June 23 changes nothing. If the numbers differ by a few percent, there is real competitive latitude and at least some possibility of movement.

Ninety seconds, four checks, and in the vast majority of cases you now know whether patience has an expected value or is simply delay dressed up as discipline.

What Would Have to Happen for This Price to Change?

If you take one sentence from this guide, take that question. It is more useful than any specific verdict above, because it survives the calendar changing, the categories changing, and you moving to a different country.

Most shopping deliberation is a comparison between the price and some internal sense of what the thing is worth. That is a reasonable thing to do, but it is a static exercise and it produces static answers. The question above is dynamic. It forces you to name a mechanism. And once you have named the mechanism, you can evaluate whether it is scheduled, whether it is likely, and roughly when.

Run it on a few things and you will notice the answers cluster into exactly the four buckets. “A merchandising team would have to decide to cut it” — promotional, scheduled, wait. “A successor would have to ship” — product, semi-predictable, check cycle position. “The currency or the freight rate would have to move” — supply, unscheduled and bidirectional, do not treat waiting as free. And then the answer people find most uncomfortable: “nothing, really. Nothing would have to happen because nothing is going to.” That last one is not a failure to find information. It is a finding, and it is the most decision-relevant one available, because it converts an open question into a closed one.

There is a second-order benefit worth mentioning. Naming the mechanism also tells you what to watch. If the mechanism is a scheduled promotion, the thing to watch is the retailer’s own announcement page, and there is no point checking the price daily. If the mechanism is a successor, the thing to watch is configuration availability, and the price is nearly irrelevant until it moves. If the mechanism is the supply clock, the thing to watch is a range rather than a trend, and the correct behaviour is to buy near the bottom of an observed band rather than to wait for a direction that does not exist. Most people watch prices. Watching the mechanism is far less work and considerably more informative.

Where This Read Breaks Down

Five honest counter-cases, because a framework that never fails is a framework that is not saying anything.

A no-clock item acquires a clock. Brands change. A manufacturer that never discounted signs a marketplace distribution deal, or gets acquired, or decides to buy share in a new market, and suddenly a category with a decade of flat pricing runs a promotion. This is rare but it does happen, and the read that was correct for nine years is wrong in the tenth. Mitigation: treat “no clock” as a strong prior rather than a certainty, and be suspicious if you notice the brand has recently appeared in channels it never used before.

Clearance wearing a promotion’s clothes. Discussed above as a failure mode, but it deserves repeating as a limitation of the method, because the four checks do not reliably distinguish them when the retailer is deliberately blurring the difference. A product-clock cliff presented inside a promotional collection during an event week will pass check one, since it produces exactly the price-history shape you would expect from event participation. The only real defence is check two, and check two depends on knowing the category’s refresh cadence, which you may not.

Two clocks running simultaneously. Televisions are the standard example. They are on the promotional clock, and also on an annual model-year clock that resets in spring. In June you are frequently offered a promotional discount on a panel that is already a year into its model cycle, which means the discount is real, the model is old, and the successor is already on shelves at a higher price. Neither read is wrong on its own; the interaction is what makes it hard. Where two clocks are live, the product clock generally dominates for anything you intend to keep more than three years.

The supply clock can run in your favour. The framing above emphasises that supply-clock items can get more expensive, and that is the underweighted risk. But the clock is genuinely bidirectional. A currency swing or a freight-rate collapse can produce a better price on an import in seventeen days than any promotional calendar would have delivered. The honest statement is that this direction is unknowable, not that it is unfavourable. If an import-priced item is not urgent and you have no view on the currency, there is no strong argument either way, and you should decide on whether you want it rather than on timing.

New brands have no readable history. Every check in this guide depends on a track record. A brand that launched fourteen months ago has no promotional history, no generational cadence, and no established distribution pattern. Reading “no clock” off an absence of data is a mistake — absence of evidence is not evidence of a fixed price. For genuinely new brands the honest position is that the clock is unknown, and the practical response is to weight the decision on the product rather than on the timing, since you have no timing information to weight.

Buying Tips for This Week

Do your browsing before Monday. Advertising budgets in event-adjacent categories step up at the start of the week and run through Thursday, which reshapes what appears in the first page of results. This does not change prices but it changes your candidate set, and the candidate set determines the outcome more often than the negotiation does.

Sort your open decisions by clock before you sort them by price. Ten minutes spent labelling everything in your saved lists will usually reveal that half of them were never waiting for anything. Close those decisions today, in either direction. An open decision has a running cost even when it does not have a financial one.

For anything you decide to wait on, write down the mechanism next to it. Not “sale,” but the specific thing: “expect event participation, historically the fourth week of June” or “successor expected, currently nineteen months into a twenty-four-month cadence.” A wait with a named mechanism can be evaluated later. A wait without one just becomes a permanently open tab.

If an item is event-adjacent and you need it before Thursday, buy it now and stop optimising. Five days is a comfortable domestic delivery window and an uncomfortable one for anything crossing a border. Availability in specific sizes, colours, and configurations tightens well before the event, and being right about a price you could not act on is not worth much.

For supply-clock items, decide against a range rather than a trend. Look at where the number has sat over the last quarter, and treat the bottom third of that band as a reasonable entry. Waiting for a downward trend in a category that does not have trends is how people spend six months not buying something they wanted in week one.

Check the shipping threshold trap before you check out. If your cart contains anything you would not buy on its own, remove it and pay the shipping. The arithmetic almost always favours the removal, and the psychological effect of the threshold is one of the most reliably exploited things in retail.

Finally, on anything you buy this week, note the retailer’s price adjustment window on the order. If a retailer offers adjustments and your item does turn out to be event-included on June 23, that window converts a bad timing decision into a neutral one at the cost of a two-minute request. Not every retailer offers it and the windows differ, which is exactly why it is worth knowing before rather than after.

Category Notes

Televisions and projectors. Two live clocks and an awkward gap between them: peak demand Thursday, peak discount June 23. Unless you need a screen for opening night this is the clearest wait in the catalogue, but check the model year before you celebrate any discount you are shown.

Soundbars and home audio. Superficially the same shape as televisions, structurally much more varied. Third-party importers operate heavily here, so the clock differs enormously between two items sitting side by side in the same collection. Read the seller before you read the price.

Small kitchen appliances. Genuine hosting demand, already visible, and the popular sizes and colours will thin before Thursday. This is a category where availability risk currently exceeds price risk, which argues for buying the specific model you want rather than waiting for a better number on a model you have settled for.

Kitchen cutlery. Almost entirely no-clock. Enforced pricing, tight distribution, and multi-decade product continuity. There is nothing to wait for, and the useful research is about steel and geometry rather than about timing.

Coffee equipment. Sharply split between import-priced hardware on the supply clock and domestic brands on the promotional clock, with very little in between. The grinders are usually imports and the machines are usually not, which is why the two halves of a coffee setup often deserve opposite decisions.

Skincare and personal care. Replenishment-driven and mostly outside the event calendar, except in creator-led channels where campaign cycles create a genuine promotional clock. Buy on your cycle, check batch and expiry on marketplace listings, and treat social-channel pricing as its own separate system.

Storage and components. The most reliably event-included category in the entire catalogue, with the flat pre-event price shape to match. The exception is cooling, cases, and passive hardware, which are frequently MAP-locked and never move at all.

Recording and music gear. Specialist retail with knowable product cycles and thin promotional participation. Cycle position is the whole analysis. Mid-cycle is safe, late-cycle is a trap dressed as an opportunity.

Writing instruments. Almost pure no-clock in the current catalogue, and a genuine secondary market on discontinued references where scarcity sets the price. Two completely different pricing systems under one category heading.

Photography and support gear. Single distributors, material-driven costs, and near-zero participation in general consumer events. Prior-generation bodies and lenses are the exception and do see real clearance, which is the product clock rather than the promotional one.

Outdoor and camping. Directly in Thursday’s demand path for anything supporting group viewing. Expect availability to tighten rather than prices to improve between now and the weekend. The specialist end of the category, particularly small-batch and imported hardware, is on entirely different clocks from the mass-market end.

Home organisation and storage. Reliably event-included at the design-led end, reliably flat at the long-lifecycle end. The tell is whether the manufacturer sells spare parts, which is a fairly good proxy for whether it thinks of the product as a durable or as a seasonal.

Shipping, Duties and Delivery Timing

Five days is a comfortable domestic window and a tight cross-border one, and that asymmetry should be driving routing decisions this weekend more than price is.

Domestically, anything ordered today with a standard service arrives well before Thursday, and anything ordered Monday still arrives with a day of slack. That slack matters more than usual, because the last pick waves before a major event are the ones where substitutions and partial shipments happen. If a specific configuration matters to you, order early enough that a problem can be fixed rather than merely reported.

Cross-border is a different calculation entirely. Standard international services are not reliable against a Thursday deadline from a Saturday order, and expedited services carry a premium that frequently exceeds the price advantage that motivated the cross-border purchase in the first place. If the item is event-relevant, source it domestically or accept that it arrives after the event. If it is not event-relevant, the deadline is irrelevant and you should optimise on landed cost instead.

On landed cost, the arithmetic that catches people out is dimensional weight. Low-value bulky goods — seating, coolers, anything with air inside it — are priced on volume rather than mass, and the shipping frequently exceeds the item. This is the single most common cross-border mistake in the categories that are hot right now, because event-driven demand skews toward exactly these bulky items.

The other recurring error is consolidation. Bundling several items into one shipment feels efficient and often is, but it can push a shipment across a value threshold that changes how duty and tax are assessed, converting a simple delivery into a formal entry with handling fees attached. Where you are near a threshold, splitting the order into two shipments is frequently cheaper despite paying shipping twice. Work out where the thresholds sit for your destination before you consolidate, not after.

Finally, note who the importer of record is. On marketplace purchases from overseas sellers this is often you, whether or not the listing made that clear, and it determines who handles any customs correspondence and who pays if the declared value is questioned. Sellers that offer delivered-duty-paid terms are quoting you a real number; sellers that do not are quoting you a partial one.

Payment, Returns and the Seventeen-Day Gap

Seventeen days is an awkward interval and it is worth understanding why before you rely on it.

Most standard return windows run thirty days from delivery. An item bought today and delivered midweek has a return window that comfortably spans June 23. That is a genuinely useful property, and it is the mechanism that makes a “buy now, reassess later” strategy viable on promotional-clock items you are not sure about. If it turns out to be included in the event at a materially better price, you have options — a price adjustment if the retailer offers one, or a return and rebuy if it does not.

The catch is that the return and rebuy path is only clean if you have not opened, used, or registered the item, and if the retailer does not charge return shipping or a restocking fee. On bulky goods and on anything requiring activation, the friction is high enough that the option is worth much less than it looks. Price adjustment windows are the far better instrument where they exist, because they cost you a message rather than a shipment, but they are shorter than return windows, typically seven to fourteen days, and they usually exclude event pricing explicitly. Read that exclusion before you rely on the strategy, because “we match our own promotional prices” and “adjustments do not apply to limited-time event pricing” are both common and they mean opposite things.

On payment, the seventeen-day gap interacts with card benefits in a way worth noting. Some purchase-protection and extended-warranty benefits run from the transaction date, so a June 6 purchase and a June 23 purchase differ by seventeen days at the far end of a multi-year window. This is a small effect but it is a real one and it points toward buying earlier rather than later on high-value items, all else equal.

For cross-border purchases specifically, pay attention to the currency the transaction settles in. Dynamic currency conversion at checkout — where the seller offers to charge you in your home currency — is almost always worse than letting your card issuer handle the conversion, sometimes by several percent. On a supply-clock item where you have already accepted currency exposure, handing an additional spread to the merchant’s conversion partner is a needless loss on top of it.

And on refunds, remember that a cross-border refund is not symmetrical with the purchase. Duty and tax paid on import are recoverable in some jurisdictions and not others, the process is rarely automatic, and the exchange rate applied to a refund is the rate on the refund date rather than the purchase date. A cross-border return can therefore lose money even when the merchant refunds in full, which is a good reason to be more decisive about fit and specification on international orders than on domestic ones.

Mistakes People Make Five Days Before an Event

Treating the calendar as universal. The core error this guide is built around. The banner applies to the store; it does not apply to every item in the store. Assuming otherwise converts a targeted strategy into a blanket delay.

Confusing attention with opportunity. Event weeks generate enormous merchandising activity, and it is easy to read that activity as evidence that prices are moving. In event-adjacent categories that are not actually event-included, what increases is the volume of badges, not the depth of discounts. A busy page is not a cheap page.

Buying the substitute at the original’s price. When a popular configuration sells out before an event, the adjacent options rarely get cheaper, and sometimes get more expensive as demand redistributes. If the thing you wanted is gone, the correct comparison is against the substitute’s own history, not against the price you were prepared to pay for something else.

Ordering bulky event goods cross-border. Dimensional weight, deadline risk, and return economics all point the same direction on chairs, coolers, and canopies. This is a category where domestic sourcing is almost always correct even at a nominally higher sticker price.

Letting the free-shipping threshold assemble the cart. A meaningful share of what gets bought in event weeks is threshold filler, and it is the purest waste in retail because it is spending that produces no wanted item. If you would not buy it standalone, removing it and paying shipping is nearly always cheaper.

Waiting on a consumable. Filters, refills, cartridges, skincare, pet supplies, and anything else you buy repeatedly are the least likely categories to be event-included and the most annoying to be without. Running out of something in order to save nothing is a common and entirely avoidable outcome of applying event logic indiscriminately.

Not writing anything down. If you defer six decisions this weekend without recording why, you will not remember on June 23 which ones had a mechanism and which ones were vague. You will then either buy all six or none, both of which discard the analysis entirely.

What’s Coming Next

Monday brings the advertising step-up and, with it, the first real reshuffling of what appears in event-adjacent search results. Expect the composition of the first page to change more than the prices do. If you have research to finish, finishing it today or tomorrow gives you a cleaner view.

Between Monday and Thursday, availability rather than price is the variable to watch in anything event-driven. The specific configurations that matter — screen sizes, chair capacities, cooler volumes — thin out first while headline items remain in stock, which makes the category look healthier than it is. Wednesday is generally the last day on which a domestic order arrives with genuine slack.

Thursday itself is an odd retail day. Demand for event goods collapses the moment the event begins, and a small number of items see opportunistic post-deadline markdowns on Friday and over the following weekend. These are real but shallow and they only cover the leftovers, so this is a path for the flexible rather than the specific.

The stretch from Friday June 12 to Sunday June 21 is the quietest window of the month and the best time to buy anything on the no-clock or supply clocks, simply because the noise level drops and you can evaluate things on their merits. It is also when promotional-clock items go conspicuously still ahead of the event, which is a confirmation signal rather than a disappointment.

Then June 23 arrives and the promotional clock finally does its thing. What matters is that you go into it with a short, specific list of items you have already established have a mechanism, rather than a long list of everything you deferred. The people who do well in June events are not the ones who wait for everything. They are the ones who waited for the right four things and bought the other twelve three weeks earlier.

Bottom line

Five days before the tournament and seventeen days before the sale, the retail calendar is the loudest object in your field of view, and its main effect on most shoppers is to make them apply a scheduling logic to items that were never scheduled. The fix is one question asked before any other: what would have to happen for this price to change?

Four answers, four clocks. A merchandising team has to decide — promotional, scheduled, waiting works. A successor has to ship — product, semi-predictable, check where you are in the cycle. A currency or a freight rate has to move — supply, unscheduled and bidirectional, waiting is not free. Or nothing has to happen because nothing is going to — no clock, and the only real question is whether you want the thing.

The twenty picks above resolve almost mechanically once the clock is identified. Every promotional-clock item is a wait, with a nameable mechanism attached. Every no-clock and supply-clock item is a buy, not out of urgency but because the alternative is deferring a decision that has no resolution date. The product-clock items split on cycle position, which is the one place where genuine judgment is required.

And the cart exercise is the part worth repeating on your own saved lists tonight. Six items, one instruction each, three different answers — and when the delivery address changed, five of the six instructions changed with it while only one held. That ratio is the real lesson of cross-border buying, and it is why a good buying decision is rarely about the product in isolation. It is about the product, the clock it runs on, and the address it is going to.
