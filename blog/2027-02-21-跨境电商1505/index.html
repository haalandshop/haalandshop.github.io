Best Things to Buy Right Now — February 21, 2027

Quick take: Yesterday’s piece was about the denominator nobody checks, and the arithmetic still holds — a price is a fraction, the page shows you only the top of it, and the bottom, how often you will actually use the thing, is invisible until you estimate it. What makes this particular week unusual is that the same percentage-off can mean two opposite things sitting in the same aisle, one shelf apart. A discount is either a supply discount or a demand discount, and late February 2027 is the one week of the year when both are on the floor at once. The post-Lunar-New-Year reload has now landed in depth: containers that left Shenzhen and Yiwu two weeks ago have cleared customs and hit FBA and overseas warehouses, so the bestsellers that were thin or sold out through January are back, often at the pre-holiday price, because more supply arrived and the marginal cost fell. That is a supply discount, and on a good object it is a genuine deal. A metre away, the Valentine’s occasion goods and the winter-clearance aisle are also marked down hard — but there the price fell because demand ended on a date, not because anything got cheaper to make. That is a demand discount, and on a dated, single-use object it is a real discount on a bad denominator. This is the week to buy the thing whose price dropped because supply arrived, and to walk past the thing whose price dropped because nobody wants it after the fourteenth. The clearance percentages look identical. The reasons could not be more different, and the reason is the only part that predicts whether you will be glad you bought it in June.

What’s Hot This Week

Four things are moving at once, and the thread connecting them is that each one is a discount with a cause, and the cause is the only part that matters.

The post-Lunar-New-Year restock is now in depth, and that is a supply discount

The Lunar New Year shutdown is the largest recurring pause in global cross-border supply, and its effects arrive on a delay. Manufacturing restarts around the sixth, but reloading a marketplace takes the better part of a fortnight: containers leave port, clear customs, and hit FBA and overseas warehouses roughly two weeks later. That lag means the week of February 21 is when the reload is actually in stock, across Amazon, Walmart, Rakuten and the rest. Two consequences follow, and they point in opposite directions from the clearance aisle. First, the bestsellers that were thin or sold out through late January and early February are back in depth, and the restock usually arrives at the pre-holiday price rather than the scarcity price — so the discount you see is a return to normal, not a fire sale on a dud. Second, and more interestingly, this is when the new-SKU wave appears, the products Chinese factories tooled up for during the shutdown and are now shipping for the first time. A restocked staple is a “buy the thing you already wanted” opportunity; a brand-new SKU is a “wait for the review base” situation, because a product three weeks off a container has told you nothing about itself yet.

Valentine’s and winter clearances are demand discounts, and they mean the opposite

The dated merchandise — themed apparel, printed mugs, rose-red everything, winter fashion that only made sense in cold weather, single-purpose holiday decor — has no residual demand after its date passes, and it is being discounted to recover shelf space. The discount is real and the product is genuinely not worth the clearance price to anyone who is not performing the gesture today. The instructive contrast sits right beside it: the gift-configured tools — a good pen, a real knife, a quality flask — clear for the same occasion reason but carry denominators in the hundreds, and the discount is free money for a buyer who ignores the occasion framing. Reading “winter sale” or “Valentine clearance” as a uniform discount is the most common error in the aisle this week, because it pushes people toward the disposable and away from the durable, and the two are priced close enough to fool a glance.

The resolution-resale flood has peaked and is self-reporting the denominator

New Year fitness and productivity hardware — studio bikes, treadmills, adjustable dumbbells, standing desks, language gadgets — was bought in a January surge of intent. By late February, a predictable fraction of it is listed, and the listings are written by people who have already admitted the thing was a mistake. That admission is reflected in the price: durable goods, often barely used, listed at a third to a half of retail by sellers with zero marketing budget and maximum motivation to be rid of them. For a buyer, this is the annual opening of a secondary market in excellent-denominator objects, sold by owners who have personally verified the denominator is one — the only place in retail where the denominator is disclosed by the seller rather than hidden. The only risk is condition, and for bulky items the logistics of resale often mean the seller is local, which removes the cross-border return problem entirely.

New-SKU arrivals are green and should be treated as evidence-free

This is the quiet trap inside the reload. Factories use the shutdown to tool up for the year’s new products, and those ship in the late-February wave. The result is a marketplace full of listings that look finished but have dozens rather than thousands of reviews. Automated merchandising treats a new listing with strong early velocity as a breakout, which overweights inventory behind products that have not been proven through a return cycle. For the next month, the single most useful filter on any platform is review count dated before this month, not the star average, because a five-star listing with forty reviews is a hypothesis, and a four-star listing with four thousand is a result.

The Two Prices in the Same Aisle

The whole method for this week reduces to one distinction. A discount is either caused by supply or by demand, and late February is the only week where you can stand in one aisle and watch both causes operate on adjacent shelves.

A supply discount happens because the thing got cheaper to provide. More units arrived, a tariff or freight rate moved, a factory came back online, a restock landed. The price falls, but the object is unchanged — it is still the pan, the filter, the garden tool you wanted in January, now simply available at the normal price again. On a good object, a supply discount is a free improvement in your cost per use, because nothing about the denominator changed. You were going to use it fifty times either way; you just paid less to start.

A demand discount happens because the thing got cheaper to want. The occasion ended, the season turned, the trend cooled, the date passed. The object is unchanged too, but the reason you were going to use it evaporated on a calendar. The clearance price is honest about the object’s prospects even when the listing copy is not: a product marked down 60% the day after Valentine’s is the market telling you, plainly, that its use count from here is one.

The trap is that both discounts print the same number on the tag. Sixty percent off looks like sixty percent off. The cause is invisible on the shelf and only reconstructable by asking one question: did the price fall because more of this arrived, or because fewer people want it now? Everything else in this guide is a footnote to that question. The post-CNY reload is a supply discount. The winter and Valentine clearances are demand discounts. The resolution-resale flood is a supply discount wearing a demand-discount costume — the price is low because the owner wants out, but the object itself has a great denominator, which is why it is the best buy of the three.

Five Ways to Tell a Supply Discount from a Demand Discount

This does not require a price-history extension or a spreadsheet. It requires reading the cause off the listing, in order.

First: look for the restock signal. A “back in stock,” “restocked,” or simply a listing that was unavailable in January and is now available in depth is a supply discount by definition — the cause is inventory, not desire. Demand discounts never say they are restocked; they say “clearance,” “last one,” “season final,” or “was $X.” The wording is the tell.

Second: ask whether the discount is date-driven or inventory-driven. If the markdown is tied to a calendar event — a holiday just past, a season just ended — it is a demand discount, because the event, not the cost, ended the demand. If it is tied to a replenishment or a freight improvement, it is a supply discount. The cleanest test: would this item be at this price in March for any reason other than the date? If the answer is no, you are looking at a demand discount on a dated denominator.

Third: check the review base for age, not just stars. A supply-discount restock returns a proven product with its existing review history intact — thousands of reviews, steady dates, normal distribution. A demand-discount clearance of a fad often shows a thin or abruptly-old review base, because the product’s demand already collapsed once before. New-SKU arrivals are the special case: green, recent, few reviews, and therefore evidence-free until March.

Fourth: separate the replenishable from the occasion item. Replenishables and durables — filters, detergents, garden tools, cookware, hand tools — earn supply discounts that reward you every time you use them. Occasion items — themed apparel, dated decor, race-day kit, espadrilles before summer — earn demand discounts that are the only reason they are cheap, and the cheapness is the warning.

Fifth: scan the sibling category. Is the entire aisle clearing, or just this SKU returning? A whole-aisle clearance is a demand discount (the season ended for everyone). A single SKU coming back while its neighbours stay put is a supply discount (this one restocked). The aisle context is the fastest way to read the cause without opening the listing.

Cross-Border News Worth Reading This Week

The post-Lunar-New-Year reload is the largest single supply event in cross-border retail, and it arrives on a delay. Most shoppers experience the shutdown as a stock-out in late January and assume it resolves in early February. It does not. The two-week manufacturing pause plus transit means the actual restock lands in the week of the twenty-first, which is why the “out of stock” badges clear in a cluster rather than a trickle. For a buyer the implication is concrete: the products you wanted and could not get in January are back now, frequently at the pre-holiday price, and the new-tooling SKUs are arriving green, with no review base and no return history to read.

New-SKU launches are peaking exactly when review bases are thinnest. Factories use the shutdown to tool up for the year’s new products, and those ship in the late-February wave. The result is a marketplace full of listings that look finished but have dozens rather than thousands of reviews. Automated merchandising treats a new listing with strong early velocity as a breakout, which overweights inventory behind products that have not been proven through a return cycle. For the next month, the single most useful filter on any platform is review count dated before this month, not star average.

The resolution-resale flood is a free audit of the first-quarter denominator. A large population bought fitness and productivity hardware in January and is listing it in February. The prices are set by owners who have personally established the object’s usage rate, which makes the resale channel the only place in retail where the denominator is disclosed by the seller rather than hidden. The categories to watch are studio bikes, treadmills, adjustable dumbbells, standing desks and language gadgets — and the condition risk is real but locally mitigable, because bulky resale tends to be regional and pickup-based.

Winter clearance is deep on the wrong things. The season-end discount is steepest on dated winter fashion and single-purpose holiday decor, which have denominators of one and were never good buys. The durable winter hardware — traction, thermal layers, heated gear with real service lives — clears only modestly, because its buyers are year-round and the sellers know it. Reading “winter sale” as a uniform discount is the most common error in the aisle this week, and it pushes people toward the disposable and away from the durable.

Valentine’s occasion goods are now pure clearance, and the lesson is free. Themed and dated merchandise has no residual demand after the fourteenth, and it is being discounted to recover shelf space. The gift-configured tools sitting next to it — a real pen, a quality knife, a proper flask — clear for the same occasion reason but carry denominators in the hundreds. The contrast is the cleanest available illustration of the whole method, and it costs nothing to observe.

Amazon, Shopify and TikTok: Where the Breakouts Are Forming

The three channels are producing genuinely different kinds of breakout right now, and the supply-versus-demand lens separates them cleanly, because each platform’s discovery mechanism has a characteristic bias about why a product’s price is moving.

Amazon is where supply discounts and high denominators win, and the ranking system knows it. Search-led discovery favours the purchase people already intended to make, and replenishable goods get the additional advantage of repeat-purchase behaviour feeding back into rank. The breakouts forming here this week are consumables and their adjacent hardware — filtration, protective disposables, coffee and robot-vacuum maintenance, anything with a subscription mechanism attached — and two forces are amplifying this in February specifically: the post-CNY restock has returned the bestsellers to depth after a thin January, and the resolution surge has pulled a different cohort of buyers into first-time purchases of replenishables they will now keep buying. The near-term breakout candidates are unglamorous, have excellent fractions, and arrive as supply discounts rather than demand ones — which is the correct combination and the reason they rarely appear on trend lists.

Shopify and the direct-brand layer are where the occasion economy concentrates, and that is a warning as much as an observation. A single-catalogue brand has no search-intent traffic to harvest, so it manufactures demand through narrative, and the most reliable narrative device is an occasion — a drop, a season, a race, a date. That produces genuinely strong launches and genuinely poor denominators, and it shows up this week as a wall of late-winter and pre-spring “new season” messaging. The exception, and it is where the durable direct-brand breakouts are forming, is the subset of brands whose entire pitch is repetition rather than novelty: single-silhouette catalogues, staple goods, products with published maintenance schedules. Those brands convert slowly and retain extremely well, and they are the ones worth watching over the next quarter as the occasion-led cohort runs into a post-event demand vacuum.

TikTok Shop is structurally biased toward the worst fractions in retail, and that is a description of its physics rather than a criticism. Discovery is driven by demonstration, and the products that demonstrate best are the ones with a visible before-and-after in under twenty seconds. Visible transformation correlates strongly with novelty and weakly with daily utility, which means the breakout mechanism selects for objects that are compelling once. The interesting development this week is a second cohort emerging alongside it: creators building around replenishment and routine rather than reveal — filter cadences, microfibre systems, robot-vacuum bags — which demonstrate badly but retain far better. Where a product breaks out on both mechanisms at once — demonstrable and consumable — the growth is unusually durable, and that overlap is the single most useful thing to look for on the platform.

The synthesis across all three is a timing rule rather than a platform preference. Amazon breakouts are safest to buy into immediately, because the ranking that produced them is built from repeat behaviour and the restock is a supply discount. Direct-brand breakouts are safest to buy after the launch window, when the occasion pressure has drained and the product either still exists in the catalogue or does not. TikTok breakouts are safest to buy roughly a month late, because the interval between a product going viral and the same product appearing in volume on resale channels is short, and that interval is the market publicly reporting the denominator. Right now, the resolution-resale flood is that report arriving on schedule.

The Sixty-Second Discount Check

This does not require research tools, price history or a spreadsheet. It requires one question asked before the item goes in the basket: did the price fall because supply arrived, or because demand left?

Name the cause out loud. Restocked, replenishable, freight-improved, factory-back-online — those are supply discounts, and on a good object they are free money. Holiday-passed, season-ended, trend-cooled, date-expired — those are demand discounts, and on a dated object they are a real discount on a denominator of one. Saying the cause forces the brain to attach the markdown to a reason, which is the step that reliably changes the decision.

Divide, and say the result out loud. Cost per use is only powerful when it is spoken as a unit price. Thirty-two cents a use sounds like nothing; eleven dollars a use sounds like what it is. The same purchase, the same number, completely different intuitions depending on whether you leave it as a total or convert it. Do the conversion. It takes four seconds and it is the only step that reliably changes decisions.

Ask whether the occasion or the object is doing the work. If the deadline evaporated tomorrow, would you still want this? For a filter, a pan, a pair of garden shears, the answer is yes and the deadline merely supplied the prompt. For anything dated, themed or named after the event, the answer is no, and the purchase is best understood as buying a memento rather than equipment. A memento is fine; it should just be priced and budgeted as a memento rather than smuggled through as gear.

Check whether renting, borrowing or going without is on the table. The denominator argument has a floor: if the honest count is one or two, the correct comparison is not against a cheaper version of the same object but against not owning one at all. This question is skipped almost universally because ownership is the default frame shopping puts you in, and it is the question that saves the most money per second spent asking it.

When a Single Use Is Worth the Whole Price

The method above, applied without judgement, collapses into an argument that nobody should ever buy anything used fewer than twenty times. That would be wrong, and the exceptions are specific enough to be listed rather than hand-waved.

When the single use is irreplaceable. A total solar eclipse lasts a couple of minutes and does not recur in the same place for a very long time. An object used once, at an event that cannot be repeated, is not evaluated by cost per use at all — it is evaluated against the cost of missing the event, which for a genuinely unrepeatable occasion can be arbitrarily large. This exception is narrow and it is heavily abused by marketing, but it is real, and a wedding, a graduation or a once-in-a-lifetime trip is a legitimate instance of it.

When the failure mode is safety rather than disappointment. Cost-per-use reasoning is about value, and value reasoning has no business anywhere near eye protection, load-bearing equipment, electrical isolation or anything holding a child. For those categories, the correct denominator is one, deliberately, because the analysis you should be running is about certification and provenance rather than efficiency. The most expensive mistake available this week is applying the frugal frame to a helmet or a harness.

When the object is insurance against a low-probability, high-cost event. A spare, a backup, a redundant part. The whole point is that it may never be used, and a denominator of zero is the successful outcome. Applying utilisation logic here inverts the purpose of the purchase entirely, which is the mechanism behind most stories that begin with somebody deciding they did not really need the second one.

When the single use has an outsized downstream payoff. A tool bought for one repair that prevents a replacement, or a garment bought for one interview. The use count is one and the return is not measured in uses. The check here is simply whether the payoff is real or retrospectively invented, and the honest test is whether you can name the specific outcome before buying rather than after.

And the reverse case, which matters just as much: a high-utilisation object can still be the wrong purchase. Something used daily but which you would not miss, which displaces something you already own, or which you use frequently only because you bought it, has an excellent cost per use and no value. The fraction is a filter for the obvious errors, not a substitute for wanting the thing.

Where This Read Breaks Down

A method is only trustworthy if you know its failure modes, and this one has four that are worth stating plainly before anybody applies it to a serious purchase.

Utilisation is not additive across a household. A cost-per-use figure calculated for one person collapses when several people share the object, and it collapses in the opposite direction when the object is nominally shared but actually used by one person who then resents it. The fraction assumes a single, honest user. Anything bought on the theory that the family will all use it needs the estimate halved at minimum, because shared-use predictions are the least accurate predictions in the whole exercise.

Some objects have their value concentrated rather than distributed. A smoke alarm, a first aid kit, a good umbrella on the one day it matters. Dividing price by uses treats every use as equal, and for a whole class of goods the uses are wildly unequal, with essentially all the value sitting in a small number of instances that you cannot schedule. Where the value distribution is that skewed, the fraction is the wrong tool and should be replaced by a straightforward question about what the bad outcome costs.

The denominator is partly endogenous, and pretending otherwise causes real errors. Buying a better version of something genuinely does increase how often it gets used, because friction suppresses usage and quality removes friction. A pan that works, a bag that opens easily, a tool that does not fight you — these get used more than their mediocre equivalents, which means the cheap option’s denominator is not the same as the good option’s denominator. The honest way to handle this is to allow a modest uplift for the better item and to be suspicious of yourself when the uplift required to justify the purchase starts exceeding about a third.

And the method says nothing at all about whether you want the thing. Cost per use is a filter for waste, not a source of desire. Applied too aggressively it produces a house full of highly utilised objects and no pleasure in any of them, and it will confidently recommend against every book, record, ornament and souvenir ever bought. The fraction is there to catch the purchases you will regret. It is not there to adjudicate the ones you will love, and it is bad at telling the difference.

What the Coming Weeks Do to Today’s Prices

Three categories change state over the next fortnight, and they change in opposite directions.

Winter-linked goods keep softening through early March, and the depth of that softening depends almost entirely on how event-shaped the item is. Dated winter fashion and holiday decor have no residual demand whatsoever and will be discounted to whatever recovers the shelf space, which is the steepest price move available in retail this quarter — and it is a demand discount on a denominator of one. Durable winter hardware behaves completely differently: traction devices, thermal layers and heated gear return to their baseline demand, which is stable, year-round and unaffected by the season’s end. Expect a modest softening as the artificial pull unwinds, not a collapse, and expect it to be brief because the underlying buyers were always there. If you want the equipment rather than the memento, early March is the better week, and the discount will be smaller than the headline clearance in the same aisle — that gap is the entire distinction between a supply discount and a demand discount.

The resolution-resale flood peaks in the next two to three weeks and then tapers. The volume of barely-used fitness and productivity hardware listed in late February is the highest of the year, and it thins as the sellers who were going to give up have given up. The practical read is that the best selection and the best prices coincide right now, and both degrade together through March. For a durable object with a great denominator, this is the annual window, and it closes without announcement. The discount here is a supply discount in disguise — the object is good, the price is low because the owner wants out — which is why it is the best buy of the week despite looking like a clearance.

The post-CNY new-SKU wave does the opposite: it builds. Listings that arrive green in late February accumulate reviews through March, and the ones that survive a return cycle earn the ranking that makes them visible. The buyer’s move is patience — let the review base form, let the first return cycle complete, and buy the survivors rather than the arrivals. A product that is three weeks off a container has told you nothing about itself yet.

Category Notes

Consumables and replenishables. The strongest denominators available and the least interesting decisions. Buy on unit price and storage space, ignore the promotional calendar entirely, and note that the subscribe-and-save mechanism is a genuine information source before it is a discount: its existence confirms a replenishment rate that somebody modelled. The post-CNY restock has returned these to depth after a thin January, and the discount is a supply discount — a real improvement in your cost per use.

Garden and outdoor prep. Late February is the start of the pre-spring build, and the listings reappear on schedule. Seed trays, hand tools, soil systems and the like have denominators in the dozens to hundreds and a use season that begins in weeks, not months. This is one of the few categories where buying now is unambiguously correct rather than merely acceptable, and the restock is a supply discount on a durable object.

Fitness and productivity hardware. The category where the fraction is most brutal and most observable this week, because the resale channel is publishing the denominator for free. Studio bikes, treadmills, dumbbells and standing desks are available barely-used at a fraction of retail from owners who have verified the usage rate is one. Condition is the only real risk, and local pickup removes most of it.

Winter sports and thermal gear. Season-end clearance, but read it as two aisles. Durable hardware clears modestly and is worth buying if you actually use it year to year; disposable winter fashion clears deeply and is never worth buying. The discount depth is inversely correlated with the object’s value, which is the trick, and every one of these markdowns is a demand discount.

Occasion apparel and footwear. Espadrilles before summer and race-day kit before a spring event both look like sensible timing plays, and both are purchases with denominators in the single digits being priced as though they were staples. If you want the item, buy it deliberately as an occasion item and at an occasion price, not as a “deal.”

Cookware, tools and glassware. The categories where published maintenance schedules and deep single-silhouette catalogues cluster, and consequently where cost per use is most likely to be genuinely excellent. These are also the categories least affected by every deadline this week, which makes them the calm corner of an otherwise deadline-driven month, and the direct-brand picks here are supply-discount-adjacent by virtue of being built for repetition.

Bulky low-cost goods and new arrivals. The post-CNY wave contains both restocked staples and unproven new SKUs. Treat the restock as a buy (supply discount) and the new arrival as a wait; the review base is the only reliable separator, and it forms in March.

Policy, Logistics and Cross-Border Notes

The post-Lunar-New-Year reload is the most consequential logistics event of the first quarter for anyone buying into or out of Asia, and its effects on shoppers are mostly delayed and mostly positive. Restocked bestsellers return to depth at pre-holiday prices, which quietly reverses the scarcity pricing of January — a supply discount delivered through freight rather than through a sale. The new-SKU wave brings genuine novelty but also a month of thin review bases and untested return cycles, which is a quality-risk window rather than a price one.

For cross-border buyers specifically, the interaction of the reload with the resolution-resale flood creates one narrow practical point. The resale channel is regional and pickup-biased for bulky goods, which removes the cross-border return problem that makes buying unfamiliar hardware from a distant marketplace risky. A studio bike bought locally from an owner who has admitted it was a mistake carries none of the reverse-logistics uncertainty of the same bike bought new from a platform on another continent. Where the denominator is the only thing that matters and condition is verifiable in person, the secondary market beats the primary one this week.

The winter-clearance logistics story is the reverse of the reload: it is a composition story, not an availability story. Retailers are not running out of winter goods; they are deliberately clearing them to make room for spring, which means the discounts are decisions rather than shortages and they persist on a schedule rather than resolving when a shipment arrives. The durable hardware discount is shallow because the decision behind it is “clear some, keep the rest,” while the disposable discount is steep because the decision is “clear all of it.” Reading the two as one sale is the error, and the fix is to ask which decision produced the tag in front of you — supply or demand.

Returns capacity is the constraint nobody prices, and it is quietly tighter in late February because the resolution surge and the Valentine occasion both produced a wave of January and early-February purchases now meeting their return windows at once. The stated return window is unchanged; the practical time to resolution is not. For cross-border purchases this widens an already meaningful gap between the policy and the experience, and it is the strongest current argument for buying deliberately rather than buying on the theory that reversal is cheap.

What’s Coming Next

Early March is the resolution-resale taper and the winter-clearance floor. The secondary market in fitness hardware thins as the sellers who were going to quit have quit, and the winter aisle stops getting cheaper because the disposable stock is already gone and the durable stock never dropped much. The honest read: the best selection and the best prices on used durable goods are both available this week, and both are gone by the middle of March.

Mid-to-late March is when the new-SKU survivors earn their ranking. The post-CNY arrivals that cleared a return cycle accumulate reviews and rise in search, while the arrivals that did not quietly sink. This is the month to buy the survivors rather than the novelties, and the review-count filter dated before March is the tool that separates them.

Spring is the next real demand wave, and it starts with garden, outdoor and home-organisation rather than apparel. The pre-spring build is already visible in late February listings and accelerates through March. The categories to watch are the ones with high denominators and a use season that begins in weeks — exactly the opposite of the occasion goods clearing this week, and exactly the ones whose restock is a supply discount worth taking.

The strategic read from here: run the cause before you run the price comparison, treat every deadline this week as narrow rather than general, buy the durable version of anything the season made you want, let the dated merchandise go past, and remember that the same percentage-off can be a steal or a trap depending on whether it came from supply or demand. The next fortnight will present an unusual number of very good discounts on objects with very bad denominators, and the discounts will be real.

Bottom line

Yesterday the argument was that a price is a fraction, the page only shows you the top of it, and the bottom is where almost all of the variance lives. Today it is the same idea seen through a single distinction: a discount is either caused by supply or by demand, and late February 2027 is the one week of the year when both are on the floor in the same aisle. The post-Lunar-New-Year reload has landed in depth, so the bestsellers you wanted in January are back at the pre-holiday price because more supply arrived — a supply discount, a genuine deal on a good object. A metre away, the Valentine’s and winter clearances are marked down just as hard, but there the price fell because demand ended on a date — a demand discount, a real discount on a denominator of one.

The tables above are organised around that single idea. The marketplace picks separate the restocked replenishables and the resolution-resale flood — both supply discounts in substance, both worth buying — from the season-end winter hardware and the green new SKUs, where the discount is either a demand discount or evidence-free and the move is to wait. The direct-brand picks separate the repetition-built catalogues, whose entire pitch is a high denominator, from the occasion-built brands, whose pitch is a date. Neither channel is available to the other, and both are more reliable than the product description, because both cost something to establish.

This week, more than almost any other, the memento and the equipment are sitting next to each other at roughly the same percentages off. The Valentine clearance, the winter clearance and the resolution-resale flood are all happening at once, and only one of the three is offering you a genuinely good object at a genuinely good price. Sixty seconds and one question — did the price fall because supply arrived, or because demand left? — tells you which is which.
