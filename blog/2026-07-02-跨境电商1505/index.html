Best Things to Buy Right Now — July 2, 2026

Quick take: Nothing dramatic happened to prices this week, but a lot happened to the fine print. A cluster of certification deadlines closed on June 30, which means a meaningful number of listings you can see today will be gone by the end of August. Europe’s new charge on small parcels went live on July 1, and the delivery companies themselves said they weren’t ready for it. And since mid-June, every store selling into the EU has been legally required to let you cancel an order with a button rather than an email. Net effect: this is an excellent week to buy things that come with paperwork, and a poor week to gamble on an unbranded battery-powered gadget.

What’s Hot This Week

A quiet purge is underway, and it starts with batteries. As of June 30, rechargeable household appliances sold on the biggest US marketplace have to meet category safety testing and have their lithium cells pass UN 38.3 transport certification. Products that don’t clear it get pulled at the end of August. The same week brought mandatory country-of-origin fields on warehouse-fulfilled listings, packaging-recycling registration for several European markets, and CE electromagnetic and RoHS reports for European sellers on the discount platforms. Almost none of this is visible on the product page. What is visible is the consequence: over the next eight weeks, a lot of cheap cordless gadgets from sellers you’ve never heard of will silently disappear. If you want a battery-powered anything, buy from a brand that publishes its certifications, and buy it now while the good and the bad are still sitting side by side.

Your right to change your mind is now a button. Since June 19, any store selling to EU consumers — marketplace giant or one-person independent site — must provide a clearly visible digital withdrawal button, reachable in no more than two steps, that sends you a timestamped confirmation. The penalty for not having it is steep, and here’s the part worth knowing as a shopper: if the button isn’t there, your cancellation window doesn’t stay at 14 days. It stretches to twelve months and fourteen days. So before you buy from an unfamiliar site, go look for the cancel button. If you find it, good. If you don’t, you’ve just been handed a year to reconsider.

Europe’s small-parcel charge started on July 1 — and it landed rough. Parcels under €150 arriving from outside the EU now carry a flat charge based on the goods category, rather than passing through duty-free. In the run-up, the three largest international carriers wrote jointly to EU finance ministers asking for a staged rollout, warning that the technical systems and data standards weren’t finished. They didn’t get it. Practically, expect a few messy weeks: parcels held longer at the border, charges that don’t match what the checkout said, and the occasional payment request on your doorstep. The clean way around all of it is to buy from stock already inside the region — orders fulfilled from an EU warehouse aren’t affected.

A flat fee punishes cheap things hardest. Because the charge is a fixed amount rather than a percentage, it barely registers on a €200 order and completely reshapes a €9 one. That single mechanic is why the sub-€15 impulse buy — the category that defined cross-border shopping for a decade — is being pruned right now. Both of the big discount platforms have been cutting their cheapest European listings and moving inventory into local warehouses. If there’s a low-value item you’ve been repeat-buying from overseas, this is the month to switch to a local seller or buy a larger quantity in one go.

Delivery dates are about to get more honest. From June 29, sellers who handle their own shipping and consistently dispatch faster than they promised have their stated handling time reset for them. It sounds bureaucratic, but it means the date on the listing should start converging on reality instead of being a cushion. Not everyone is happy about it — some sellers have said they’ll simply pad their timelines instead — so for the next month, treat unusually long stated handling times as a negotiating posture rather than a fact.

American shoppers lose the direct-from-overseas option, and gain speed. Two of the largest discount platforms have now closed self-shipped and direct-mail routes into the US, pushing everything through domestic warehouses. One of them has also raised US prices sharply since late June, with some categories more than tripling. The trade-off is real and worth understanding: fewer weird long-tail products, higher prices on what remains, and materially faster delivery on everything still listed.

Boring beauty is out; ingredient beauty is in. The strongest movers in personal care this month aren’t formats or brands, they’re molecules. Kojic acid is the standout — search interest and video views on it have gone up more than fifteenfold in a month — and mass-prestige Western cosmetics brands are now launching directly onto the video-commerce platforms at real scale, which had not been happening a year ago. Expect better formulations at mid-market prices for the rest of the summer.

Buying Tips

Check for the cancel button before you check out. On any store selling into Europe, a visible one-click withdrawal option is now the law, not a courtesy. Its presence tells you the merchant has done its compliance homework. Its absence tells you two things: be careful, and you have far longer than fourteen days to change your mind.

Prefer products that can prove things about themselves. This is the practical takeaway from a very technical week. Anything with a rechargeable battery, anything worn by a child, anything with a water- or stain-repellent finish, and anything sold into Europe in its own box is now subject to documentation it didn’t need in May. Brands that already had the paperwork are unaffected. Brands that didn’t are about to vanish. Buying the documented option costs a little more today and costs nothing in warranty arguments later.

If you’re in Europe, filter for in-region stock. The new parcel charge doesn’t apply to orders shipped from a warehouse inside the EU, and given that the carriers publicly said the systems weren’t ready, in-region stock also spares you the border delays. Check the ships-from field before the price field this month.

Buy fewer, larger orders instead of many small ones. A fixed per-parcel charge rewards consolidation and punishes drip-feeding. If you were going to buy three cheap things from the same overseas seller over three weeks, buy them together.

Treat a very long stated handling time as a choice, not a constraint. With dispatch-speed rules now enforced, some sellers are padding their timelines defensively. If two listings are otherwise identical, the shorter promise is usually the more confident seller — not the more reckless one.

Ignore superlatives, especially soon. Several markets are tightening the rules on words like “best,” “only,” and “number one” in listings, requiring evidence before they can be used. That reform is arriving unevenly, so for now assume any unqualified superlative on a product page is decoration.

What’s Coming Next

The delisting wave arrives at the end of August. Non-compliant battery appliances come off shelves August 30, and packaging registration enforcement follows in the same window. Anything you’re on the fence about in that category, decide before then — not because prices will rise, but because the listing may not exist.

Children’s product filing goes electronic on July 8. Mandatory electronic certificate filing for US imports of children’s goods starts next week. Expect short-term gaps in toys, kids’ clothing, and nursery items as sellers without complete certificates get pulled, and expect the survivors to be better documented.

A second parcel fee lands in November. The charge that started this week gets an additional handling component from November, on top of existing sales tax. For European shoppers, the cost of an overseas parcel in Q4 will be visibly higher than it is today. Anything you know you’ll want for the holidays is cheaper bought in summer.

New tariff bands are on the table. A proposal is circulating that would add up to double-digit percentage duties across a very wide range of origin countries. It isn’t in force and may change shape, but if it lands, the affected categories are the familiar ones: small electronics, home goods, footwear, furniture, and textiles.

Regional sale events keep rolling. The major mid-year sale has already run in North America and Europe, but equivalent events land in India this weekend and in Australia the week after. If you shop those regions, the discount cycle you thought you’d missed hasn’t happened yet.

Bottom Line

This was a week of paperwork, not price tags, and paperwork is easy to ignore right up until it removes something from your basket. The useful reading of it is simple. The market is being split into products that can document themselves and products that can’t, and the second group is on a clock that runs out in late August. Meanwhile, buying has quietly got safer: you can cancel with a button, delivery dates are being forced toward honesty, and the cheap-and-anonymous end of the catalogue is being cleared out.

So: buy the certified battery device rather than the anonymous one, buy from in-region stock if you’re in Europe, consolidate small orders into big ones, and check for the cancel button before you hand over a card. None of that is exciting. All of it will save you money and an argument.
