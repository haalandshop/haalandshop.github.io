Best Things to Buy Right Now — July 16, 2026

Quick take: The single most important shopping story of this month is not a sale — it is a prospectus. A fast-fashion giant preparing to list its shares had to publish, in public, exactly what the last two years of parcel taxes did to its cost structure. The answer: fulfillment now eats 47.7% of operating expenses, US revenue fell 14.3% in the first quarter, and the company has openly said US prices may need to go up. Translation for your cart: the floor under ultra-cheap imported goods is rising, and it is not coming back down. Buy the imported thing you have been putting off. Buy the domestically-made thing whenever the gap is under 20%.

Mid-July is normally a dead week. This year it is not, because the machinery that made cheap things cheap is being repriced in public, and for the first time you can read the actual numbers instead of guessing.

Below: what changed, why your feed’s price tags are drifting up, and twenty specific things worth buying this week — ten from marketplaces, ten direct from brands. This week’s list deliberately skews toward things that are made near you, or things you keep for years, because that is where the math has moved.

What’s Hot This Week

1. The ultra-fast-fashion repricing — the numbers are finally public

A company that sells 4,700 new styles a day to 273 million active shoppers across 160 markets does not usually have to show its homework. This month it did, because it is going public.

The filing cleared its domestic regulatory hurdle on July 10, clearing the way for a Hong Kong listing expected in the next several weeks. What the paperwork reveals matters far more to shoppers than the share price will.

Here is the shape of it:

Revenue$32.1B$38.7B$41.8B$9.05B (+1.1% YoY)

Fulfillment as % of opex——42.8% (Q1’25)47.7%

Read that table twice. Gross margin went up. Net income collapsed. That is the signature of a business whose product costs are fine and whose shipping and duty costs are not.

The cause is not mysterious. The US ended its duty-free treatment for low-value parcels in May 2025. The EU removed its own €150 exemption. Two of the three largest markets for direct-from-factory shipping simultaneously stopped subsidising it. US revenue for this one company fell 14.3% to $2.04 billion in a single quarter. Europe now represents about 35.4% of its revenue and is next in line for the same treatment.

The valuation tells the same story from the other direction: roughly $40–50 billion now, against about $100 billion at the 2022 peak. Investors are repricing the company from a hyper-growth tech platform to a logistics-heavy retailer with trade friction baked in.

What this means for you, concretely:

  • The $6 top is becoming an $8–9 top. Not overnight, and not everywhere, but directionally and permanently. The duty is a real cost that somebody now pays, and it will not be the platform.
  • Direct-from-China shipping is being replaced by local warehouses. That is good for delivery speed and returns, and bad for the price. You are trading three weeks and a lottery ticket for four days and a markup.
  • The value comparison has narrowed. When an imported garment was $6 and a domestically-made one was $40, there was no comparison to make. At $9 versus $38 — after a mid-summer markdown — there is. Start actually running that comparison.
  • Third-party sellers on these platforms are now a large share of the catalogue. The platform-plus-marketplace hybrid means quality variance inside a single storefront is much wider than it used to be. Check the seller, not just the brand.

None of this is a reason to boycott anything. It is a reason to stop treating ultra-low prices as a permanent feature of the landscape. They were a policy artifact, and the policy changed.

2. Parcel taxes now cut both ways — and Brazil went the other direction

The interesting wrinkle this month is that the global trend is not uniform. While North America and Europe added cost to small imported parcels, Brazil removed it.

A presidential decree eliminated the 20% federal import tax on parcels under $50, and cut the rate on goods between $50 and $3,000 from 60% down to 30%. After the change, a Brazilian shopper buying a sub-$50 item from abroad pays roughly 17% in state-level tax and nothing federal. The levy had only been introduced in mid-2024 — it lasted less than two years.

So the world map of cheap cross-border shopping now looks like this:

MarketDirection of travelPractical effect for shoppers
United StatesTightened (2025)Import duty now priced into low-value parcels
European UnionTighteningLow-value import fee arriving; expect price and fee-line changes
United KingdomTighteningParcel handling and split-pay both under new rules
BrazilLoosened (2026)Sub-$50 imports meaningfully cheaper again
MexicoTightenedLocal tax registration and certification now enforced

If you shop from more than one country — expats, students abroad, anyone with family shipping across borders — this table is the single most useful thing on this page. The arbitrage moved. It did not disappear.

There is a second-order effect worth knowing. Direct-mail shipping as a default is over in most large markets. Brazil now runs an automated customs system that cross-checks declared value against actual contents, with penalties running to multiples of the goods’ value and holds lasting one to three months. Mexico requires local tax registration and mandatory certification on appliances and furniture. Large direct-mail parcels run breakage rates of 12–15% and loss rates above 8%.

Which is why 71% of Brazilian shoppers say they will pay a premium for 3-to-5-day delivery, and 65% of Mexican shoppers prefer a seller with local stock and local after-sales support. Speed and recourse beat price in a way that was not true three years ago.

3. Returns got much cheaper to send back — and that changes what is safe to buy

This one is invisible from the front end and it matters enormously.

Since April 1, 2026, cross-border retail export goods can be returned through any customs port, rather than being forced back through the original port of export. Layered on top: goods returned in original condition within six months are exempt from import duty and VAT.

The measured results are dramatic:

  • Reverse logistics cost down roughly 30%, with the first week of the new regime showing average logistics cost declines near 45%.
  • Per-item return cost falling from about 58 yuan to roughly 30 yuan — close to half.
  • Return cycle compressed from 20–30 days to 3–5 days in the best cases, and under 15 days generally.
  • Return volume up 217% year over year in the first week — because sellers who previously abandoned returned goods now find it economical to take them back.

Why you care: the categories that sellers historically refused to accept returns on — apparel, footwear, anything with fit risk — are the exact categories where returns just got affordable. Expect return windows on cross-border listings to get quietly more generous through the second half of the year. Expect more sellers to list apparel and shoes cross-border at all.

Practical advice: when you see a cross-border listing with a genuine 30-day return window, that is now a credible signal rather than a bluff. Six months ago a seller offering that was either large enough to absorb the loss or not intending to honour it. Today the economics actually support it. Check the return terms before you check the reviews.

4. Where the growth went — and why your feed looks different because of it

The centre of gravity in online retail moved this year, and it shows up in what gets promoted to you.

Latin America is the story. The regional market is tracking toward $215.3 billion in 2026, growing at roughly 1.5x the global average, off an e-commerce penetration rate of only about 14% — versus 30%+ in the US and much of Europe. The dominant regional platform posted first-quarter GMV of $19 billion, up 42%, with cross-border GMV up 68% and advertising revenue up 73%. A competing platform reported Brazil as its fastest-growing market and turned profitable there. A third saw its Brazilian monthly GMV grow roughly 25x within three months of launch.

Southeast Asia, by contrast, has entered its own version of maturity: platform commissions there now run 18.5% to 39% all-in depending on category and promotion, up sharply over two years. Sellers describe net margins compressing from 25–35% down to 10–15%.

Two things follow for shoppers:

  1. Seller consolidation is accelerating. Across the largest Western marketplace, active seller count fell about 16% over the past year while total traffic rose nearly 5% — meaning average traffic per surviving seller climbed 25%. Fewer than 8,000 sellers now account for half of third-party volume in the US, down from about 15,000 less than three years ago. You are being shown a narrower, more professional, more heavily-advertised set of products than you were. That means fewer obvious duds — and fewer genuine finds.
  2. Cost pressure travels downstream. Higher platform take rates in Asia and higher fulfillment costs everywhere both land in the same place: your price. When a seller’s all-in platform cost goes from 20% to 30%, the listing price moves or the product gets cheaper to make. Usually both.

Platform & Category Watch

A quick read of where each channel sits this week:

  • Amazon — The big summer event landed June 23–26 this year, out of July for the first time since 2021, and pulled $26.4 billion in US online spend across all retailers over four days, up 9.3%. Day one alone was $8.3 billion. But basket data tells the real story: average order size $47.66, down from $53.34 a year ago, and 69% of items sold for under $20. People stocked up on essentials rather than splurging. Because the event moved earlier, mid-July is now the genuine post-event clearance window rather than late July. Advertising costs are still running 10–15% above the May baseline, which means listing prices in health, personal care and home essentials have not fully relaxed yet. Electronics and appliances normalised faster.
  • TikTok Shop — The top-ten product board contracted sharply, from roughly $25.8 million to $19.9 million in combined revenue month over month, with half the chart turning over. Skincare still holds the top slot. The interesting movers are cheap: a $20–23 extrait de parfum tier is now a genuine top-five category, and a cordless pressure washer at $30.66 moved 64,270 units. Commission rates on the chart ranged from 6% to 25% — the supplement at the top of that range should tell you where the persuasion pressure sits.
  • Independent stores / DTC — The clear winners this quarter are brands that manufacture regionally or publish their cost structure. When imported competitors are visibly repricing, “here is what this costs to make and here is our markup” becomes a genuine sales argument rather than a marketing pose.
  • Europe — Watch for a new line item at checkout as the low-value import fee phases in. Buying from a platform that warehouses inside the EU sidesteps the per-parcel handling charge entirely. Meanwhile end-of-season summer clearance is at its deepest point of the year right now.
  • Latin America — Best conditions in years for sub-$50 cross-border purchases after the tax reversal, but only through sellers with local stock. Direct-mail from abroad is now slower and riskier than the saving justifies.
  • Categories heating up — Outdoor cleaning equipment, affordable fragrance, hair repair, home deep-cleaning tools, protective gloves and DIY safety gear, refurbished tools.
  • Categories cooling — Anything bought in bulk during the June event window. Personal care and grocery staples are still working through stocked-up household inventory; discounts there are thin and will stay thin into August.

Buying Tips for This Week

1. Run the narrowed-gap test. Before buying an ultra-cheap imported item, find the nearest domestically-made equivalent and compare. If the gap is under 20%, buy the local one — you get faster shipping, a real return path, and no exposure to the next round of parcel fees. If the gap is 4x, the import still wins. The point is that this comparison is now worth doing, and eighteen months ago it was not.

2. Read the return window as a solvency signal. Cross-border return costs fell by roughly half this year. A seller offering a genuine 30-day return on an imported item is now making a credible commitment rather than an empty one. A seller offering 7 days or “final sale” on a $60 apparel item, in this environment, is telling you something about either their margins or their confidence in the product.

3. Buy the boring replacement now, not in the panic. Bedding, cookware, sunscreen, work gloves, filters. Every one of these is cheaper in July than in the month you actually need it. This is the least glamorous advice on the page and the one that saves the most money.

4. In Europe, prefer platforms with local warehouses. Not for ideology — for arithmetic. An item shipped from inside the EU avoids the per-parcel handling fee and the customs hold. The listed price may be higher; the delivered price frequently is not.

5. In Latin America, the tax change is real but only helps if delivery works. Sub-$50 imports genuinely got cheaper. But direct-mail parcels are running double-digit breakage and 8%+ loss rates, and customs holds run one to three months. Buy from sellers with local stock. Pay the small premium for 3-to-5-day delivery — most of your neighbours already do.

6. Watch the commission tiers, not the enthusiasm. On social commerce this month, the supplement at the top of the chart paid creators 25%. The pressure washer paid 6%. Both sold well. Only one of them has an army of people financially motivated to tell you about it.

7. Skip anything you bulk-bought three weeks ago. The June event window pulled a lot of household consumption forward. Personal care and grocery staples are not going to get meaningfully cheaper until late August because everyone’s cupboards are still full.

What’s Coming Next

Next two weeks:

  • A major fast-fashion listing is expected to price and debut, with the associated wave of coverage. Watch for platform-side promotions timed around it — listings often get temporarily aggressive around a listing event.
  • End-of-season summer clearance deepens across Europe and hits its floor in the last week of July.
  • Back-to-school demand begins to firm up electronics pricing. The open-box laptop window closes first.

August:

  • Back-to-school peak. Anything dorm, laptop, or storage-related gets more expensive and less available, in that order.
  • European low-value import fee mechanics become visible at checkout for more retailers.
  • Early autumn and seasonal decor arrives while it is still 32°C outside. Ignore it until the second half of the month unless you want a specific sell-out item.

September and beyond:

  • Expect the imported-goods price floor to settle 15–25% above where it sat in 2024, permanently. The platforms have shifted to local warehousing, which costs more and delivers faster. That trade is now structural.
  • Expect return policies on cross-border goods to get quietly better, because they finally can.
  • Expect fewer, larger sellers on every major marketplace. Better average quality, thinner tails, less serendipity.

Bottom Line

The most useful thing you can do this month is update a mental model. Ultra-cheap imported goods were not an efficiency miracle — they were a policy subsidy, and the subsidy has been withdrawn in most of the world’s largest markets. A company selling $41.8 billion of clothing a year at 70% gross margin still lost money last quarter, entirely because of what it now costs to get a parcel across a border.

That does not mean stop buying imported goods. It means the comparison between the $9 imported thing and the $38 local thing is now a real comparison, and the answer is no longer automatic.

Three concrete moves for this week:

  1. Buy the deep-clean and outdoor gear now — pressure washer, upholstery cleaner, work gloves. Peak use, pre-autumn pricing.
  2. Buy one boring replacement you will need in October — bedding, cookware, or a filter. It is cheaper today than it will be the day you need it.
  3. If you were going to buy the imported version of something you will keep for five years, buy the local version instead. The gap closed. You just have not looked in a while.

And if you shop across borders: the map changed this year, in both directions. Brazil got cheaper. Europe and North America got more expensive. Check which side of that line you are standing on before you check out.
