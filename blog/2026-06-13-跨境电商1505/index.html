Best Things to Buy Right Now — June 13, 2026

Quick take: Ten days from today the largest membership sale of the year opens, and you are about to be told, from every direction, to wait for it. Before you do, consider what actually sets the price you pay this month. It is not the promotional calendar. It is the cost of attention, and two days ago a tournament bought effectively all of it. The World Cup opened June 11, runs to July 19, spans three host countries and forty-eight teams, and for the next five weeks the most expensive thing any seller can buy is a shopper who is looking at their product instead of a match. That single fact produces three consequences you can act on today. First, any category with a tournament tie-in is now carrying an attention surcharge — televisions, jerseys, phones, snack food, streaming hardware, anything sold on the back of the event has inflated placement costs baked into its shelf price, and those costs do not disappear on June 23. Second, and inversely, the categories with no tie-in have been starved of promotional budget, because that budget was pulled to fund the tentpole. Starved of budget means starved of traffic, and a listing that stops converting is a listing whose automated pricing starts cutting. The quiet aisles are the cheap aisles right now. Third, and most usefully, the two hours around a kickoff are the softest demand window of the day in the host markets; dynamic repricing and time-boxed coupons react to conversion holes within hours, not weeks. Shop at kickoff, not at full-time. Layered on top is a structural asymmetry almost nobody will mention: the United States deal submission window closed on June 9, four days ago. The American June 23 assortment is already frozen. Nothing new can enter it, which means waiting to see what goes on sale has an informational value of exactly zero for a US shopper — the set is decided, and anything showing no positioning by now is not in it. In Europe the window stays open until June 19, six more days, and a seller who submits in the final six days is submitting because their inventory told them to, not because they planned to. Late submissions skew to overstock, and overstock discounts are the only kind that are ever fully real. Ten days is not a waiting period. It is a window in which the answer is already partly visible, and today is the Saturday to read it.

What’s Hot This Week

The next five weeks contain the most congested retail calendar in recent memory, and the congestion has a shape. Understanding that shape is the difference between a vague sense that sales are coming and an actual plan for what to put in a cart today.

The price of attention just went up, and it is in your shelf price

Start with the thing that changed on Wednesday. The tournament that opened June 11 is the first to run with forty-eight teams, the first hosted across three North American countries in thirty-two years, and it occupies the calendar continuously through July 19. Group stage runs to roughly June 27. Knockouts begin immediately after. There is no quiet fortnight anywhere in that stretch.

For a retailer, a tournament of this size is not primarily a sales event. It is a scarcity event in the one input every seller needs and none can manufacture: a person’s attention. Placement costs in the host markets rise sharply in the windows around matches, and they rise for everyone simultaneously, including sellers with no connection to football whatsoever, because the auction does not care why you want the impression. The effect is broad and it is mechanical.

Here is the part that matters to a shopper. That cost does not evaporate. It is a real operating expense incurred in June, and it lands in the same margin pool that funds discounts. A seller who spends heavily to be visible during the tournament has less room to cut prices afterward, not more. So the intuitive move — buy the thing everyone is promoting, because surely all that promotion means competition and competition means low prices — gets the causality backwards. Heavy promotion in a scarce-attention month is a signal of cost, not of competition. The advertised category is the taxed category.

Run the list of what is being shouted about right now. Large-format televisions. Soundbars. National-team apparel and replica kits. Snack food and beverage multipacks. Beverage refrigerators. Flagship phones positioned on camera quality for stadium use. Streaming sticks and sports-tier subscriptions. Every one of those is being sold into the most expensive attention environment of the year, and every one of those has that expense sitting inside the price. If you need a television before the knockouts, buy one; needs are needs. But do not confuse the volume of the marketing with the quality of the deal. Those are close to inversely related this month.

The quiet aisles are where the money moved out of

Now take the inverse, because it is the actionable half.

A promotional budget is finite. When a seller decides to fund a tournament push, the money comes from somewhere, and it comes from the categories that have nothing to do with the tournament. Home organisation. Hand tools. Water treatment. Pet supplies. Coffee equipment. Bike components. Supplements. Rugged and industrial electronics. Mini PCs and desktop audio. None of these has a football angle, and all of these have quietly lost their share of the promotional budget over the past two weeks.

A listing that loses its promotional support loses traffic. A listing that loses traffic sees its conversion rate and its velocity fall. And a listing whose velocity falls, in a market where a very large share of sellers run automated repricing, gets cut — not as a marketing decision, but as an algorithmic response to a demand signal. The cut is not announced. There is no banner. The price simply drifts down, and it drifts down in the categories where nobody is looking, which is precisely why nobody notices.

This is the single most useful thing to understand about the week of June 13. The best-priced goods in the market right now are the ones with no reason to be in the news. That is not a contrarian pose. It is what happens when a finite budget gets reallocated to a tentpole and the leftovers get repriced by machines.

The US list is closed. The European list is not.

The second structural fact is a timing asymmetry that gets almost no coverage because it lives on the seller side of the platform, and it produces genuinely different advice depending on where you shop.

The American deal submission window for the June 23 event closed on June 9. That was four days ago. Whatever is in the US event is in it; whatever is not, is not. The assortment is locked. This has a consequence that is easy to state and hard to internalise: for an American shopper, the strategy of waiting to see what goes on sale now has zero informational value, because the information already exists and is simply not yet displayed. The set is decided. A product whose seller has shown no promotional positioning, no inventory build, no listing changes in the past two weeks is overwhelmingly likely to be outside the event, and waiting ten days will not change that. It will only cost you ten days of availability during a period when freight and inventory are both tight.

In Europe the window is still open, through June 19. Six more days of submissions. And the composition of those six days is not random. A seller who planned to participate submitted weeks ago, with a curated selection and a margin model. A seller submitting in the final week is usually submitting for one reason: inventory that has not moved is now a problem, and the event is the fastest available exit. Late submissions skew heavily toward overstock.

That gives European shoppers a genuinely different playbook. Overstock discounts are the only category of discount that is reliably real, because the seller’s alternative is carrying the unit into autumn at a storage cost. Where an American shopper is looking at a frozen, planned, margin-optimised list, a European shopper in the last six days is watching a live list absorb the market’s excess inventory. On comparable goods, the European cuts arriving between now and June 19 are likely to be the deeper and more honest ones. If you shop across both, the arbitrage is not subtle: the US list is knowable today and modest; the European list is still forming and will contain the real clearance.

Discovery is narrowing, in two directions at once

Two quieter shifts are changing what you can find, independent of what anything costs.

The first is the branding of the discount tier. The large cross-border platform that built its reputation on unbranded, factory-direct goods now runs something close to forty percent of its transaction volume through branded storefronts. The generic tier has not disappeared, but it has stopped surfacing itself. Search results that would once have returned twelve near-identical unbranded listings now return a branded storefront, then a sponsored branded storefront, then the generics somewhere below the fold. The classic move of buying the factory version of a branded product still works, but it no longer works by searching the product name. It works by searching the component, the specification, or the model number of the part, and by being willing to go three screens deep. If that sounds like more effort than it used to be, that is because it is, and that is the point of the change.

The second is a contraction in creator-led discovery for youth categories, driven by the United Kingdom’s restriction on under-sixteen social access. Whatever one thinks of the policy, the commercial effect is clean: categories that were discovered primarily through creator content aimed at teenagers are being pushed back toward marketplace search and toward parent-facing channels. Discovery through search is far less promotionally volatile than discovery through a viral clip. So for that whole band of products — youth audio, gaming accessories, trend apparel, novelty tech — expect fewer sudden discounts and more price stability. There is less to wait for. If you need something in that group, the case for holding out to June 23 is weaker than it has been in years.

The weekend itself

One more thing specific to today. This is the first full weekend of group-stage play, which means the host markets will experience the deepest and most predictable browsing troughs of the entire five-week run over the next forty-eight hours. Match windows empty out shopping traffic. Repricing systems observe the emptiness. Time-boxed coupons, which are typically configured to fire when redemption falls behind pace, fire.

The practical version of this: if you are going to buy something this weekend, buy it during a match rather than after one. Not because prices are formally different, but because the machinery that sets prices is reacting to a demand hole in real time, and the hole is at its deepest while the ball is in play. It is a small edge. It is also free.

Reading the Ten-Day Window

Put the pieces together and the ten days between now and June 23 sort cleanly into three buckets. Almost every product you are considering falls into one of them, and the bucket determines the timing.

Bucket one: buy now. Anything in a quiet, non-tournament category, where prices are drifting down on reallocated budget rather than on announced promotions. Anything in the youth or creator-discovered band, where the promotional volatility has structurally decreased and there is little left to wait for. Anything you can confirm is outside the American event set — which, given the June 9 lock, is now a knowable fact rather than a guess. And anything seasonal with a hard use-by date, because summer goods bought in the second week of June are bought at the start of their useful season rather than the end.

Bucket two: watch the European window until June 19. If you shop into European storefronts, the next six days are the only genuinely live part of this whole calendar. Late submissions are arriving, they are arriving because inventory forced them to, and the resulting cuts are the most honest ones in the entire five-week stretch. Check on the seventeenth, eighteenth, and nineteenth specifically.

Bucket three: genuinely wait for June 23. Deep-catalogue, high-velocity, low-ticket goods where volume economics still make a real discount worthwhile for the seller, and where the tournament surcharge is minimal because these products were never advertised against the event in the first place. Consumables, accessories, and commodity electronics belong here.

What almost nothing belongs in is a fourth bucket that a lot of people will nonetheless use: wait for June 23 and buy the thing currently being advertised most loudly. That combination pays the attention surcharge and then buys into the most crowded, most margin-defended part of the event assortment. It is the single worst-value decision available this month, and it is the one the marketing is designed to produce.

The Attention Surcharge, Worked Through

It is worth walking the mechanism through slowly, because the conclusion sounds like a slogan and it is not one. It is arithmetic, and once you have seen the arithmetic you will read every promotion you encounter this month differently.

Consider two sellers with identical products, identical costs, and identical margins. Both sell a mid-priced household item. In an ordinary May, both spend roughly the same modest amount to acquire a customer, both hold back a slice of margin for promotional depth, and both can afford a meaningful discount at a summer event because that discount is the last claim on a margin pool that nothing else is competing for.

Now introduce a five-week tournament that consumes the attention of the host markets. Seller A decides to participate — the product has a plausible summer angle, the marketing team wants presence, and the calendar says be visible in June. Seller A bids into an auction where every other advertiser in the market is bidding at the same time for the same finite pool of impressions, most of which are now being consumed by football. The clearing price rises for everyone, including for Seller A, and including on impressions that have nothing to do with the tournament. Seller A’s acquisition cost per customer goes up, and it goes up in June, which is the same month the discount was supposed to come out of.

Seller B, selling something with no conceivable tournament angle, does the opposite. Marketing pulls the June budget entirely, because bidding into a spiked auction for a product nobody is searching for during a match is the worst possible use of the money. Seller B goes dark for a month.

Here is what happens next, and it is the part that matters.

Seller A now has an inflated cost sitting inside June, and a discount commitment that was priced against May’s economics. Something has to give, and what gives is the depth of the discount. The promotion still runs, because it was announced and the calendar demands it, but it runs at a shallower cut than it would have. The marketing is loud, the price move is small, and the loudness is precisely what paid for the smallness.

Seller B, meanwhile, has gone dark and is watching traffic fall. There is no announcement, no banner, no campaign. But the listing is still live, the inventory is still in a warehouse accruing storage cost, and the repricing system is still running. That system observes a velocity decline. It does not know or care that the cause is a football tournament in another category entirely. It sees units-per-day falling below target and it does the only thing it is configured to do, which is lower the price until velocity recovers. Nobody at Seller B decided to have a sale. A sale is nonetheless happening.

So at the end of the month you have two products in front of you. One is heavily promoted, prominently placed, and marked down by a token amount off a price that has an advertising surcharge inside it. The other has no marketing at all and has quietly drifted down several percent on an algorithmic response to a demand hole that a tournament created in a category with no connection to the tournament.

Almost every shopper will buy the first one, because the first one is the one they can see. The visibility is the product of the spend, and the spend is the reason the discount is thin. Being able to see a deal is weak evidence that it is one, and in a scarce-attention month it is mildly negative evidence.

The corollary is what makes this actionable rather than merely interesting. If quiet drift is the dominant discount mechanism this month, then the discount is not on a date. It is already in the price, today, and it will keep drifting for as long as the attention stays expensive — which is through July 19. There is no moment to wait for because there is no event to wait for. There is only a slow, unannounced markdown happening in the parts of the market that the marketing budget abandoned.

Buying Tips for the Week of June 13

Shop during matches, not after them. The browsing trough in host markets during a live match is the deepest, most predictable demand hole of the day, and automated repricing and pace-triggered coupons respond to it within hours. This costs you nothing to exploit.

Treat loud marketing as a price warning. In a month when attention is the scarce input, heavy promotion signals high acquisition cost, and acquisition cost is funded from the same margin that funds discounts. The most-advertised product in a category is rarely the best-priced one right now.

If you shop American storefronts, stop waiting for information. The assortment locked on June 9. What is in the event is already determined. If a product has shown no positioning change in the past fortnight, it is not in the event, and ten more days of waiting will produce nothing except ten days of exposure to inventory risk.

If you shop European storefronts, check on the seventeenth, eighteenth and nineteenth. Those are the final submission days, they skew to overstock, and overstock is where real discounts live. This is the only genuinely live window in the entire calendar.

Search by specification, not by product name, on the discount platforms. With roughly forty percent of volume now flowing through branded storefronts, the generic tier no longer surfaces itself. Search the component, the model number, or the specification, and be willing to go past the first two screens.

Do not wait on youth and creator-discovered categories. Discovery in that band has structurally contracted, which reduces promotional volatility. There is measurably less to wait for than in previous years.

Buy seasonal goods at the start of the season. Fans, filtration, grilling equipment, racks, and cycling gear bought on June 13 deliver a full summer of use. The same item bought on June 23 at a marginally better price delivers ten days less. On seasonal goods, the use value dominates the price difference almost every time.

Check total landed cost, not the headline. Freight on the transpacific lanes remains elevated and bulky goods carry that cost visibly. A rack, a fan, or a floor-care unit can swing materially on shipping between now and the end of the quarter.

Buy the specific model, not the category. Assortment composition can shift around the event boundary for origin-sensitive goods. If a particular configuration matters to you, secure that configuration rather than assuming the category will still contain it in two weeks.

Category Notes: What to Buy Now and What to Hold

Home and floor care. Buy now. The category has no tournament exposure, prior-generation units are heavily repriced ahead of refreshes, and the functional gap between generations in self-washing systems has narrowed to features most people never enable.

Desktop and personal audio. Buy now. All audio marketing went to soundbars and home theatre. Two-channel and desktop audio has been abandoned by the promotional budget and is drifting accordingly. This is the clearest single instance of the reallocation effect on the whole list.

Computing and small-form-factor. Buy now for mini PCs, which price against components rather than calendars. Hold on budget tablets, which have the volume economics that make an event discount genuinely worthwhile.

Mobile devices. Split. Flagship phones marketed on stadium photography are paying full attention surcharge — avoid or ignore the marketing and buy on specification. Rugged and utility devices are outside the promotional gravity entirely and are priced well now.

Tools and hardware. Buy now. Precision hand tools essentially never appear in event assortments. There is nothing to wait for, and the secondary market is clearing professional surplus at prices the primary channel will not match.

Supplements and consumables. Buy now. Expiry-driven rotation means pricing responds to inventory age rather than to marketing, which makes this category responsive to any demand trough and immune to the surcharge.

Outdoor cooking and summer appliances. Buy now, emphatically. These are seasonal goods at the start of their season, sold in channels that do not participate in the June event. Waiting costs you use and gains you almost nothing.

Apparel and footwear. Buy now on anything counter-seasonal — autumn technical layers, boots, outerwear — which is at its annual floor. Direct brands are clearing outgoing colourways to fund incoming ranges, and the functional difference between the two is typically nil.

Cycling, racks and travel gear. Buy now. The season is underway, freight on bulky items rises through the quarter, and none of it touches the event.

Televisions, soundbars, kits and match-day goods. Hold unless you have an actual need before the knockouts. This is the most surcharged part of the market and the most crowded, and June 23 will not fix either problem.

Platform by Platform

The attention surcharge is universal, but it does not land evenly. Each major channel has a different exposure to it, and knowing which is which tells you where to look first.

The large American marketplace. Maximum exposure, and also maximum information. Its June assortment locked on the ninth, which makes it the only channel where you can reason about the event with certainty rather than hope. Its advertising ecosystem is the largest in commerce, which means it is also where the auction spike is most severe and most fully passed through. The practical read: use it for the quiet categories, where third-party sellers running automated repricing are drifting down without announcing it, and treat anything in its front-page promotional surface this month as marketing rather than value.

The American general merchandisers. Moderate exposure. Their competing sales open on the twenty-second, one day ahead, and the multi-year pattern holds — the day-before events are broader in assortment while the main event goes deeper on a narrow list. Their advantage this month is that a large share of their volume is store-fulfilled and therefore insulated from cross-border freight entirely. If you want selection rather than depth, the twenty-second is the better day, and the total landed cost is more predictable than anything shipping internationally.

The warehouse clubs. Minimal exposure, and structurally so. They do not participate in marketplace events, do not bid meaningfully in the open ad auction, and run their pricing on member economics rather than on promotional calendars. This is why a grilling thermometer or a summer appliance from this channel will not be beaten on June 23 — there is nothing on June 23 that applies to it. In a distorted month, the channel that ignores the distortion is the clean read.

The short-video commerce platform. High exposure to the discovery contraction, low exposure to the auction. Its summer incentive programme is doing considerably more for prices than the June event will, and its subsidy structure in the United Kingdom, Europe and Japan remains the most aggressive of any channel. But its discovery engine is the one most affected by the youth access restriction, which means the categories it was best at surfacing are the ones with the least promotional volatility ahead. Buy on it now for the subsidies; do not wait on it for a swing that structurally has less reason to arrive.

The large cross-border discount platforms. This is where the branding shift bites hardest. With roughly forty percent of volume now moving through branded storefronts, the search experience has changed more than the pricing has. The goods are still there and still cheap. They are simply no longer the first thing you see, and the first thing you see is now a brand paying for that position in an auction that got more expensive this month. Search by specification. Go past the fold. The value is intact and the path to it is longer.

The Southeast Asian marketplaces. Low exposure to the North American attention spike, for the obvious reason that the tournament’s host markets are elsewhere and the match times are inconvenient. This is the most underrated fact on this list. These platforms are running normal June economics while Western channels are running distorted ones, which makes them the best relative value in the entire cross-border landscape right now — particularly for rugged devices, mobile accessories, and utility electronics where they have genuine category depth.

The Latin American marketplace. Similar logic with a twist. It sits inside a host region and has real tournament exposure in the categories you would expect, but its long tail — cycling, tools, outdoor — is as quiet as anywhere else and competitively priced. Split your read by category rather than treating the platform as a single entity.

The auction and secondary marketplace. Structurally counter-cyclical and often forgotten in June. Professional and trade surplus clears here regardless of what the consumer calendar is doing, and precision tools in particular reach prices the primary channel does not attempt. It has essentially zero event exposure, which this month is a feature.

Component and specialist electronics retailers. Low exposure. They price against component markets rather than retail calendars, which is why desktop audio and small-form-factor computing are two of the strongest categories on this week’s list. Nothing about a football tournament changes what a speaker driver or a mini PC chassis costs.

Direct brand storefronts. The lowest exposure of anything. Direct brands own their customer relationships, are not obliged to bid for every impression, and run inventory logic on season rather than on event. That is why the entire direct-to-consumer table above reads buy now: not because those brands are unusually generous this month, but because they are the only part of the market currently pricing normally. When everything else is distorted, normal is the bargain.

Three Mistakes to Avoid This Week

Mistake one: treating the June 23 event as a deadline rather than a filter. The event is not the last chance to buy something at a good price. It is a narrow window that applies genuine depth to a specific and fairly small slice of goods — cheap, fast-moving, deep-catalogue stock — and applies theatre to everything else. Treating it as a deadline produces urgency, and urgency in a month where the loudest products are the worst-valued ones is exactly the wrong emotional state to shop in.

Mistake two: assuming a discount you can see is bigger than one you cannot. This is the central error the whole month is engineered to produce. A visible discount required visible placement, visible placement required winning an auction that got more expensive in June, and the cost of winning it came out of the discount. An invisible discount required nothing except a repricing algorithm noticing that a product stopped selling. The second one is frequently the larger number, and it is sitting on a listing with no banner on it.

Mistake three: waiting on the categories with the least reason to move. Two groups have structurally reduced volatility ahead of them: the youth and creator-discovered band, where the discovery contraction has removed the mechanism that used to produce sudden swings, and the direct brand channel, which does not participate in the event at all. Waiting on either is waiting for a thing that has no cause to happen. Meanwhile both are carrying real inventory risk on the specific configurations people actually want, which means the cost of waiting is availability rather than money — and availability, unlike price, does not come back.

Policy and Logistics Watch

A short set of structural items that shape availability rather than headline price, worth knowing but not worth panicking about.

Freight on the transpacific westbound lanes remains materially above last year, and that cost is most visible in bulky, low-density goods — furniture, racks, large appliances, anything that fills a container by volume before it fills it by weight. If you are buying something big, buying it earlier in the quarter is usually cheaper than buying it later, independent of any promotion.

Origin-of-manufacture rules continue to broaden in scope, and the practical consumer-facing effect is assortment composition rather than sticker shock: specific configurations appear and disappear from listings as sellers rebalance sourcing. The defence is simple and has already been stated — buy the model, not the category.

Enforcement activity around intellectual property on the major marketplaces has intensified considerably, with listing freezes running well above prior-year levels. For a shopper this is mostly a good thing, but it has one practical implication: a listing that is unusually cheap for a branded good, from a seller with no history, is more likely than before to be a listing that will not exist next week. Order fulfilment risk, not just product risk.

Cross-border fulfilment capacity in Europe continues to expand, which is a slow tailwind for delivery times into European markets and one of the quieter reasons the European window is the more interesting one over the next six days.

What’s Coming Next

June 14 to 19 — the European submission tail. The only live window in the calendar. Late submissions arriving because inventory forced them to, skewing to overstock, producing the most honest discounts of the season. Check the last three days specifically.

June 19 to 22 — the pre-event lull. Once the European window closes, both assortments are frozen and the entire market goes quiet while everyone waits. Historically the worst few days of the month to shop: no new information, no new discounts, and rising availability risk on anything popular.

June 21 — the gift deadline that already passed its useful ordering date. If you have a Father’s Day purchase outstanding, standard shipping is already tight. This is a buy-today item or a buy-locally item, not a wait item.

June 22 to 23 — competing retailer events open. The major American general merchandisers open their competing sales the day before, and the pattern for the past several years has been that the day-before events are broader in assortment while the main event is deeper on a narrow set. If you want selection, the twenty-second is often the better day.

June 23 to 26 — the main event. Four days, roughly twenty-two markets, over two hundred million members, thirty-five-plus categories. Expect genuine depth on low-ticket high-velocity goods and considerably less than advertised on everything with a large price tag.

June 27 to 28 — group stage ends, knockouts begin. The attention surcharge does not fall here; it concentrates. Fewer matches, higher stakes, more expensive impressions per match. The reallocation effect on quiet categories persists and arguably deepens.

Early July — the counter-programming window. Once the event ends and the tournament moves to knockouts, sellers who sat out June come back with clearance to compete for the residual summer demand. Historically this is where mid-ticket goods see their best pricing of the summer, and it is the one future window genuinely worth holding out for on anything you did not buy this month.

Bottom line

Ten days before the largest sale of the year, the advice that will be everywhere is to wait ten days. The reason to distrust it this year is not that the event is fake — it is real and it will produce real discounts on a specific and predictable slice of goods. The reason to distrust it is that the event is no longer the main thing setting prices in June. A five-week tournament is, because it made attention scarce, and scarce attention is an operating cost that lands in exactly the same margin pool that funds discounts.

That produces a clean rule. Money moved toward the loud categories and out of the quiet ones. The loud categories now carry the cost of that attention in their price. The quiet ones lost their promotional support, lost their traffic, and are being marked down by machines that noticed. Buy the quiet ones. Buy them during a match, when the demand hole is deepest and the repricing is most reactive.

Then apply the geography. American assortments locked four days ago, so waiting buys you no information you cannot already infer — the answer exists, it is simply not on display yet. European assortments stay open until June 19, and the sellers arriving in that final stretch are arriving with inventory problems, which is the only condition that reliably produces an honest discount.

Nineteen of the twenty picks above say buy now. That is not impatience and it is not a sales pitch. It is what falls out of a month in which the discount mechanism is budget reallocation rather than scheduled promotion, and reallocation shows up in the price immediately rather than on a date. The one thing genuinely worth waiting for is cheap, high-turnover, deep-catalogue stock, where volume economics still make a real cut worthwhile for the seller.

Everything else — the fan, the filter, the boots, the tools, the speakers, the thermometer, the bike lights — is cheaper today than the marketing calendar suggests it should be, for reasons that have nothing to do with anyone deciding to be generous. Ten days from now those same goods will be competing for shelf space against an event that was never designed to include them, in a market where every impression costs more than it did in May.

The sale is in ten days. The discount is already here, in the aisle nobody is looking at.
