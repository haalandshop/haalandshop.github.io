Best Things to Buy Right Now — June 1, 2026

Quick take: It is Monday, June 1. The tournament opens ten days from now on June 11, and the big June sale runs June 23 to 26, three weeks and change away. If you have been watching prices for the last fortnight you have probably noticed something odd: not much is moving. The numbers on the tags are sitting still. What is moving is the contents of the box. Where a listing said “wireless keyboard” it now says “wireless keyboard with keycap set and switch puller.” Where a machine was a machine, it is now a machine plus a starter pack. Where a lens was a lens, it is a lens with a hood, a UV filter, an adapter and a cleaning cloth, and the headline says the accessories are worth ninety dollars. This is bundle season, and bundle season is not a discount season — it is the opposite. A bundle is the one promotional tool that lets a seller raise the amount you actually pay without touching the price field that everybody is watching. It leaves the reference price intact, which matters enormously three weeks before an event where discount depth is calculated off that reference price. It defeats every price-comparison tool you own, because a comparison engine cannot match a SKU that exists nowhere else. And it converts inventory nobody wants into something that reads as generosity. The whole thing rests on one assumption the seller needs you to make without noticing: that the extras are worth roughly what the listing says they are worth. They are not. They are worth what you would pay for them standing alone, on a Tuesday, when nobody was selling you anything — and for most bundle contents that number is close to zero. So price the bundle as if the extras are free, and then ask whether the remaining number is a good price for the main item. That single move dissolves most of what you will see this month. What follows is the arithmetic, a four-way taxonomy of bundles that separates the two honest kinds from the two dishonest ones, why the composition of a bundle is a public confession about a warehouse, an inversion nobody talks about — that the slower you consume the extras, the closer to zero you should value them — and twenty picks sorted by whether the bundle in front of you is worth taking.

What’s Hot This Week

Ten days out from a tournament and three weeks out from a sale, the market is in a very specific holding pattern, and the pattern has a shape worth learning because it repeats before every major event.

Prices Are Frozen and Boxes Are Getting Fuller

Across nearly every category with a meaningful June sale presence, headline prices have gone flat. Not down, not up — flat, in a way that looks like nothing is happening. Something is very much happening. A seller who wants a deal slot in the June event has to demonstrate a discount against a reference price, and that reference is computed from recent price history. Cutting the price now damages the discount they can show in three weeks. So the price field freezes solid, and every promotional instinct the seller has gets redirected into the only channel still open: what goes in the carton.

You can see it if you know what to look for. Listings that sold a single item in April now offer a “value pack” as the default variant. Product titles have grown a comma and a list. The main image has gained a fan of accessories arranged around the hero product with little price flags on them. Free-gift-with-purchase banners have reappeared. None of this is a price cut. All of it is designed to read as one.

The Categories Where Bundling Is Heaviest

Bundling clusters where three conditions overlap: the main item is expensive enough that a percentage discount would hurt, the category has a natural accessory ecosystem, and the accessories are cheap to produce. That describes photography, PC components, audio, kitchen appliances, hair and beauty tools, 3D printing, and anything sold to hobbyists. Those are exactly the aisles where you should be most suspicious this month.

It also describes the categories where the bundle is genuinely necessary. A tripod without a head is not a product. A camera body without a lens is not a product. Some bundles exist because the item does not function unbundled, and those are a completely different animal from a bundle assembled to move dead stock. Telling them apart is most of the skill.

The Quiet Corner: Categories With No Accessories to Add

There is a third group that gets ignored in bundle season and benefits from it. Items with no accessory ecosystem — a pair of boots, a knife, a pair of glasses, a pair of jeans — cannot be bundled convincingly. Nobody is going to add a free shoehorn and call it a two-hundred-dollar value. Sellers in those categories, denied the bundle lever, are left with only two options: hold the price and go quiet, or actually cut it. A meaningful share of them quietly cut, because they have no promotional theatre available and still need to move units. The categories that cannot be bundled are where the only real price movement is happening right now. That is not a coincidence, it is a direct consequence.

The Zero-Value Test

Here is the whole method, and it takes about four minutes per listing.

Step one: find the single-unit street price. Not the manufacturer’s suggested price, not the crossed-out number on the bundle listing. The actual price the main item alone is transacting at this week, from a seller you would be willing to buy from. If the item is only sold as a bundle, note that — it changes the analysis and we will come back to it.

Step two: subtract. Bundle price minus single-unit street price equals the implied price of the extras. This is the number the seller does not want you to compute, because it is the only honest statement of what they are charging you for the additions.

Step three: price the extras independently. Search each one on its own. Not the claimed value, the transacting price. A “premium carrying case” is a fourteen-dollar case. A “professional filter kit” is an eleven-dollar filter kit. A “starter set” of consumables is often a genuine forty dollars, and that matters — the test is not designed to prove every bundle is bad, it is designed to find the ones that are.

Step four: apply the discount you actually deserve. Sum the street prices of the extras, then multiply by the fraction of them you would have bought this month anyway. For most people, on most bundles, that fraction is zero or close to it. An item you did not want, at a price below what it usually costs, is not a saving. It is a smaller waste.

The decision rule: if the implied price of the extras exceeds about thirty percent of their summed street value, the bundle is a markup dressed as a deal, and you should buy the main item alone. If the implied price is near zero — which happens more often than cynicism suggests, particularly on end-of-line kits — take the bundle and treat the extras as a genuine bonus.

The reason this works is that it forces the comparison the bundle is engineered to prevent. A bundle listing invites you to compare bundle price against claimed bundle value, which is a rigged comparison because the seller sets both numbers. The zero-value test compares bundle price against single-unit price, which is a comparison the seller does not control, because the single-unit price is set by a market.

One refinement worth adopting: do the subtraction before you look at the claimed value. Once you have read “a hundred and twenty dollars of accessories included,” the number is anchored in your head and every subsequent estimate drifts toward it. Compute first, read the marketing second.

Four Kinds of Bundle, and Only Two Are Honest

Not all bundles are the same trick. There are four distinct structures, and they fail in different ways.

The multi-pack. Two of the same thing, or four, or ten. This is the honest one, and it is honest for a structural reason: the unit is identical to the single-unit product, so per-unit arithmetic works perfectly and no valuation judgement is required. Divide and compare. The only failure mode is buying more than you will use before the item degrades, which is a real risk for anything with a shelf life and no risk at all for a hard good. Multi-packs of filament, storage media, batteries, socket sets in a single drive size, plain sheets — these are usually exactly what they appear to be.

The functional bundle. The main item does not work without the extra, and the extra is specific to it. A tripod and its head. A hub and the adapter for your specific rotor standard. A tent and its fitted footprint. These are usually good deals, and again there is a structural reason: the accessory has a small or nonexistent aftermarket, so the manufacturer is not dumping surplus into your box, they are selling you a complete product in one transaction. A fitted footprint has no independent market to be dumped into. That is the tell.

The accessory bundle. The main item plus a constellation of cheap, generic add-ons with impressive claimed values. Camera plus bag plus tripod plus card plus cloth plus cleaning kit. Appliance plus a set of silicone accessories. Keyboard plus keycaps plus puller plus lube plus a mat. This is the dishonest one, and the giveaway is that every extra has a large aftermarket where it trades for a fraction of the claimed value. Price these at zero, almost always, and buy the main item alone.

The consumable bundle. The main item plus a supply of whatever the main item eats. Printer plus filament. Machine plus a supply of pods. Razor plus blades. Appliance plus proprietary refills. This is the most dangerous structure, because it is the only one that looks better the more carefully you examine it — the extras have real, recurring, verifiable value. The problem is not the value of the extras, it is what accepting them commits you to. The consumable supply is where the manufacturer’s margin lives across the whole life of the product, and a generous starter supply is an investment in making sure you never price-shop the refills. If the consumable is proprietary, a bundle is a subsidy on the razor to lock in the blades. If the consumable is an open standard, the same bundle is genuinely fine.

So: multi-packs and functional bundles, yes, most of the time. Accessory bundles, no, almost never. Consumable bundles, only after you have checked whether the consumable is open or closed.

What the Extras Tell You About the Warehouse

Here is the part that turns bundle-reading from defence into intelligence.

Bundle composition is not designed around what you want. It is designed around what the seller has too much of. Nobody adds their fastest-selling accessory to a bundle — that item is already moving at full price, and putting it in a box at an implied discount is destroying margin for no reason. The things that go in a bundle are the things sitting on a shelf, aging, occupying space that costs money by the cubic foot per month. A bundle is a public confession about a warehouse.

That makes the accessory list readable. When a brand that has never bundled suddenly starts including a specific accessory across its whole range, that accessory was over-ordered. When the bundled extra changes from one month to the next, they are cycling through problem stock. When the extra is a discontinued colour or the previous generation of something, they are clearing a transition. And when a bundle includes an item from a completely unrelated product line, you are looking at a warehouse trying to solve a storage cost problem, not a merchandising team trying to build a coherent offer.

The practical consequences are two. First, if you actually want the bundled accessory, it is about to get cheap standalone, because bundling is usually the first step in a clearance sequence and not the last. Waiting three or four weeks and buying it alone often beats taking the bundle. Second — and this is more useful — the presence of a heavy accessory bundle on a main item is weak evidence that the main item is selling fine. You do not need to prop up a product that is not selling with accessories that are also not selling; that combination just doubles your problem. Bundles get attached to healthy sellers, because a healthy seller is the vehicle that carries the dead stock out of the building. If you see a deeply bundled main item, do not read it as a distressed product. Read it as a distressed accessory riding a healthy one.

The inverse read is stronger still. A main item that is not being bundled, in a category where everything else is, is a product with nothing to carry — either it is too weak to carry anything, or the brand has clean inventory. Check the stock depth, and if it is deep, you have found a brand that is not clearing anything, which tends to correlate with a brand not about to discount either.

One Bundle, Four Consumption Rates

Every previous framework in this series has had an axis that changes the answer — how fast your goods arrive, what kind of buyer you are, where you live. The axis for bundles is how fast you consume the extras, and it is the single variable that determines what the extras are actually worth to you. Two people looking at the identical bundle should reach opposite conclusions, and the difference is entirely here.

Fast consumables, measured in weeks. Coffee, filament for someone who prints constantly, blades for someone who shaves daily, printer supplies in a busy household. You will exhaust the bundled supply inside a month or two, which means you would have bought it anyway at close to full price. Value the extras at close to their full street price. These bundles are frequently the best deals available, and the only check that matters is whether the consumable is proprietary. If it is open-standard, take the bundle without much further thought.

Slow consumables, measured in years. Water filters, appliance belts, replacement blades for a tool you use twice a season, ink for a printer that prints forty pages a month. This is where bundles quietly fail. A four-year supply of filters bundled with an appliance whose realistic service life is five years sounds like foresight. In practice you will move house, the model will change, the filters will sit in a cupboard, and a meaningful fraction will exceed their shelf life before they reach the machine. Value these at maybe a third of street price, and be honest about how many you will physically install.

Durables that never deplete. A case, a stand, a cable, a tool, a tripod plate. You need exactly one of these, forever. If you own one already, the bundled second one is worth precisely zero, and no amount of quality changes that — a very good second thing you do not need is worth the same as a bad one. Value at zero if you own one, at street price if you genuinely do not. The trap here is that these are the most common bundle contents, and they are the ones people most consistently overvalue, because quality is visible and redundancy is not.

Items you will never use once. Bundled software licences you will not activate, generic mounts that do not fit your setup, promotional extras from an unrelated category, a subscription trial you will forget to cancel. These have negative value. Not zero — negative. On a cross-border purchase they add weight, they add declared value, they occupy volumetric space, and they can push a shipment across a threshold. You are paying freight to import an object whose destiny is a drawer.

The payoff is an inversion. Conventional bundle intuition says more stuff for the money is better, and that the way to evaluate a bundle is to add up what is in the box. The consumption-rate axis says the opposite: the slower you consume something, the closer to zero you must price it — which means bundles are worth most to the highest-volume users and worth least to exactly the people who find them most attractive, namely first-time buyers stocking up on a new hobby. The person buying their first 3D printer is the one most tempted by a ten-roll filament bundle and the one least likely to reach roll six before switching materials, changing machines, or losing interest. Bundles are priced for enthusiasts and sold to beginners.

The operational rule that falls out: before you look at what is in the bundle, work out how many months it would take you to consume it at your real usage rate — not your aspirational one. If the answer is longer than you expect to own the main item, the extras are worth almost nothing regardless of what they cost.

Why Bundle Season Peaks Three Weeks Before a Sale

The timing is not random and it is not aesthetic. It is mechanical, and understanding the mechanism tells you how long this window lasts.

A seller three weeks out from a major event has a specific problem. They need to show a compelling discount during the event, and the size of the discount they can show is measured against their own recent price history. Anything they cut now shrinks the discount available then. Meanwhile they still have monthly targets to hit in the interim, and a flat price for six weeks is itself a signal that they have stopped competing. They need to appear promotional without being promotional.

The bundle solves this perfectly. It raises average order value, it reads as generosity, it does not touch the price field, it does not enter the price history that the event discount is computed against, and it moves dead stock out of the building before the event floods the warehouse with new inventory. That last point is underrated: warehouse space in the weeks before a major sale is genuinely scarce, and clearing slow accessories is not just a margin decision, it is a logistics one.

So the bundle window opens roughly four weeks before an event and closes when the event starts, at which point the bundles mostly disappear and are replaced by straight percentage cuts on single units — because during the event, the discount is the promotion, and adding accessories to an already-discounted item is giving away margin twice.

Two practical consequences. First, a bundle available now will likely not be available during the sale, so if the zero-value test says the bundle is genuinely good, waiting for the event may lose you the offer rather than improve it. Good bundles are a now-or-never proposition in a way that price cuts are not. Second, the single-unit price you are comparing against is itself artificially frozen right now, which means the zero-value test performed today slightly overstates how good every bundle looks. The single-unit price has three weeks of downside in it that the bundle price does not. Adjust accordingly: if a bundle only just passes the test today, it will fail it on June 23.

There is a way to date the window on any specific listing rather than assuming the general pattern applies. Look at when the bundle variant first appeared relative to when the price last moved. A bundle that appeared shortly after the last price change is a substitute for a further cut, and it will be withdrawn when cutting becomes possible again. A bundle that has existed continuously for months is not seasonal at all — it is that seller’s permanent packaging, which means the single unit may never be offered and the whole timing analysis is irrelevant. The two look identical on the page and behave completely differently, and the only way to tell them apart is to check whether the bundle predates the current promotional cycle.

The same logic explains a pattern that confuses people every year: bundles get worse as the event gets closer. Early in the window, sellers bundle their genuinely surplus accessories, because those are the ones costing them money to store. By the third and fourth week the good surplus is gone and the bundles are padded with whatever is left, which is usually the items that were surplus for a reason. The first bundles of a season are the honest ones. If you are going to take a bundle at all this month, the arithmetic favours doing it in the next ten days rather than the ten after that.

How to Read a Bundle Before You Commit

A compressed checklist, in the order that fails fastest.

Does a single-unit variant exist on the same page? If yes, the subtraction is trivial and you should do it immediately. If the single unit has been removed from the variant selector recently, that is deliberate, and it is the strongest signal on the page that the bundle is a markup. Sellers do not hide a single-unit option that flatters them.

Is the claimed value stated in round numbers? Genuine accessory pricing is untidy — things cost eleven dollars and thirty-seven cents. Claimed bundle values are almost always round, because they are constructed rather than summed. A “hundred and fifty dollar value” is a marketing number. A “hundred and forty-three dollar value” is at least arithmetic.

Does the bundle change the model number? Bundle SKUs frequently carry a suffix, and that suffix can put you outside the manufacturer’s standard registration path, outside the comparison set on review aggregators, and occasionally outside the standard warranty channel. If the model number on the bundle listing does not appear on the manufacturer’s own site, you are buying a reseller assembly, not a manufacturer product.

Can you buy the extras later? If yes, the bundle’s only real value is the discount, and the discount is what the test measures. If no — if the accessory is genuinely fitted, limited or discontinued — the bundle has option value beyond price, and the test understates it.

What happens to the extras if the main item fails? In a functional bundle, nothing, because the extras were specific to the item and become worthless. In an accessory bundle, also nothing, because they were worthless already. This question mostly exists to make the previous four feel less abstract, but it is a fast sanity check on whether you would still be happy with the transaction in a bad outcome.

Is the bundle heavier than the single unit by more than about a third? On a cross-border order this is not a trivia question, it is a cost question, and we come to it below.

Category Notes for the First Week of June

Photography and optics. The most bundle-saturated aisle in retail and the easiest to win. Filters, hoods, bags, straps and cleaning kits have an aftermarket so liquid and so cheap that the correct valuation is genuinely near zero in nearly all cases. The exception is anything mount-specific or model-fitted. Buy bodies and lenses alone; buy support gear as a matched kit.

PC components and storage. Multi-packs here are honest and worth taking, since the unit is identical and per-unit maths is clean. Heatsink and enclosure add-ons are not — both trade for very little standalone and are frequently the previous revision. Watch for bundles that pair a current-generation drive with a last-generation enclosure that will not run it at full speed, which is a real and common combination.

Kitchen and small appliances. The heaviest silicone-accessory bundling of the year runs right now, in front of the June event. Almost all of it is the classic accessory structure and should be priced at zero. The genuine exception is anything that ships with a proprietary consumable, where the maths flips and the question becomes whether you are comfortable being locked to one refill supplier for the product’s life.

The Outdoor and Seasonal Split

Outdoor and camping is bifurcating in a way worth noting. Northern-hemisphere summer gear is at peak demand and nothing is being discounted, but a lot of it is being bundled, mostly with cheap ancillary items. Meanwhile the technical end of the category — fitted footprints, matched pole sets, model-specific accessories — is running genuine functional bundles that are worth taking, because those items are made in small runs and go out of stock rather than going on sale. The rule for this category is unusually clean: generic extras, price at zero; fitted extras, buy now or not at all.

Audio. Split it the same way as the tables do. Interfaces, amplifiers and sources come with accessory bundles that should be zeroed. Loudspeakers come with cable and stand offers that should also be zeroed, since both trade far below claimed value. But anything model-fitted — a matched stand designed for a specific cabinet, a manufacturer’s own crossover upgrade — behaves like a functional bundle and is priced accordingly.

Hair and beauty tools. This is the one category where the standard advice inverts, and it is worth understanding why rather than just memorising it. A multi-attachment styling tool is not a main item with accessories bolted on, it is a system where the attachments are the functionality — the motor is nearly identical across the range and what you are buying is the set of heads. Zeroing those extras would be a straightforward error. The genuine trap in this aisle is different: attachments are model-locked, the aftermarket is thin to nonexistent, and buying the reduced set now to add heads later almost always costs more in total than the full kit. Decide the configuration once, at purchase, and buy the whole thing.

Apparel and footwear. Effectively unbundlable, which is the point made earlier arriving as practical advice. There is no accessory to add that anybody believes has value, so brands in this category have no theatre available and are competing on price, fit and availability alone. The one thing that behaves like a bundle here is the size-and-colour matrix: a full run in stock is a real benefit, and a picked-over run is a real cost that no discount compensates for. In apparel, availability is the bundle. Buy while the matrix is complete rather than waiting for a cut that will land after your size has gone.

Crossing a Border With a Bundle

Every framework in this series has to survive contact with an international shipping label, and bundles have two failure modes that domestic buyers never encounter.

The Threshold You Cannot Dodge

A bundle ships as one consignment with one declared value and, usually, one classification determined by the item that gives the shipment its essential character. That sounds like a simplification in your favour and it is often the opposite.

The declared value of a bundle includes the extras — at their invoiced value, not at the zero you have correctly assigned them. So you can find yourself paying duty on a filter kit you are going to throw in a drawer. Worse, the extras can push the shipment across a de minimis or duty threshold that the single unit would have cleared underneath. A main item comfortably below a threshold plus ninety dollars of accessories you did not want is a shipment that now attracts duty on the whole value, including the main item. The extras did not cost you their claimed value, they cost you the tax on the entire order.

And here is the part that has no domestic equivalent: you cannot split a bundle to get under a threshold. Two separate orders can be timed, staggered, or routed to arrive as distinct consignments. A bundle is one SKU, one carton, one entry. The single most useful cross-border cost-management technique available to a consumer — controlling shipment composition — is taken away from you the moment you add a bundle to the cart. That alone is worth a meaningful discount, and no bundle listing prices it.

The weight point compounds it. Bundle cartons are larger and heavier, dimensional weight is calculated on the box rather than the contents, and accessories are usually low-density. A bundle can easily cost thirty to fifty percent more to ship than the single unit while adding items you valued at nothing. Freight on worthless goods is the purest form of negative value there is.

The Return You Cannot Split

The second failure mode is the return. A bundle almost always returns as a unit. You cannot keep the machine and send back the accessories, and you generally cannot keep the accessories and send back the machine. This is fine until something goes wrong, at which point it stops being fine in two ways.

First, bundle returns are refused for missing components far more often than single-item returns, because there are simply more components to be missing. Keep every box, every cable, every printed card and every piece of foam until well past the return window. On a cross-border return where you are already paying the freight and absorbing the currency spread, having a refund reduced for a missing cleaning cloth is an expensive way to learn this.

Second, the warranty chain is longer. A bundle assembled by a reseller has a seller of record who is not the manufacturer, and the manufacturer’s serial lookup may not recognise a reseller assembly as an authorised sale. That gap does not matter while the return window is open. It matters enormously in month fourteen, which is exactly when you will need it, and it is the reason the model-number check earlier in this guide is worth the ninety seconds it takes.

The composite rule for cross-border buyers: subtract the extras from the value, add them to the freight, and assume you cannot return them separately. Under those three adjustments, most accessory bundles stop being marginal and become clearly negative.

Where This Framework Breaks Down

The zero-value test is a good default and it is wrong in five identifiable situations, all worth knowing.

When the bundle is the only import path. Some products are simply not sold as single units into some markets. If the bundle is the only way to get the item across a border at a reasonable price, the extras are not optional and their price is not a markup, it is an entry fee. Complaining about the arithmetic does not change the availability.

When the manufacturer amortises deliberately. A handful of bundles are genuinely priced below the sum of their parts because the manufacturer wants the platform adopted — kit lenses are the classic case, sold at a price no standalone sale would justify, precisely because a body without a lens does not create a customer. Real, and rarer than sellers would like you to believe.

Below about forty dollars. The analysis costs more attention than the outcome is worth. Four minutes of research to avoid a nine-dollar overpay is a bad trade unless you enjoy the process. This framework earns its keep from roughly a hundred dollars upward and scales well from there.

When you are buying a gift. Presentation has value to a recipient even when it has none to you. A bundle that arrives as a complete, considered set is worth more as a gift than the same main item alone plus a mental note that the accessories were overpriced. Zeroing the extras is correct for your own purchases and mildly wrong for other people’s.

When the extra is genuinely unobtainable later. Limited runs, discontinued fittings, small-batch accessories from brands that make things in the low hundreds of units. Here the extra has option value that no price comparison captures, because the comparison set will be empty in six months. This is the main reason the direct-brand table has more “bundle works” verdicts than the marketplace table.

There is also an honest limitation in the timing argument. The claim that bundle season ends when the event starts is a strong regularity, not a law. Some sellers run bundles straight through an event, particularly on items where the accessory surplus is severe enough that clearing it outranks discount optics. If you find a bundle that passes the test today, the risk of waiting is real but it is not certain.

What’s Coming Next

Three things to watch over the next fortnight.

Bundles will start converting into straight discounts around June 18 to 20. That transition is the clearest available signal of which items got deal slots. A product that drops its bundle and holds its price is preparing for an event cut. A product that keeps its bundle into the event week did not get a slot and is doing the best it can. Watching the switch is a free preview of the sale assortment about a week before it is announced.

Warranty terms are the next thing worth learning to read, and they are the highest information-per-word section of any listing. A warranty length is not a marketing decision, it is an actuarial one — the manufacturer is publishing their private estimate of how often the thing fails and when. A brand offering five years on a product category where everyone offers one knows something about their failure rate that no review sample of a hundred units will tell you. That is the angle for the next guide.

Watch the categories that cannot be bundled. They have been quietly cutting all month with no announcement, because they have no other lever. As the event approaches and attention moves to the aisles running loud promotions, the unbundlable categories get even less traffic and their prices keep drifting. The last week before a major sale is historically the best moment of the quarter to buy a pair of boots, and nobody ever writes that down because there is no banner to point at.

Bottom line

Prices are frozen and boxes are filling up, and those two facts are the same fact. Three weeks before a major sale, a seller cannot cut a price without damaging the discount they need to advertise later, so every promotional impulse gets rerouted into the carton. What arrives in that carton is not chosen for you, it is chosen for the warehouse — the slow stock, the previous revision, the accessory that was over-ordered in February.

The defence is one line of arithmetic. Take the bundle price, subtract what the main item costs on its own, and look hard at the number that remains. That is what you are being charged for the extras. Then price the extras honestly: full value if you will consume them within weeks, a third if it will take years, zero if you already own one, and less than zero if they are crossing a border with you. Most of the time the remaining number is worse than the single-unit price and the decision makes itself.

Two refinements are worth carrying. Check who assembled the bundle — a marketplace seller clearing third-party surplus and a manufacturer completing its own product are doing opposite things with the same word, and they deserve opposite default assumptions. And check whether the accessory has an aftermarket — if it does, it is worth almost nothing to you in a box, because you can buy it any time for less. If it does not, if it is fitted and specific and made in small numbers, the bundle may be the only chance you get.

The picks above are sorted on exactly that basis. Six of ten marketplace items are buys, six of ten direct-brand items are buys, and the three cleanest buys on the whole page are the three brands that offer no bundle at all — boots, glasses and denim, sitting in the quiet corner of a market too busy filling boxes to notice that somebody is still just cutting prices.
