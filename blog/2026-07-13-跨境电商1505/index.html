Best Things to Buy Right Now — July 13, 2026

Quick take: Global port congestion just hit a four-year high — nearly 3.7 million containers’ worth of capacity, about 11% of the world’s container fleet, sitting in a queue waiting for a berth. Ocean rates jumped again on 1 July. And for most shoppers, delivery times barely moved. That gap is the whole story of this week. The stuff you are buying increasingly never touches a queue, because it was already in a warehouse in your country before you clicked. That changes which listing you should pick, how much a “deal” is worth, and when the summer things you want will finally get cheap. This week also happens to sit in the flattest, least promotional stretch of the entire retail calendar, which makes it unusually honest.

What actually changed this week

1. The queue got worse and your parcel got faster

Container port congestion reached its highest level in four years. Roughly 3.7 million TEU of capacity — about one in every nine container ships afloat — is tied up waiting for a berth. North Asian ports account for 38% of the global backlog, North Europe another 13%, with South-east Asia, the Mediterranean and Africa each on 9%. Ships wait three days at Shanghai, two at Singapore. Benelux and German gateways are running delays of up to a week. Rotterdam’s export dwell time hit seven days, and one of its terminals briefly shut down in extreme heat.

The trigger was weather. Typhoon activity through the East China Sea between 8 and 10 July disrupted Shanghai, Ningbo, Kaohsiung and Taipei, bunching vessels that then arrived everywhere at once. Underneath that is a structural squeeze: cargo-mile demand is growing 7.3% while vessel supply grows 5.4%, and Cape of Good Hope routings still add 10 to 14 days to every affected voyage.

Carriers did what carriers do. The Shanghai container freight index passed 3,200 at the end of June. Shanghai to North Europe rose 12% for a 40-foot box to $5,766. Shanghai to the Mediterranean gained 10%. US west and east coast rates each climbed 7%, to $6,067 and $7,384 per 40-foot container, with transpacific east coast tipped to breach $8,500 this month. Eleven Asia–North Europe sailings were blanked in June against seven planned.

None of which showed up as a delivery delay for most people. That is the interesting part.

2. The shelf moved into your country

The reason the queue did not bite is that the fulfilment model flipped. Cross-border e-commerce exports routed through overseas warehouses grew 3.3 times in the first half of this year. During the mid-year sales, local-warehouse dispatch overtook direct cross-border shipping for the first time — local stock now covers more than half of orders in Poland, Spain and France, one major platform’s Brazilian warehouse saw large-item volumes rise more than 70%, and its European official warehouses ran at 25 times normal order volume. Another platform has said it expects up to 80% of its European orders to eventually ship from local stock. Warehouse networks now cover 27 European countries with next-day service in four core markets.

The mix of what sits in those warehouses changed too. Operators report their inbound mix shifting away from low-margin phone accessories and toys toward small appliances, functional beauty and outdoor tools — heavier, higher-value goods that only make economic sense when they are pre-positioned.

For a shopper, this is not logistics trivia. An item shipping from a domestic warehouse and the identical item shipping from origin are two commercially different products. One has a stable price, a normal returns window, and no border-fee surprise. The other is exposed to freight rates, customs queues and a returns process that may effectively not exist.

3. The discount stopped being the signal

The big summer event finished on 26 June — the first time since 2021 it ran in June rather than July. It set a record: $26.4 billion in US online spend across four days, up 9.3%, with day one alone at $8.3 billion. But both the depth and the breadth of discounts came in below 2024 and 2025 levels. Shoppers turned up anyway.

Underneath the record, baskets shrank. Average order value fell 11% to $47.66. Household spend fell 8% to $143.45. Nearly seven in ten items sold for under $20. Buy-now-pay-later carried $2.1 billion, about 6.6% of orders. The behaviour split: most people bought cheap consumable refills, while a deal-hunting minority used the deepest cuts to trade up on big-ticket items — the share of the priciest goods rose 51% in electronics, 37% in toys and 30% in appliances, while home and garden fell 5% and grocery fell 4%.

Read that as a shopper, not a seller. Retailers discovered they can hold volume with shallower cuts. The headline percentage on a tag is now the least reliable number on the page. What matters is the actual price against the actual thing.

One cost floor did move: on 12 July, US ground shipping commercial rates rose 11.8% and the cheapest lightweight tiers were eliminated entirely. Expect free-shipping thresholds to creep up over the next few weeks, and expect small, light, cheap items to quietly stop being worth ordering alone.

4. Your search box moved into a chatbot

Millions of independent storefronts were switched on inside conversational AI this month by default — ask an assistant for “commuter shoes for wide feet” and it now retrieves, compares and recommends from a live product catalogue, with the brand keeping its own pricing, payment and checkout. The same switch feeds several other assistants. In parallel, the largest AI provider dropped its commission-taking instant checkout and explicitly stepped back to letting merchants use their own.

The volume is real. AI-driven orders have grown roughly fifteen-fold in a year. Around 58% of consumers now use generative AI instead of a traditional search box to find products. During the June event, AI-referred traffic rose 89% year over year and converted about 40% better than other channels. On product pages, AI-referred shoppers convert around 50% higher than organic search.

Here is the catch nobody puts in the announcement. An assistant ranks on structured product data — dimensions, materials, compatibility, specs. It does not read your reviews the way you do, and it cannot see the photo that would have made you hesitate. So it systematically favours whoever filled in the spec sheet properly, which correlates with “organised brand” but not necessarily with “best product”. And a 50% higher conversion rate means people are comparing less before buying.

The shelf-distance ladder: how far away is the thing you are buying?

This is the single most useful thing to check before you click this month, and almost nobody does it. Four rungs, easiest to hardest.

The tell for rung 4 is not the price. It is the absence of a duty line and the width of the delivery estimate. A 15-to-40-day window is not a shipping estimate, it is a shrug.

Three practical rules that fall out of this:

  1. For anything with a size, a fit or a battery, insist on rung 1 or 2. Returns are the entire risk, and returns only work when the warehouse is close.
  2. If two listings for the same product differ by less than about 15%, take the closer one. The delivery and returns difference is worth far more than the gap.
  3. Multi-category orders into Europe now cost more to split. Fees apply per tariff classification, so a basket spanning three categories is charged three times. Buying a kettle, a T-shirt and a phone case as one order does not save you anything; buying three kettles does.

What is rising into the gap, meanwhile: menswear and underwear basics, bedding and linens, textiles and soft furnishings, T-shirts, and non-sports trading cards. Nutrition and wellness supplements now generate the highest revenue per store of any category, followed by vitamins and minerals, general health, sports trading cards and fragrance.

Category watch: what is actually moving

Beauty is still the strongest single category, and the winning angle narrowed. In the US, a single skincare brand’s store cleared an estimated $17–21 million in the week, with a PDRN collagen eye patch alone at $6.5–8 million. The UK’s top store was a colour-cosmetics academy brand at $3.3–4 million, with a low-price makeup set at $2.2–2.6 million. Malaysia’s leader was a lip set at an estimated $35–43 million. The pattern is consistent: a visible result, a simple explanation, and a bundle. Beauty devices — at-home IPL in particular — sell only where the demonstration is strong.

Baby and family care is the surprise. Indonesia’s top store for the week was a baby-care brand at an estimated $32–39 million. Family-care bundles have moved from stable to fast-growing.

Live shopping in the UK is compounding. Livestream sales are up 3.4 times year over year with more than 6,000 live sessions daily, and books of all things are up more than 250% year over year. Roughly 47% of UK Gen Z and millennial shoppers have browsed or bought on a social storefront in the past three months, against 21% across all age groups. Social shopping has broken out of fashion and beauty into home and kitchen, cleaning, electronics and food.

Health testing quietly went global. One major platform has opened multi-panel in-vitro diagnostic test strips — combined influenza A and B, COVID and RSV — for worldwide sale, with official warehouses already running in Spain, France and Poland. Germany and the US are expected to open by the end of August. Cheap at-home respiratory testing is about to become a normal shelf item ahead of winter.

The trust layer got stronger, quietly

Several changes this month all point the same direction: the amount a seller must risk before they can sell to you went up.

  • The US arm of a major social marketplace replaced its flat $1,500 seller deposit with a three-tier structure from 8 July — a $1,500 base, plus a category deposit, plus a risk-scenario deposit ranging from $500 to $10,000. Higher-risk categories now cost real money to enter.
  • A major cross-border platform became the first to join Italy’s national anti-counterfeiting programme. Consumer counterfeit reports now go straight into an expedited channel with a 24-hour priority verification commitment.
  • A large South-east Asian marketplace launched a live store-health centre on 12 July, giving buyers a visible compliance and fulfilment status per shop.
  • A draft revision to China’s e-commerce law went out for public comment in early July. It introduces proportional penalties of up to 5% of a platform’s prior-year turnover for the most serious violations, raises the ceiling on unreasonable-fee and merchant-restriction penalties from ¥2 million to ¥5 million, adds tiered enforcement tools, makes platforms jointly liable for compliance work they outsource, and extends jurisdiction to overseas activity that harms domestic consumers. It also bans platforms from imposing unreasonable trading restrictions or fees on the merchants selling to you — which, over time, shows up as price.

One thing to distrust this week, though. A backend fault hit European marketplace sites in Germany, France, Italy and Spain, wrongly reassigning product categories and causing bestseller rankings to collapse or vanish outright. It has been acknowledged and rankings will restore automatically. Until they do, the bestseller badge on those sites means nothing. Ignore it and read reviews instead.

There is also a softer signal on safety. New US electronic filing requirements for consumer-product safety data took effect on 8 July, and on day one enforcement was relaxed — shipments are not being blocked for missing data. But the enforcement moved rather than disappeared: it shifted from stopping goods at the border to auditing after release, with five-year record-keeping and the threat of seizure and penalties later. Practically, that means the next few months will let through a slightly wider range of imported goods than the rule intended. Buy children’s items, anything electrical and anything that goes in a mouth from sellers with a real brand presence.

Most likely to break out on Amazon, Shopify and TikTok Shop

Amazon. From 31 July, the upstream warehousing programme in the US stops accepting anything measuring 18 inches or more in length, 14 or more in width, 8 or more in height, or weighing 20 pounds or more. Any one of the four disqualifies it. That quietly advantages the compact version of every bulky product — folding fitness equipment, compact air purifiers, collapsible storage, slim-profile furniture — because compact SKUs keep the cheap upstream buffer that oversize ones just lost. Expect the compact variant to be better stocked and better priced through Q4 than the full-size one. Separately, the pre-qualification screen for the featured offer slot is being removed globally through the end of the year, which puts more sellers into the buy-box rotation and should tighten pricing.

Shopify and independent stores. Discovery is now partly agentic, and agents rank on structured data. The brands about to break out are the ones whose products can be described precisely: footwear with honest sizing, tools with exact dimensions and compatibility, consumables with clear ingredient lists. Duty-inclusive pricing went live on 10 July for cross-border stores, so the displayed price on a well-run independent site is increasingly the landed price. Independent stores now derive an average 31% of volume from outside their home country, rising to 38% for brands in the $5–50 million band.

TikTok Shop. The breakout shortlist is beauty with a demonstrable result, nutrition and wellness supplements, menswear and underwear basics, bedding and soft furnishings, and non-sports trading cards. Phones and electronics accessories, fashion accessories, T-shirts and home decor are the least concentrated categories, meaning a new brand can still win there on creative alone.

Buying tips for this week

  1. Check the dispatch location before the price. On any listing, find where it ships from and where returns go. If those two answers are not in your country or customs bloc, mentally add 20% to the price to cover the risk. This one habit is worth more than any coupon.
  2. Stop reading the discount percentage. Discount depth fell this year and volume held anyway, which proves the percentage is now marketing rather than information. Compare the actual number against what the item cost six weeks ago, not against a struck-through figure.
  3. Bundle by category, not by errand. If you are ordering into the EU, fees are charged per tariff classification. Three items from three categories in one parcel is charged three times. Three items from the same category is charged once.
  4. Buy small, light items in groups now. Lightweight postal tiers were eliminated on 12 July and ground rates rose nearly 12%. Free-shipping thresholds will follow within weeks. Consolidate the small stuff.
  5. Treat an AI recommendation as a shortlist, not an answer. Assistants rank on structured specs, which rewards whoever filled in the form best. Take its three suggestions, then go read the one-star reviews yourself. That is the step the 50% conversion lift is quietly skipping.
  6. Ignore bestseller rankings on European marketplace sites for now. A backend fault scrambled categories and rankings across four countries. Reviews and question threads are unaffected; the badges are not.
  7. If you want compact, buy it after 31 July; if you want oversize, buy it before. The upstream warehousing rule change closes the cheap buffer for large and heavy items. Bulky goods lose a cost cushion going into Q4; compact versions keep theirs.
  8. Put fading summer categories on a list instead of in a cart. Swimwear, outdoor furniture and camp kitchen gear all just rolled over. Five to six weeks of patience is worth roughly a third of the price on all three.

Bottom line

This week’s headline was a shipping crisis that did not reach the customer. One in nine container ships is queuing, rates jumped again, and the shelf still had things on it — because the industry spent two years quietly moving inventory into the country before you order it, and it finally crossed the halfway line. That is genuinely good news for shoppers, but only if you learn to tell rung one from rung four, because both are sitting on the same page at similar prices, and only one of them will actually let you send it back.

The second thing that happened is subtler. The discount stopped carrying information. A record sales event ran on the shallowest cuts in years and still broke records, which tells retailers they never needed to cut that deep. Meanwhile discovery is migrating into assistants that rank on spec sheets and convert 50% better precisely because people are comparing less. Between shallower discounts and less comparison, the burden of judgement is shifting back onto you.

So the honest advice for a flat, unpromotional week in the middle of July: buy the boring, close, well-specified thing. Take the domestic warehouse over the 15%-cheaper mystery box. Buy the consumables and the bedding and the travel gear that are actually in season. Write down the swimwear and the patio furniture and come back for them in September. And when an assistant hands you a confident answer, treat it as the beginning of the research rather than the end of it.
