Best Things to Buy Right Now — July 10, 2026

Quick take: Two weeks from today, a ten percent surcharge applied to every single item imported into the United States since February hits its legal expiry date and switches off automatically. Nobody can extend it. That sounds like the best news a shopper has had all year, and every freight newsletter in the world is running a countdown clock. It is worth understanding why the clock is pointing at the wrong thing. The surcharge that expires on 24 July has already been replaced by a differently-shaped one that is higher for most of what you buy and, unlike the first, has no expiry date at all. What actually changes on 24 July is not the amount. It is that the tariff stops being flat and starts depending on where a thing was made — and, by a piece of timing that is either a coincidence or extremely good planning, the largest marketplace made country of origin a mandatory, non-skippable field on every inbound shipment ten days ago. For the first time, the field that determines the price is printed on the page. Meanwhile the same marketplace turned on twelve-month price history, rewrote how search ranks products, and took away sellers’ ability to chase you after a bad review. Four things, one week, all of them in your favour if you know where to look. Here is what to do about it, plus twenty things worth buying.

What actually changed this week

1. The tariff that expires on 24 July has already been replaced

Start with the mechanics, because the mechanics are where the misreading happens.

Since 24 February, everything arriving in the United States from anywhere has carried a flat ten percent import surcharge. It was introduced under a provision of trade law that comes with a hard statutory ceiling of one hundred and fifty days. That ceiling lands on 24 July at one minute past midnight Eastern time. There is no presidential mechanism to extend it and no legislation moving through Congress to replace it. On that date, the ten percent genuinely does vanish.

What has been much less widely reported is what was built to catch it. A separate trade action, opened in early June and finalised at a hearing on 7 July, establishes a two-band replacement covering essentially all consumer goods. The higher band, twelve and a half percent, applies to forty-six economies including mainland China and Hong Kong, China, plus Japan, South Korea, India, Vietnam, Australia and Brazil. The lower band, ten percent, applies to fourteen economies including the European Union, the United Kingdom, Canada, Mexico, Indonesia and Pakistan. The carve-outs are energy, rare earths, a short list of pharmaceuticals and aircraft components — which is to say, nothing that arrives in a parcel.

Run the arithmetic on a typical consumer good made in China. Before 24 July it carries the long-standing twenty-five percent trade action plus the ten percent temporary surcharge, so roughly thirty-five percent. After 24 July it carries the same twenty-five percent plus the new twelve and a half percent, so roughly thirty-seven and a half. The number goes up by two and a half points, and the framing changes from a temporary measure with a countdown to a standing one with no scheduled review.

There is a second action still to land. A parallel investigation into structural overcapacity — steel, solar, electric vehicles, batteries, chemicals, industrial machinery — is due to report this month, with rates unannounced. Those categories carry a genuine risk of triple-stacking: the old trade action, the new twelve and a half percent, and whatever the overcapacity finding produces.

So the honest consumer summary is this. If you have been holding off on a purchase waiting for 24 July, stop waiting. Nothing you buy gets cheaper that day. The one thing that does change is far more useful than a discount.

2. Country of origin stopped being decorative

From 24 July, the tariff on a product is no longer a flat rate applied to everyone equally. It is a two-band rate that depends entirely on where the item was manufactured. A pair of headphones assembled in Vietnam and a functionally identical pair assembled in Mexico now sit two and a half percentage points apart before anything else is counted, and that gap is permanent rather than provisional.

Ten days before that becomes true, on 30 June, the largest marketplace began enforcing mandatory country-of-origin declaration on all inbound fulfilment shipments across its United States, United Kingdom and European stores. Any listing without the origin field completed can no longer create a new shipment at all. Sellers found this out abruptly — a lot of shipping plans were blocked in the first week — and the practical consequence for shoppers is that the origin field on a product page went from patchily populated and frequently wrong to universally present and legally consequential inside a fortnight.

Put the two together and you get something genuinely new: a field on the product page that reliably predicts the direction of that product’s price over the next six months. It is not a perfect predictor, because a seller’s landed cost also depends on freight, warehousing and how much stock they already hold. But it is the best single-glance signal available, and until two weeks ago it was not reliably available at all.

In cookware, hand tools, cast iron, some furniture and a handful of apparel categories, the domestic option is no longer the extravagant choice it was in 2024. That is a real change and it is not going to reverse.

3. You can now see twelve months of price history

This is the most immediately useful thing in this article.

The largest marketplace’s shopping assistant now surfaces price history directly on the product detail page, with thirty-day, ninety-day and three-hundred-and-sixty-five-day views. Not a browser extension, not a third-party tracker with patchy coverage, not something you have to know to install. It is on the page.

Anyone who has used a price tracker knows why this matters, but a lot of people have never used one, so here is what you are actually looking at. A twelve-month price chart on a marketplace listing almost always falls into one of four shapes, and each one tells you a different thing.

The sawtooth. Price drops five to fifteen percent for a week, returns to baseline, repeats every three to six weeks. This is coupon-cycle management. The baseline is the real price and the dips are the real price too — you are simply choosing which week to buy. Set a reminder rather than paying baseline.

The step-up before an event. Price rises quietly over two to three weeks, then a large percentage discount is applied from the new, higher anchor during a sale event. The chart makes this obvious and nothing else does. In a normal year this shape appears in the second half of June ahead of the summer event; this year the event moved to late June, so the shape is now visible in the first half of June. If you see a step-up starting now, the item is being prepared for an autumn event.

The one-way ratchet. Price rises in small increments, never falls back, no dips at all. This is a seller passing through cost — freight, fees, tariff — on a product with no competitive pressure. The ratchet does not reverse. Buy it now or accept you will pay more later. Expect to see a lot of these in August as the July freight peak and the new tariff bands work through.

The genuine markdown. Price drops and stays down for more than three consecutive weeks. This is either a discontinued line, an inventory problem, or a seller facing a storage fee cliff. It is the best of the four to buy into, with one caveat: check whether the reviews after the drop are talking about a revised version of the product.

One further note. The history is only useful if you also check who is selling. The same listing can pass between sellers, and a price drop that coincides with a change of seller is not a discount — it is a different offer for the same page.

4. Search, reviews and seller accountability all moved in the same week

Three quieter changes landed within days of each other and they interact.

On 7 July the ranking algorithm behind marketplace search completed a significant revision, raising the weighting of a product’s actual conversion rate by roughly sixty percent and adding a penalty for keyword-stuffed titles and back-end fields. The practical effect on what you see is that page one shifts toward products people genuinely buy and keep, and away from products that were merely well-optimised. Broad, low-differentiation listings — the sort with forty comma-separated keywords in the title — lose visibility. This is a rare instance of a ranking change that is straightforwardly good for the person doing the shopping.

Separately, the marketplace removed the button that let a seller contact a buyer who left a one-, two- or three-star review. Unless you initiate contact yourself, a seller can no longer reach you after a bad review. Anyone who has ever left a three-star rating and then received a politely worded offer of a refund in exchange for taking it down knows exactly what this was for. The important consequence is about time: reviews written from this point forward are written under less pressure than reviews written before it. When you sort a product’s reviews, the newest ones are now the cleaner sample. That is the opposite of the usual advice.

Third, the account health system changed so that each policy violation now runs its own independent one-hundred-and-eighty-day clock and expired penalties are removed automatically rather than lingering. This sounds like pure seller-side housekeeping, and mostly it is, but it has one shopper-visible effect: fewer sellers get pushed into sudden account suspension mid-order because of a stale violation from eight months ago. Fewer abrupt cancellations on things you have already paid for.

5. Europe is deleting the bottom of the market

Since 1 July the European Union’s one-hundred-and-fifty euro duty-free threshold no longer exists. In its place is a flat three euro charge per tariff classification per parcel during the transition, with an additional two euro handling fee arriving in November. All twenty-seven member states apply the three euro charge, but national top-ups vary and the variation is worth knowing:

  • France has suspended its two euro national fee for now
  • Italy has postponed its national charge to October
  • Romania has been charging since early this year
  • Netherlands and Belgium are applying the EU rate only, with no national addition

On a thirty euro order the three euro charge is a ten percent tax, and it does not scale — it is the same three euros on a two euro order. That arithmetic has now visibly killed a tier of the market. One of the two largest low-price cross-border platforms has begun bulk-delisting sub-fifteen-euro items across its European storefronts and has shifted roughly sixty percent of its European order volume into local warehouses, which sidesteps the per-parcel charge entirely but raises the floor price. The other has seen its European traffic fall sharply, has pulled back hard on paid acquisition, and has found that discounting no longer arrests the decline — because the problem is structural, not promotional.

For a European shopper this reads as follows. The five-euro impulse purchase shipped individually from overseas is over. What replaces it is a slightly higher floor price with faster delivery and a landed cost you can actually see at checkout, which is a better deal in every respect except the headline number. And there are two short arbitrage windows: France and Italy are currently cheaper on small cross-border parcels than their neighbours, and Italy’s window closes in October.

One practical rule that comes straight out of the fee structure: the three euro charge is levied per tariff classification, not per parcel. Four T-shirts in one order is one charge. A T-shirt, a phone case, a candle and a set of screwdrivers in the same order is four. Bundle within a category.

Every quarter, marketplaces subsidise the categories they want to grow, and this quarter the subsidies are unusually concentrated and unusually easy to read. This is a genuinely predictive tool because a fee rebate always ends up partly in the shelf price — a seller who gets eighty percent of their commission back can price a sofa in a way they could not in June.

7. A platform died this week, quietly

Vietnam’s Sendo Farm ceased operations. A few years ago Sendo was routinely listed as one of the four major platforms in the Vietnamese market. It is now gone, and the ordinary consequences of a platform shutting down apply: stored balances, unredeemed vouchers, open returns, and warranty claims routed through the platform rather than the manufacturer.

This is worth generalising into a habit rather than treating as regional news, because platform consolidation is running fast in several markets at once. Southeast Asia is down to a small number of viable general marketplaces. Regional players in Europe and Latin America are being squeezed from both ends. The rule that comes out of it is dull and effective: do not hold value inside a platform you would not describe as one of the top two or three in its market. Spend gift-card balances, redeem loyalty points, do not stack vouchers for later, and for anything with a real warranty, register with the manufacturer directly rather than relying on the marketplace order record to survive.

8. Returns from overseas just got cheaper to run

A change on the export side that will show up on the shopping side within a quarter: China’s cross-border returns regime now allows goods to be returned through any port, rather than requiring re-entry through the port they left from. Reverse logistics has always been the single biggest reason cross-border sellers write restrictive return policies — the cost of getting one item back across a border routinely exceeds the item’s value, so the policy becomes “keep it” for cheap goods and “no returns” for expensive ones.

Lower reverse-logistics cost changes the calculation specifically in the middle and upper price bands. Expect to see meaningfully better return terms on higher-value cross-border listings — electronics, appliances, quality apparel — appearing over the second half of the year. If you have avoided buying a four-hundred-dollar item from a cross-border seller because the return policy was unworkable, that objection is on its way to being solved.

Platform and category watch

The largest marketplace. Origin declaration mandatory since 30 June. Ranking revision on 7 July with conversion weighted about sixty percent more heavily. Negative-review contact removed. Twelve-month price history on the product page. Account health penalties now independently timed. Holiday deal submissions opened on 8 July with a fifty dollar early-submission discount, a 8 September deadline for the main event and 20 October for the November one — which tells you that this year’s holiday pricing is being locked in right now, in July. Business-buyer tooling also got an AI assistant in the UK store, which matters more than it sounds: the business side of that marketplace is a very large channel, and tooling that lets buyers compare supplier contract terms automatically will push commodity pricing down across the office, cleaning and maintenance categories.

Short-video commerce. Week twenty-eight rankings, covering 6 to 12 July, show beauty holding the top slot in almost every market but with the winning angles diverging sharply by country. In the United States the leading store did an estimated seventeen to twenty-one million dollars, driven by a repair-positioned eye mask at six to eight million on its own, with the rest of the top ten dominated by skincare and colour cosmetics; the non-beauty entrants worth noting were die-cast car models and multi-function snack hoodies. In Indonesia the leading store was baby and maternity care at thirty-two to thirty-nine million dollars, which is the single strongest signal on the board and marks family care as a serious category rather than a niche. In Malaysia a lip set led at thirty-five to forty-three million, with an IPL hair-removal device also charting. The United Kingdom was topped by a women’s probiotic supplement — supplements outranking cosmetics is unusual there — with men’s workwear, baby appliances, handheld garment steamers and fitness equipment filling out the list. Thailand showed the most extreme concentration, with a local ginseng-lemon serum far ahead, white denim shorts second, and, oddly, fuel injector cleaner taking several slots. Mexico remains small at under six hundred thousand dollars for the leading store but with clean category signals.

The useful read across those six markets is not “beauty is hot.” It is that the same week produced six completely different number ones. A category that has just taken off in one market is at its peak price there and its most competitive price in the market where it took off two years ago. Skincare in the United States is mature and therefore keenly priced; the same products in Mexico are early and expensive. Baby care in Indonesia is at the top of its curve right now, which is precisely the wrong moment to buy into it.

Southeast Asia. The 7.7 promotional cycle closed on 10 July, with Thailand strongest in beauty, personal care and household goods and Vietnam strongest in womenswear, consumer electronics accessories and mother-and-baby. The Singapore store of one major regional platform is changing campaign fee structure on 14 July, moving to per-unit charging for non-programme merchants — expect small upward price adjustments there in the second half of the month. Indonesia introduces a half-percent platform-withheld income tax on domestic sellers from 1 August across the four largest platforms, which is small but will be passed through.

Latin America. The largest marketplace’s regional fast-track is offering registration subsidies of up to twelve thousand dollars in Brazil and six thousand in Mexico to three thousand brands this year, along with a roughly one dollar per unit reduction in Mexican fulfilment fees. A regionally dominant marketplace has also rolled out a new overseas-warehouse inventory performance system that ties a seller’s storage allocation to a score, with bonus capacity above seventy points. Both point the same way: more local stock, faster delivery, better pricing in Mexico and Brazil through the second half.

Europe. Covered above, but one addition. The three euro per-classification charge and the November two euro handling fee together make the per-parcel cost of small cross-border orders roughly five euros by the winter. National retailers holding local stock have not had a structural cost advantage this large in a decade, and it shows in the marketplace picks below.

Independent stores and Shopify. Cross-border is now a core channel rather than an aspiration: merchants on the largest independent-store platform derive an average of thirty-one percent of their volume from outside their home market, up from twenty-two percent in early 2024, and thirty-eight percent for brands in the five to fifty million dollar band. The infrastructure change that made that possible arrived on 10 July, when duties-inclusive pricing became available in managed cross-border selling — duties, import taxes and conversion costs built into the displayed price rather than sprung at checkout. That is the single most requested feature from merchants selling into Europe and Australia, for the obvious reason that a shopper who sees an unexpected customs charge at the final step abandons the cart.

There is a second thing happening on the independent side that is less comfortable. AI shopping agents are now a measurable share of traffic, and they are ruthless comparators — they evaluate total landed cost including shipping and return friction across dozens of stores in milliseconds. Brands are responding by investing in structured product data, and some are experimenting with offering different prices to authenticated agents than to human browsers. That practice sits in a grey area nobody has ruled on. The defensive move as a shopper is simple and slightly annoying: if you are using an AI assistant to shop, spot-check two or three of its recommendations directly in a browser before buying.

Also worth knowing: scripts-based checkout customisation on the enterprise tier of that platform stopped executing on 30 June. When those break, they do not error — they simply stop applying. If a store you buy from regularly has a volume discount or free-shipping threshold that has silently stopped working since 1 July, that is why, and it is worth an email to their support rather than assuming the offer was withdrawn.

Most likely to break out on the big three

On the largest marketplace. Anything where a domestic-origin option exists at within twenty percent of the imported price. The tariff bands make that gap close further through the autumn, and the origin field is now populated, so the comparison is finally possible to make in one glance. Cast iron and clad stainless cookware, hand tools, and mattresses are the three categories where this is most visible right now. Second, expect a surge in listings with short, clean, human-readable titles as the ranking revision penalises keyword stuffing — treat a tidy title as weak evidence of a seller who is optimising for buyers rather than crawlers.

On independent stores. Duties-inclusive pricing turning on for cross-border checkout means European and Australian shoppers can now buy from American and British brands without the customs ambush. The categories that were most damaged by that ambush — mid-priced apparel, cookware, bags, skincare sets in the eighty to two-hundred-and-fifty band — are the ones that will suddenly convert. If you are outside the United States and you have abandoned a cart on a US brand’s site in the last two years because of an unclear duty position, go back and look again this month.

On short-video commerce. Heavy goods. The over-fifty-pound and over-twenty-six-inch fulfilment channel opened this month, and the sixteen-to-fifty-pound rates dropped by up to five dollars a unit. Furniture, home gym equipment, large appliances and outdoor gear are entering a channel that has never carried them before, which means launch pricing, aggressive creator commissions and a lot of unproven listings all at once. The opportunity is real and so is the risk — buy the ones with a named brand behind them and skip the ones with a generic name and four reviews.

How to buy this week — six rules

  1. Stop waiting for 24 July. The expiring surcharge is being replaced with a higher, permanent one for most consumer origins. If a purchase is already justified, the cheapest day is a day in July, not a day in August.

  2. Read the origin field before the review count. It is now mandatory, populated and legally consequential, and from 24 July it determines a two-and-a-half point spread that does not expire. Where two similar products are close on price, the lower-band origin is the more price-stable purchase.

  3. Open the twelve-month price chart on anything over a hundred dollars. Identify which of the four shapes you are looking at. Sawtooth means wait a fortnight. Ratchet means buy now. Step-up means an event is being prepared. Sustained markdown means check the recent reviews for a product revision.

  4. Sort reviews by newest, not by most helpful. Sellers lost the ability to contact you after a low rating this month. Reviews written from July onward are the least pressured sample the platform has ever had.

  5. In Europe, bundle within a category. The three euro charge is per tariff classification, not per parcel. Four items from the same category is one charge. Four items from four categories is twelve euros. France and Italy are cheaper than their neighbours right now, and Italy’s advantage ends in October.

  6. If you want something large and heavy, look at the auction marketplace and short-video commerce this quarter. Both are subsidising bulky-goods fulfilment right now — one with a commission rebate of up to eighty percent, the other by opening an oversize channel and cutting heavy-parcel rates. Those subsidies expire; the furniture will not be this cheap in November.

Bottom line

The genuinely important thing that happened this week is not the tariff. Tariffs move, get replaced, get litigated and move again, and the net effect on any single purchase is usually a few percent that gets absorbed somewhere in a supply chain you cannot see.

What happened this week is that four pieces of information that were previously hidden became visible on the same page at the same time: where a thing was made, what it has cost for the last twelve months, whether people who bought it actually kept it, and whether the person who wrote the bad review was allowed to keep it up. None of those were reliably available in June. All four are available now, they are free, and they are on the product page rather than buried in a tool you have to know about.

That is a much bigger deal than two and a half percentage points. Use them. And if you were going to buy something durable anyway, buy it in the next fortnight, because 24 July is a swap rather than a cliff and the direction after it is up.
