Best Things to Buy Right Now — May 26, 2026

Quick take: Sixteen days to the tournament, twenty-eight to the June sale, and every guide published between now and then will rank things by discount depth. Yesterday’s argument was that the return policy governs the first thirty days and is completely unpriced. This is the sequel, because the return policy has nothing to say about year three, and year three is where the money in a durable purchase is actually decided. The strongest published signal of long-term quality is not the warranty, not the review average, and not the brand’s reputation. It is whether the manufacturer publishes a spare parts list. A company that will sell you a named gasket, a numbered fastener, a specific bearing or a particular switch has told you something about the product’s expected working life that no marketing copy can imitate, because publishing a parts diagram is an operational commitment with real inventory cost, real design constraints and real support burden sitting behind it. Nobody publishes an exploded view of a product they expect to be landfill in twenty months, because the diagram would cost more to maintain than the product earns. That asymmetry is the whole insight: the parts list is expensive to fake and cheap to check. What follows is why a parts catalogue functions as a disclosure document, what publishing one actually commits a company to, the difference between a consumable and a part, how to run the check in five minutes before you buy, what a missing list implies, where the read breaks down, and twenty picks sorted by what you can open rather than what the tag says.

What’s Hot This Week

Three things are moving, and all three are about disclosure rather than price. The pre-event fortnight is quiet on the forward side, which is precisely why the documentation layer is briefly visible before the discount noise buries it.

Parts catalogues are thinning ahead of the event

Support pages get edited before a tentpole event in the same way policy pages do, and in the same direction. Parts that were listed as individually orderable quietly become “contact support”, assemblies get consolidated so that four small components become one expensive module, and older model years drop off the compatibility selector entirely. The reasoning is operational rather than sinister: an event compresses a quarter of demand into four days, warehouse space gets reallocated to forward stock, and slow-moving parts inventory is the first thing to lose its shelf. The practical consequence is that a parts page you check today may be thinner in four weeks, and the version that matters is the one live when you need the part in 2029. Screenshot the diagram. Parts pages carry no change log and no notification obligation, and a catalogue that quietly loses half its line items tells you the product line is being wound down long before any announcement does.

Repair disclosure rules are pulling diagrams into the open

The regulatory direction across major markets has been consistent for several years now: certain categories must make spare parts available for a defined period, must publish repair information, and must not use software or fastener design to obstruct independent service. The interesting effect is not compliance itself but the sorting it produces. Brands that were already publishing diagrams treat the requirement as paperwork. Brands that were not are now publishing the thinnest possible list that satisfies the letter of it. You can tell the two apart in about a minute, because a genuine parts catalogue is organised by exploded view with part numbers, while a compliance catalogue is a flat list of four items with no diagram and no cross-reference to model years. The first is a company that already knew which screw was which. The second is a company that had to go and find out.

Aftermarket parts sellers are stocking before the tournament

The independent parts trade runs on a different calendar to retail. Sellers of gaskets, brush heads, filters, hinges, mounting brackets and fan assemblies build inventory ahead of the demand wave that follows any big consumer-electronics and appliance moment, because the returns and failures land six to ten weeks after the sale, not during it. Right now that stock is arriving, which means the aftermarket depth for a given model is unusually easy to assess. A product with a thick independent parts market is a product that a lot of people have found worth fixing — an aggregate judgement made by thousands of owners voting with repair money, which is a far more honest quality signal than a review score collected in week one by someone who has owned the thing for nine days.

The Parts List Is a Disclosure Document

Treat a parts catalogue the way you would treat a financial disclosure: not as marketing, but as a statement the company is operationally bound by. Marketing copy is free to produce and carries no obligation. A parts diagram carries obligations in four directions at once, and each of them costs money.

The first obligation is inventory. Every part number on a published list implies stock somewhere, held for years, earning nothing, occupying space that could hold sellable product. The second is design stability, because a published part number is a promise that the part it refers to will keep fitting. The third is support, since a diagram invites contact from people who intend to open the product, and every one of those contacts costs staff time. The fourth is the tacit admission that the product can be opened at all, which constrains how it is assembled.

Now consider what those four obligations imply about the company’s own forecast. No rational organisation accepts years of dead inventory, frozen design, support load and assembly constraints for a product it expects to be discarded in two years. The maths does not work: the cost of maintaining the catalogue exceeds the revenue from parts sales on a short-lived product by a wide margin. So the diagram only appears when the company genuinely expects a long service life — long enough that parts revenue, brand loyalty and reduced warranty exposure make the overhead worth carrying.

That is why this signal is hard to counterfeit. A brand can claim durability in a headline for nothing. A brand can print a ten-year warranty and quietly rely on the fact that most claims are never filed, that receipts get lost, and that the failure will probably fall outside the covered list anyway. But a brand cannot publish a hundred-part exploded diagram without actually building the operation that stands behind it. The claim costs nothing; the catalogue costs money every quarter. When you find a real parts catalogue, you are not reading a promise — you are reading evidence of expenditure already incurred on the assumption that you will still own the thing in a decade.

What Publishing a Parts Diagram Commits a Company To

It is worth being specific about the costs, because the specificity is what makes the signal trustworthy. Three commitments do most of the work.

Inventory that earns nothing

Spare parts are the worst inventory a company can hold. Demand is unpredictable in timing and tiny in volume, the units are small and awkward to store, and the revenue per line item is trivial next to the finished product. A replacement heating element might sell forty units a year against a product that ships forty thousand. Worse, the obligation runs longest exactly when the product is least commercially interesting — you need the part in year eight, when the model has been discontinued for five years and every rational inventory system says to write it off. Holding that stock is a deliberate decision to lose money on a line item in order to keep a promise. Companies that make that decision have usually made it because service revenue and repeat purchase from long-term owners outweigh it, which is only true if the products actually last.

Design freeze and the cost of a running change

Manufacturers make quiet running changes constantly: a supplier switches, a tolerance shifts, a moulding gets revised to shave cost. Published part numbers make those changes expensive, because every change either has to remain backward compatible or has to be documented as a new part with a model-year cross-reference, and both options cost engineering time. A company with a published catalogue has effectively given up the cheapest cost-reduction lever available to it, which is silently changing the product while keeping the name. The corollary is useful in the other direction: when a brand’s parts list stops cross-referencing older model years, the design has been changed in ways nobody wants to document, and the product you are looking at is not the product the reviews were written about.

The support burden the diagram creates

Publishing a diagram tells customers that opening the product is a legitimate act. Some fraction of them will do it badly and then get in touch, and the company will absorb that cost. Firms that fear this outcome hide the diagram, use one-way fasteners, and route every problem to whole-unit replacement, because replacement is operationally simpler and, on a cheap product, genuinely cheaper. So the presence of a diagram also tells you the company believes its product is worth the argument. That belief is expensive to hold and easy to abandon, which is why it correlates so tightly with things that actually survive.

Consumable availability is only reassuring when the consumable is what breaks.

Luxman and Koorui both depend entirely on third-party parts and split on whether the independent ecosystem is real. A forty-year-old integrated amplifier has no factory support whatsoever, yet the platform itself carries capacitor kits, replacement pots, service manuals and donor chassis, plus a professional recap trade that treats these units as routine work. That is a functioning parts economy, and it makes an old amplifier a safer long-term purchase than most new ones. The monitor has the superficial version of the same thing: boards and panels are listed, but they are generic salvage with no model mapping, and the panel is both the failure mode and roughly the price of the whole unit. An aftermarket that cannot supply the failing component is an aftermarket in name only.

Deepcool and Neewer are both affected by seasonal model churn and split on whether churn matters. Case model numbers rotate constantly, and it makes almost no difference, because a chassis has no proprietary parts — the fans are standard sizes, the fasteners are standard threads, the panels are glass and steel, and the effective parts list is a published industry specification that predates the product by decades. Churn is harmless when the standard is the catalogue. Light stands invert this: the failure is the leg collar, the locking knob or the spigot, those are model-specific mouldings, and the model was replaced two seasons ago. When the failing part is proprietary, churn is not an inconvenience, it is the end of the product.

Oigen and Fossibot both have no parts listings at all, for opposite reasons. A sand-cast iron pan has no parts because it has no assemblies — it is one piece of metal, the only maintenance is seasoning, and the failure mode is dropping it on concrete. The absence of a parts list here is information rather than a gap, and monolithic construction is the oldest form of repairability there is. A portable power station is the opposite: it is a dense assembly of cells, a battery management board, an inverter and a fan, every one of which is a wear item, and none of which is offered. A sealed battery product with no cell replacement path is not a durable good, it is a three-year subscription with the payments taken up front.

Best Direct-to-Consumer and Brand Picks

Each one describes what SKUs happen to exist in an intermediary’s listing database — an artefact of what resellers chose to stock, how salvage supply flows, and how a platform’s model-number taxonomy behaves over seasons. None of them is a statement by the manufacturer about its own product, because the manufacturer is not the one publishing.

The values below are the opposite kind of claim. “Exploded diagram published”, “parts shop with named components”, “repair service but no parts sold”, “whole-unit replacement under warranty” and “service manual with standard fasteners” all describe a manufacturer’s disclosure and service posture, and only the party that owns the drawings, the tooling and the bill of materials can adopt one. A marketplace reseller cannot publish an exploded view of something it did not design, cannot run a repair bench for something it did not build, and cannot honour a replacement guarantee it did not write. Run the logic the other way and it holds just as firmly: “model number churn breaks parts” is meaningless applied to a brand’s own store, because a brand’s site has exactly one catalogue, curated by the party that decides what the model numbers are. A sauna heater is an appliance with a clear wear profile — elements, thermostats, contactors, stones — and every one of those is a named, stocked, individually priced component that an electrician can fit in an afternoon. That converts a heater from a purchase into a serviceable installation, and it is the strongest form of this signal there is. The lounge chair is honest but impractical: replacement cushions, covers and mechanisms exist, they are made to order in the original fabric, and both the price and the wait sit close to the territory of reupholstery by a local workshop. Yoshihiro and Nicks Boots both offer factory service without selling components, and the verdicts split on whether the service reaches the actual failure. A knife has no parts by construction, so a sharpening, thinning and re-handling service is complete coverage — every way a good knife degrades is on the service menu, and a blade that gets thinned properly every few years outlives its owner. The boot looks like a parallel case because resoling is famous and excellent, but resoling addresses the sole, and what actually ends a heavy work boot is the vamp crease and the upper’s stitching, neither of which is a sold component nor a routine service item. Repair coverage is only as good as its overlap with the failure distribution, and current build queues mean the boot is a decision to make with a fitting rather than at distance.

Tubus and Aclima both promise replacement rather than repair, and the divergence is about what replacement admits. A steel rack carries an unusually literal version of this: a replacement shipped anywhere in the world quickly, which exists precisely because the product is used far from home and a broken rack strands a loaded bicycle. Replacement here is not a substitute for durability, it is insurance layered on top of a component that essentially does not fail. The base layer is the other case. When a brand’s remedy for wool is a new garment rather than a darn or a patch, it is telling you the expected life is short enough that repair infrastructure was never worth building. Replacement-only remedies on textiles are a quiet statement about expected lifespan, and the honest response is to buy at the price that lifespan justifies.

Carlos Santos and Jura both rely on documented service with conventional fasteners, and split on who is allowed to do the work. A Goodyear welt is effectively an open industry standard: the construction is documented everywhere, the tooling is a century old, and any competent cobbler anywhere can resole the shoe without the brand’s permission or parts. That is the most robust form of serviceability, because it does not depend on the manufacturer continuing to exist. The espresso machine has documentation and standard screws too, but the brew unit on most models is not user-removable, descaling and service routines are gated through authorised channels, and independent repair depends on parts distribution the brand controls. Serviceability that requires the manufacturer’s cooperation is a weaker asset than serviceability that does not, and it is worth less than the current price implies.

Consumables Are Not Parts, and the Difference Is the Whole Game

The most common mistake in this analysis is counting consumables as evidence of repairability. They are not the same category of thing, and conflating them produces exactly the wrong conclusion.

A consumable is a component the manufacturer wants you to buy repeatedly: the filter, the brush head, the pod, the belt, the blade cartridge, the bag. Its availability proves nothing about longevity, because selling consumables is a profit centre with its own commercial logic. Plenty of throwaway products have superb consumable supply, precisely because the consumable is where the margin lives. In some cases the availability is actively adverse — a razor with abundant cartridges and a sealed handle, or a printer with plentiful ink and no serviceable head, is optimised for recurring revenue rather than length of service.

A part is a component the manufacturer would rather you never needed: the hinge, the bearing, the switch, the pump seal, the control board, the structural moulding. Selling one is a small, awkward transaction that generates support load and no meaningful margin. Consumables are offered because they make money. Parts are offered because the company decided the product should keep working. Only the second is a signal.

There is a reliable tell that separates the two on a brand’s own site. Consumables are merchandised: they appear in the main navigation, they have their own product photography, they are offered in multi-packs and on subscription, and the copy encourages you to keep a spare. Parts are buried: they live under support rather than shop, the photography is a technical drawing or nothing at all, they are sold singly, and the copy is neutral to the point of discouraging. The commercial energy behind a component tells you which of the two it is, and the buried one is the one that carries information. A brand that merchandises its filters harder than it documents its hinges has made its priorities legible without meaning to.

The practical test takes one question. For the product in front of you, name the component most likely to end its useful life — not the one that wears out fastest, the one whose failure means the object goes in a skip. Then check whether that specific component has a part number. A gasket list on a moka pot, brush heads on a sealed-battery toothbrush and salvage panels on a monitor all fail this test in the same way: the catalogue is real, the coverage is real, and none of it touches the thing that actually kills the product. Coverage is only meaningful measured against the failure distribution, and the failure distribution is usually one or two components deep.

Why Two Identical-Looking Products Differ by a Decade

Put two products of the same category and price side by side. Same specification sheet, similar reviews, comparable finish. One will still be working in twelve years and the other will be gone in three, and the difference is very rarely in the specification.

It is in the assembly decisions, and those decisions are visible if you know where to look. The long-lived one is held together with threaded fasteners, uses a serviceable bearing rather than a moulded pivot, keeps its electronics on a board that can be reached without destroying the housing, and places its wear items where a hand can get to them. The short-lived one is bonded, ultrasonically welded, or clipped together with barbs that break on first removal, and its wear items are buried behind assemblies that must be cut apart. Neither approach appears on the specification sheet, because neither is a feature — they are consequences of a decision about how long the object is meant to exist.

Cost pressure explains the split. Adhesive is faster than screws on a production line, welded shells need no fasteners and no assembly labour, and a moulded pivot is cheaper than a bearing by a factor that matters at volume. Every one of those choices is correct if the product’s design life is short, and every one of them is wrong if it is long. So the manufacturer makes them according to its own honest forecast — and then publishes, or does not publish, a parts diagram that matches. The diagram is downstream of the assembly decision, which is why it is such a reliable proxy. You cannot easily inspect glue versus screws through a product photo, but you can always check whether anyone thought to give the screws part numbers.

The gap compounds. A serviceable product accumulates an ecosystem: independent repairers who know it, forums that document its faults, sellers who stock its parts, and a resale market that prices it as an asset. A sealed product accumulates none of these, so when the first failure arrives there is no path except replacement, and the decision is made for you.

The Five-Minute Parts Check

This is worth doing before any purchase over roughly the price of a week’s groceries, and it genuinely takes five minutes.

One: name the killing component. Before searching anything, decide what will end this object. For anything with a rechargeable battery, it is the cell. For anything that pumps or compresses, it is the pump seal or the compressor. For anything with a hinge or a lid, it is the hinge. For anything heated, it is the element or the thermostat. For textiles, it is abrasion at the stress points. You are almost always right on the first guess, and the guess sets up everything else.

Two: search the brand plus “spare parts” or “replacement parts”, not the product name. You are looking for a catalogue at the brand level, not a single listing. Note whether what comes back is an exploded diagram with numbered callouts, a flat list of four items, or nothing. The diagram is the strong signal, the flat list is the compliance signal, and nothing is nothing.

Three: check whether your killing component is in there. This is the step people skip, and it is the only step that matters. A hundred-item catalogue that omits the cell, the pump head or the hinge has told you the answer.

Four: check backwards compatibility across model years. Look for a model-year selector or a cross-reference table. If parts are listed only for the current generation, the catalogue is a marketing artefact that will vanish when the model does. If parts are listed for products discontinued five years ago, the commitment is real and demonstrated rather than promised.

Five: check the independent market. Search the model number plus “repair” and see whether anyone outside the brand has bothered. Teardown videos, forum threads with part numbers, independent sellers stocking sub-assemblies — all of this is aggregate evidence that the product was worth fixing, produced by people with no incentive to flatter it. A thin official catalogue plus a thick independent one is often a better position than the reverse, because the independent market survives the manufacturer’s discontinuation decisions.

What you do with the result is simple enough to state as a rule. A pass on all five steps justifies paying more than the comparison shopping suggests, because you are buying a longer object. A pass on steps one to three but a failure on model-year continuity means buy it, but buy the wear parts now while they exist. A failure at step three, where the killing component has no part number, means reprice rather than reject: divide by the realistic service life and see whether the annual cost still beats the alternative, because plenty of sealed products are worth owning at the right number. The check is not a filter that rejects things, it is a conversion that turns a sticker price into a cost per year, and cost per year is the only figure that lets you compare a serviceable product against a disposable one honestly.

What a Missing Parts List Actually Implies

Absence is evidence, but it is not always the same evidence, and reading it correctly requires separating three quite different situations.

The first is monolithic construction. Some products have no parts list because they have no parts: cast iron, a solid wooden board, a forged blade, a ceramic vessel, a leather strap. These are the most repairable objects in any home, in the sense that maintenance restores them indefinitely and there is nothing to fail independently. Absence here means the question does not apply, and treating it as a negative signal will steer you away from the most durable things you can buy.

The second is genuine sealed design. A product that could plausibly be serviced — it has a battery, a motor, a board, moving parts — and offers nothing has made a deliberate choice. The design life is short, the intended remedy is replacement, and the price should be read as a rental for that period. This is not automatically a reason to walk away, but it is a reason to divide the price by the realistic number of years and compare that figure rather than the sticker.

The third is scale. A small manufacturer may make excellent, serviceable, standard-fastener products and have no parts catalogue simply because publishing and maintaining one is beyond a team of nine people. The tell is construction rather than documentation: visible screws, conventional bearings, standard hardware, and a willingness to answer a direct email about a component. A small brand that will sell you a part by email is offering the same thing as a large brand’s catalogue, minus the web page. Ask before assuming.

Fasteners, Adhesive and the Physical Tell

When documentation is unavailable, the object itself will tell you, and product photography usually gives away more than the copy does.

Look at the seams. A visible parting line with recessed screw bosses means the housing is designed to come apart. A continuous seam with no fasteners anywhere means it is glued, welded or clipped, and opening it is destructive. Look at the base of anything that sits on a surface — that is where fasteners hide, often under feet or a label, and a photograph of the underside is worth more than the entire feature list.

Look at how wear items are positioned. A filter behind a latched door, a battery under a screwed plate, a belt reachable through a panel: all of these say somebody expected replacement. A filter that requires removing the top assembly says nobody did. Look at the fastener type as well. Standard cross-head or hex fasteners mean any toolkit works. Security bits mean the manufacturer anticipated entry and decided to make it slightly annoying, which is a mild negative but usually surmountable. One-way fasteners and rivets on a consumer product mean entry was designed against, which is decisive.

None of this requires owning the product, and all of it survives marketing. A brand can write “built to last” without consequence, but it cannot photograph a glued seam as a screwed one. When the copy and the construction disagree, the construction is the honest party.

Where This Read Breaks Down

Three limits, and they are real enough to change decisions.

The first is that repairability and quality are correlated, not identical. A product can be beautifully serviceable and mediocre — some brands publish exhaustive parts catalogues precisely because their products need parts more often than the competition, and a thick catalogue can be a symptom of a high failure rate rather than a long design life. Cross-check against how often the parts are actually being bought. A catalogue with deep stock of one specific component is telling you that component fails a lot.

The second is that some categories are genuinely not worth repairing, regardless of whether they can be. Below a certain price, the labour cost of any repair exceeds replacement, even if every part is available and every screw is standard. On a twenty-dollar appliance the parts question is nearly irrelevant and the time spent checking is wasted. This read earns its keep on things costing hundreds, on anything installed rather than placed, and on anything you intend to keep for a decade.

The third is that the signal can lag reality. A published catalogue reflects the commitment the company made when the product was designed, which may be years ago and under different ownership. Brands get acquired, product lines get outsourced, and the parts operation is an early casualty of cost-cutting because it is a pure expense line. The catalogue can stay online long after the stock behind it is gone. The check for this is availability rather than listing: a part shown as perpetually backordered is not a part, it is a page. Listed and stocked are different claims, and only one of them is worth anything.

Category Notes

Anything with a lithium cell. The cell is the design life, full stop. Ask one question — is the cell replaceable, by anyone, at any price — and let the answer dominate. A power station, a toothbrush, a vacuum or a speaker with a sealed pack is a three-to-five-year object whatever the build quality suggests, and should be priced accordingly.

Heated appliances. Elements and thermostats are the wear items, they are cheap, and they are the clearest divide in this whole exercise. Brands that sell elements make appliances that last twenty years. Brands that do not make appliances that last four, and the two can look identical in a showroom.

Anything with a pump or compressor. Check whether the pump is serviceable separately or sold as a sealed assembly. A sealed pump priced near a new machine is a total loss disguised as a repair option, and it is extremely common in pressure washers, espresso machines and dehumidifiers.

Displays and panels. Effectively unrepairable at consumer level in every case. The panel is most of the cost, panel replacement is rarely economic, and no parts catalogue changes this. Buy on warranty length and price, not on repairability, because the option does not exist.

Textiles and footwear. The relevant question is construction, not parts. Welted and stitchdown footwear can be resoled by anyone; cemented cannot. Woven and knitted goods can be darned and patched; bonded and taped seams cannot. The construction method is the parts list for anything made of cloth or leather.

Monolithic goods. Cast iron, forged steel, solid timber, stoneware. No parts, no failure modes worth the name, maintenance rather than repair. These are the quiet winners in any long-run cost comparison and they never appear in a repairability discussion because there is nothing to discuss.

Policy, Logistics and Cross-Border Notes

Parts availability is more geographically uneven than product availability, and the gap catches people out. A brand may publish a full catalogue and ship parts only within its home region, which turns a documented repair into an international freight problem for a component worth eleven dollars. Check whether the parts shop ships to you before treating the catalogue as an asset, because a diagram you cannot order from is decoration.

Small parts also cross borders badly. A tiny consignment attracts handling and clearance charges that scale poorly against its value, and a gasket set can arrive costing four times its price. That does not eliminate the benefit, but it does mean the sensible move is to buy the predictable wear items with the product rather than individually years later — a spare gasket set, an extra element, a second filter housing. Buying the parts at the same time as the object is nearly always cheaper than buying them when you need them, and it removes the discontinuation risk entirely.

Where the product is made matters less than where the parts are held. A brand manufacturing overseas with regional parts distribution is in a better position than one manufacturing locally and centralising parts at a single warehouse. Ask where the parts ship from, not where the product is built.

One timing note for the weeks ahead. Event-period purchases of anything installed or serviceable are worth pairing with a parts order in the same transaction, because parts stock is thinnest immediately after a demand spike and the wait stretches for months. If you intend to buy a heater, a stove, a rack or a machine in the June sale, order the wear components alongside it rather than in September.

What’s Coming Next

The parts list tells you whether an object can survive year three. It does not tell you whether anyone will still want it, and those are different questions with different answers. What a three-year-old example of a product sells for, as a fraction of its original price, is a verdict delivered by people who have actually owned the thing, priced in a market with no marketing in it. Bottom line

It is the last Tuesday in May, sixteen days before a tournament and twenty-eight before a sale, and the discount conversation has not started. Use the remaining quiet on the question the discount conversation will never touch.

Three things follow. First, check the diagram before the review. A parts catalogue is expensive to maintain and impossible to fake, while a review score is free, early, and written by people who have owned the product for a fortnight. One of those is evidence of a company’s own forecast about how long its product will work; the other is a first impression. Spend the five minutes on the one that costs the company money.

Second, measure coverage against the failure, not against the item count. A hundred part numbers that omit the sealed cell, the welded pump head or the proprietary hinge tell you less than four part numbers that include the element and the thermostat. Name the component that will end the object before you look at anything, and check for that one. Most catalogues answer the question in about thirty seconds once you know which question you are asking.

Third, read absence carefully rather than harshly. No parts list on a cast iron pan means there is nothing to break. No parts list on a sealed battery product means the design life is short and the price is a rental. No parts list from a nine-person workshop that uses standard screws probably just means nobody has built the web page yet — so ask. The same missing catalogue means three completely different things, and the construction of the object tells you which.

And keep the asymmetry that makes the whole exercise pay. A discount is a one-time saving on the purchase price. Repairability is a recurring saving on every year you keep the thing, and it compounds silently in the direction nobody measures. The sale in four weeks will decide what you pay once. The parts diagram decides what you pay for the next decade, and it is readable today, for free, by anyone who thinks to look.
