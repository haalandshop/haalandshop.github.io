Best Things to Buy Right Now — July 1, 2026

Quick take: July 1 is a fee-reset day across the industry, and this year the reset points in an unusual direction. One major marketplace just cut its selling fee on used and refurbished goods by roughly 60 percent, which is the sort of change that shows up on price tags within weeks rather than years. Meanwhile oil fell more than five percent, freight is loosening, and the biggest sale of the second half of the year has been officially scheduled for October. Translation: buy used electronics now, buy summer gear now, and put anything big and boring on a calendar reminder for autumn.

What’s Hot This Week

Second-hand just got structurally cheaper, and today is day one. As of this morning, one of the largest Western marketplaces has torn up its old tiered commission system and replaced it with flat per-category rates — roughly 7 percent on complete electronics, 12 percent on accessories, and 14 percent on home, sport, and fashion. Buried in that table is the interesting part: for items listed in specific used and refurbished conditions, the rate drops to about 5 percent. For some sellers that is close to a 60 percent cut in what the platform takes. Sellers do not hand savings over instantly, but a fee cut of that size is not something a competitive category absorbs quietly. Over the next four to six weeks, expect refurbished listings on that platform to get visibly cheaper relative to new. If you have been circling a vacuum, a pair of headphones, a camera body, or a laptop, this is the month the maths changed in your favour.

The same rulebook now punishes bad sellers with a surcharge. Alongside the new rates, sellers with a below-average service rating and an elevated return rate get charged extra — and if they haven’t fixed it after four months, another percentage point on top. This is the first time a big marketplace has priced seller quality directly into the fee schedule rather than just burying it in search ranking. As a shopper, the practical read is simple: bad sellers now have a running cost, which means they will either clean up or leave. Give the platform a quarter and the average listing quality should measurably improve.

Shipping labels are being taken over by the platform, too. A new user agreement went live at the end of last week introducing a unified shipping-label system: eligible items must ship on the platform’s own labels, buyer-paid postage goes to the platform rather than the seller, and on free-shipping orders the label cost is simply deducted from the seller. Sellers hate it. For buyers it means one thing worth having — the postage you are quoted at checkout is far more likely to be the postage that actually gets paid, and tracking stops being a mystery link to a carrier you’ve never heard of. On the UK side, the change-of-mind window on car and motorcycle parts is now 30 days with the platform subsidising half the return label. If you’ve been putting off a part you’re not certain fits, that risk just got a lot cheaper.

Oil dropped hard, and freight is about to follow. Crude fell sharply in the last stretch of June — the July light-sweet contract to just over $80 a barrel, down more than 5 percent, and the August Brent contract to around $83.50, down over 4 percent — after geopolitical tension eased. Fuel surcharges are the fastest-moving component of both air and sea freight, so the pass-through is real, if lagged by four to eight weeks. That is the near-term story. The longer-term one is bigger: the world’s largest container line has warned that the global newbuild orderbook now equals about 30 percent of the existing fleet, with most of those ships arriving around 2027. A capacity glut that size means shipping-cost pressure on imported goods should ease through next year rather than tighten. If you were expecting freight-driven price rises to keep pushing up the cost of bulky imports, the curve has flattened.

The autumn sale has a date, which changes how you should shop July. The next flagship discount event of the year has been locked in for October 8. That is fourteen weeks away — close enough to be worth waiting for on big-ticket, non-urgent, non-seasonal items (a television, a robot vacuum, a laptop refresh), and far too far away to matter for anything you’d actually use this summer. The useful mental split: seasonal and consumable, buy now; durable and deferrable, put it on the October list. Also worth knowing, the holiday-season return window has been extended again this year, which quietly makes October purchases lower-risk than they used to be.

Children’s products and battery gear are getting a paperwork layer next week. From July 8, importers of goods regulated by the US consumer-safety authority — children’s items, a chunk of home goods, and most battery-powered products — must file the core data from their certificates electronically through customs at the point of entry, rather than producing it later on request. Combined with the certification deadlines that closed at the end of June, the effect is cumulative: for the rest of the summer, the cheapest unbranded end of these categories will thin out. This is not a reason to panic-buy. It is a reason, if you are buying anything for a child or anything with a rechargeable cell, to prefer a brand that can show you a test report over one that is merely cheap.

The mid-year video-commerce sale ends Friday. The big mid-year event on the largest video-shopping platform runs through July 3, and the final stretch is where the clearance actually happens — overstocked, multi-variant, hard-to-photograph items get dumped. Auction-format livestreams have been the surprise of this event, tripling year on year after the platform trimmed commission for them. If you enjoy that format, the next two days are the peak. If you don’t, the useful signal is that anything still discounted on July 3 is inventory the seller genuinely wants gone.

The discount platforms have stopped buying your attention and started keeping it. One of the two big low-price platforms cut its US advertising spend dramatically in the first five months of the year — down 95 percent on one social network, down about three quarters on two more — while shifting budget towards Pinterest and keeping three quarters of what’s left on one ad network. And yet US monthly active users still grew 21 percent year on year. That combination tells you the land-grab phase is over. Practically: fewer aggressive coupon blasts and referral games, more emphasis on price and delivery speed. The same platform’s move into a major US retailer’s logistics network has cut its per-unit delivery cost by roughly 40 percent, and that is the kind of saving that does reach the shelf. Expect the headline prices to hold or improve even as the gimmicks fade.

Where things ship from now matters more than which app you use. A very large amount of capital is being pointed at local fulfilment right now: over €10 billion committed to upgrading European distribution centres, hours-not-days delivery launched across three Mexican cities, Prime landing in South Africa at under $4 a month, a million-plus drone deliveries logged by one US retailer, and a major electronics chain rebuilding itself around a third-party marketplace, a collectibles category, and AI-assisted shopping. The through-line: the winning purchase is increasingly the one already sitting in a warehouse near you. Before you buy anything cross-border this month, check whether a local-stock version exists — it will usually beat the imported listing on total cost once charges and waiting are counted.

One thing to keep an eye on rather than act on. A proposal is circulating to add tariffs of up to 12.5 percent on imports from around 60 economies, with a lower 10 percent band for a handful. It is a proposal today, not a rule, and proposals of this kind routinely change shape. Don’t restructure your shopping around it. Do note that if it lands, it lands on categories that are already price-sensitive — which is one more argument for buying the durable version of a thing now rather than the disposable version repeatedly later.

Buying Tips

Learn the refurbished vocabulary, because it now has money attached. “Certified refurbished” and “manufacturer refurbished” mean the original maker or an authorised partner tested the unit and stands behind it, usually with a warranty of a year or more. “Seller refurbished” means somebody plugged it in. “Open box” means nobody did anything except open the box. Today’s fee change rewards the top tiers specifically, so the listings that get cheapest fastest should also be the safest ones. Filter on condition before you filter on price.

Check whether the listing ships from local stock. This is the highest-leverage habit of 2026 and it takes ten seconds. Cross-border parcels into Europe now carry a flat charge regardless of value, US direct-mail routes from the discount platforms have closed, and customs data requirements are tightening on both sides. A listing fulfilled from a warehouse in your region avoids all of it. Where the seller offers both, the local option almost always wins on total cost once you count the charge, the wait, and the return path.

Look for the cancel button before you look at the price. Since mid-June, any store selling to EU consumers must provide a visible digital cancellation function reachable in no more than two clicks. It is a two-second test that tells you a great deal about whether a store you’ve never heard of is a real operation. If it isn’t there, that’s your answer.

Buy the consumables with the machine. Filters, film, gaskets, caps, pods, blades. The single most common regret in every category above is discovering three months later that the replacement part is discontinued, back-ordered, or costs 40 percent of the original device. Availability of spares is a better durability signal than any review score.

Don’t chase a coupon that’s being deprecated. The discount platforms are visibly shifting from acquisition spending to margin. The era of stacking four promotions on a $6 item is ending, not because prices are rising but because the theatre around them is being dismantled. Judge the final price, ignore the strikethrough.

Two dates for the diary. July 3 — the mid-year video-commerce event closes, and the last 48 hours are where genuine clearance happens. October 8 — the autumn flagship sale. Anything durable, expensive, and not needed this season belongs on the October list.

What’s Coming Next

Refurbished goes mainstream over the next quarter. A 60 percent cut in platform commission on a category does not stay invisible. The realistic sequence: sellers test higher margins for two or three weeks, competition erodes them, and by mid-August refurbished electronics are priced noticeably below where they sit today. The categories to watch are cordless vacuums, headphones, tablets, camera bodies, and small kitchen appliances — high-value, low-consumable, easy to test. Expect the other marketplaces to respond, because they will have to.

Freight-driven price pressure fades rather than builds. Fuel is down mid-single digits, and the structural picture is a large wave of new container capacity arriving into 2027 against soft demand. Peak-season bookings will still be tight for the next couple of months — that is seasonal, not structural — but the multi-year direction on the shipping component of imported goods is now downward. If you have been holding off on something bulky in the expectation that shipping will make it worse, you can stop.

The cheap end of regulated categories keeps thinning. Battery-powered goods, children’s items, and a slice of home goods now carry electronic certificate filing at the border on top of certification deadlines that already closed. The visible consequence over the summer is fewer no-name listings and slightly higher floor prices in those categories, offset by better average quality. Buy the documented version.

Local warehousing becomes the default, and delivery speed converges. With billions being spent on regional fulfilment, hours-scale delivery expanding into new markets, and one discount platform cutting its per-unit delivery cost by around 40 percent by piggybacking on established logistics, the gap between “ordered from overseas” and “ordered locally” is closing on speed and widening on price. By autumn, the overseas-direct listing will mostly be a worse deal, not a cheaper one.

AI-assisted shopping arrives before trust does. Forecasts put agent-driven purchasing at north of €300 billion in Europe over the coming decade, and a major electronics retailer is already building it into its stack with two of the largest AI companies. But only about four in ten shoppers currently say they’d trust an AI to buy for them — and the split by category is telling: many are comfortable letting software pick electronics, very few will let it pick groceries. Expect the first genuinely useful version of this to be spec-driven comparison shopping, exactly the kind of task where refurbished-versus-new gets confusing.

Demand is cooling, which is a buyer’s tailwind. US manufacturing output was flat last month after four months of growth, and the discount platforms are pulling back on paid acquisition. Softer demand plus easing freight plus a big scheduled sale event equals a market where waiting is cheap and impatience is expensive — on everything except the seasonal items you’d actually use in the next eight weeks.

Bottom Line

Today’s changes are mostly invisible from the product page, which is exactly why they’re worth knowing about. A fee schedule got rewritten in a way that makes second-hand structurally cheaper. Fuel fell and the shipping outlook softened. The big autumn sale got a date. And the paperwork requirements on cheap battery-powered goods tightened one more notch.

The resulting playbook for July is unusually clean. Buy refurbished for anything electronic and durable — the discount is real and it’s about to get better. Buy new, from brands that repair what they sell, for the things you want to still own in 2031. Buy summer gear now, because seasonal markdowns peak mid-season, not after. And put the television, the laptop, and the robot vacuum on a list dated October 8.

The one thing not to do is buy an unbranded rechargeable anything from a seller you can’t identify. That was always slightly risky. As of the end of last week, it’s also a category being actively cleared out.
