Best Things to Buy Right Now — July 17, 2026

Quick take: Three things moved this month, and none of them was a sale. The commission structure that decides which products creators push you got rewritten on July 7. Split-pay at checkout became regulated credit in the UK this month. And Halloween — genuinely, in July — became the biggest single category on the seasonal board. If you only take one idea from this page: the enthusiasm in your feed has a price tag attached, and this week you can finally read it.

Mid-July is the honest part of summer. The big deal events are behind us, the back-to-school stampede has not started, and most of what is on sale is on sale because somebody guessed wrong about the weather. That makes it a good week to buy carefully rather than quickly.

It is also a strange week, because the machinery of online shopping changed underneath everyone without a single price tag moving. Below is what actually happened, what it means for your cart, and twenty specific things worth buying — ten from the big marketplaces, ten direct from brands.

What’s Hot This Week

1. The commission reset — why your feed suddenly looks different

On July 7, a restructured affiliate payout system came into full force across TikTok Shop, and it is the single most consequential shopping story of the month even though it was written for sellers rather than shoppers.

The mechanics, briefly:

  • Baseline commission rates rose 2 to 4 percentage points in fashion, beauty, and home.
  • A Verified Creator tier now grants elevated rates — up to about 20% in some beauty subcategories — but requires passing a content compliance certification.
  • The open affiliate storefront was deprecated for creators under 10,000 followers, who are now funnelled into a separate curated pool that brands must opt into.
  • Eligibility tightened broadly for creators under 50,000 followers.
  • Product listings are now surfaced in the shoppable feed based on add-to-cart signals rather than raw engagement.

The average US affiliate commission rate now sits around 13%, and it has been trending upward, not downward — brands are competing for a shrinking pool of eligible creators.

Here is the part that matters to you. Commission rates are not uniform. They cluster hard by category:

CategoryTypical creator commission
Beauty, health, wellness15–30%
Supplements15–25%
Fashion and apparel10–20%
Home products10–15%
Electronics and tech5–13%

Read that table as a map of persuasion pressure. When a creator recommends a serum, they may be earning five times what they would earn recommending a pair of headphones. That does not make the serum bad. It does mean the volume of beauty and supplement recommendations you see has almost nothing to do with those products being better, and almost everything to do with margin structure. Beauty and supplements carry the fattest commissions because those categories carry the fattest product margins.

The inverse is the genuinely useful signal: electronics sit at the bottom of the commission table at 5–13%. When someone with a real audience enthuses about a $200 gadget, they are working for a fraction of what a $30 serum would pay them. That recommendation is more likely to be actual enthusiasm.

Two more consequences worth knowing:

  • Live shopping is the most expensive format for you. Live commissions routinely run 20–30%, versus 10–15% for standard video. That premium exists because live converts better — the urgency, the countdown, the limited-time bundle. You are paying for the pressure that is being applied to you.
  • The nano-creator purge means fewer, more polished reviewers. The brands that were running 300 small creators at 8% are consolidating to 40 verified creators at 18%. Expect content that is better produced and less candid. The scrappy person filming in their kitchen is being priced out.

None of this is a scandal. Affiliate marketing is how a very large share of social commerce is funded — creator-driven sales account for roughly 60–70% of US GMV on the platform. It just helps enormously to know the number behind the smile.

2. Split-pay became regulated credit — and your checkout may start saying no

Buy-now-pay-later crossed a line this month. UK regulation of BNPL as consumer credit begins in July 2026, with EU rules following in November. Australia and the Gulf states already treat it as credit. Malaysia is rolling out licensing through the year. India runs it inside digital lending rules. Brazil stays light-touch. The US, notably, went the other direction and withdrew its federal rule.

For a shopper, regulation means one concrete thing: affordability checks. The four-payment option that used to be a single tap is becoming an actual credit decision, with the possibility of being declined at checkout on an order you assumed was fine.

At the same time the option is spreading. Affirm is launching in the UK through Stripe from this month, meaning a large slice of British merchants can switch on instalments with no development work. The merchant-side numbers explain the enthusiasm: Stripe merchants adding Affirm in North America saw revenue rise roughly 14%, and on orders of $250 and above, conversion improved about 21%.

That last figure is the one to sit with. A 21% conversion lift on expensive orders does not mean split-pay makes expensive things cheaper. It means splitting the number makes people say yes to things they were about to decline. The mechanism is anchoring — you evaluate “$62 every two weeks” instead of “$249,” and $62 feels survivable in a way $249 did not.

Use it deliberately or not at all:

  • Good use: a genuinely needed, higher-ticket, long-lifespan item — a mattress, a laptop, a winter coat — where you have the money but prefer the cash-flow smoothing, and the plan is truly interest-free.
  • Bad use: anything you found in a feed in the last ten minutes. If the instalment plan is what made the purchase feel possible, that is the purchase telling you something.
  • New this month, UK shoppers: assume the check is real. If you are planning a large purchase and split-pay is part of the plan, do not leave it until the moment of checkout.

Elsewhere in the world, the payment layer is a genuine reason certain things are cheaper. In emerging markets, up to 66% of carts are abandoned when instalment options are missing. In South Africa, split-pay now accounts for about 67% of alternative payment method transactions, with instalment order values running 60% to 330% higher than card orders. In Brazil, instalments cover roughly 58% of retail transactions, and Pix — the instant-payment rail now reaching 177 million people, about 83% of the population — handles about 51% of all payments by volume. If you are shopping cross-border from one of these markets, the local payment method is almost always the cheaper and more reliable path than an international card.

3. Summerween is real, and it is the biggest thing on the seasonal board

Halloween has stopped being a night. Recent survey work puts it plainly: 57% of US adults now say Halloween is a full season rather than a single day, and nearly 60% of Gen Z are aware of or have participated in “Summerween” — celebrating spooky season in the middle of July and August.

The supply side is fully committed. Major candy portfolios are shipping the complete Halloween lineup nationwide starting in July. One heritage ornament maker reports its first Halloween order of 2026 was placed on April 23, and that Halloween category traffic to its site rose 155% from June to July. On the handmade marketplaces, demand for Halloween digital printables and planners jumped about 32% this month.

Retailers have opened early collections across the board — the elevated home-decor chains, the big flat-pack stores, the craft chains. The yard-statement pieces, including the giant skeleton that reliably sells out, typically appear in early August.

Now the honest part, because this is a shopping guide and not a press release. Early Summerween stock is not discounted. You are trading price for selection. That gives you a clean decision rule:

  • Buy now if the item is one that genuinely sells out — the collectible-adjacent decor, the licensed pieces, the giant yard figures, anything with a fandom attached.
  • Wait if it is generic seasonal filler — orange string lights, plain pumpkins, basic costume components. Those will be discounted in October and demolished on November 1.
  • Buy now regardless if it is something you will use outside of October anyway. A pumpkin-shaped lamp is a lamp. A black cat doormat is a doormat. A clove-and-pumpkin candle is a candle you will burn through September, October, and November.

One more note on where this money is going. Discovery for this trend is overwhelmingly social — among Gen Z, 53% spot Halloween trends on TikTok, 49% on YouTube, 48% on Instagram — but the transaction still happens in physical retail, with 43% buying at large-format stores and 41% at grocery. Translation: the feed sets your list, the store closes the sale, and the store is usually cheaper for the boring stuff.

4. Secondhand stopped being a compromise

The quietest structural shift of 2026 is that resale became a default rather than a fallback.

US total resale volume reaches roughly $123.5 billion in 2026, up 6.2%, with e-commerce accounting for 75% of it. There will be about 45.2 million US online fashion resale buyers this year — nearly 20% of all digital buyers — spending an average of $380 each on those platforms. Globally, resale apparel is on track for around $288 billion, up 12.5%, after growing 82% between 2021 and 2025.

Two behavioural numbers tell the real story. Consumers now plan to spend 34% of their apparel budget secondhand over the next twelve months, rising to 46% among Gen Z and millennials. And 46% say that if they can find an item secondhand, they will not buy it new at all.

What changed is friction, not virtue. 48% of shoppers now say search, personalization, and discovery make buying secondhand as easy as buying new — AI is doing the merchandising work that unique, one-off inventory always demanded. Roughly 39% of younger shoppers have bought secondhand apparel inside a social commerce feed rather than on a dedicated marketplace.

And it is not just clothes. Apparel is only about a quarter of total recommerce; the other three quarters are electronics, furniture, sporting goods, auto parts, and collectibles — categories with less competition and often better margins for the buyer. Footwear resale in particular grew about 80% year over year across branded programs in 2025.

Practical version: before you buy anything durable and branded at full price this week — shoes, outerwear, a camera, a phone, a bag, a piece of furniture — spend ninety seconds checking the resale price. The gap is frequently 40–60%, and on electronics the certified-refurbished channel now comes with warranties that make the risk calculation genuinely boring.

Platform & Category Watch

Amazon. The electronics bestseller board is doing what it does every July: last-generation and value-tier hardware gets quietly marked down while attention is elsewhere. Budget 4K sets, previous-step audio, and tracking accessories are all sitting below list. This is a good window for the boring purchase you keep postponing.

TikTok Shop. The commission reset is the whole story. Expect the beauty and supplement share of your feed to grow, not shrink, and expect production quality to rise while candour falls. On the ranking side, medical-aesthetic skincare and functional foods dominate the US board, with a PDRN collagen eye patch holding the top slot at an estimated $6.5–8 million weekly GMV and an electrolyte powder in second. Worth noting that the international boards look nothing alike — Indonesia’s top seller is a baby spa gift set at an estimated $27–34 million, an order of magnitude above the US leader, and the UK board is led by makeup sets and probiotic gummies. Different countries, entirely different appetites.

Home Depot, Michaels, IKEA, Anthropologie. The Summerween beneficiaries. Early collections are live at all four, priced at full retail, with the scarcity concentrated in statement pieces rather than consumables.

Back Market, eBay, ThredUp, Poshmark, Whatnot. The recommerce layer is now a legitimate first stop rather than a bargain-hunting detour. Live auction formats have spread across the resale marketplaces, and they are excellent for collectibles and terrible for impulse control — the same urgency premium that makes live shopping expensive on the social platforms applies here.

Shopify and independent stores. Duty-inclusive pricing continues to spread, which is unambiguously good for cross-border shoppers: you see the landed cost at checkout instead of getting a courier invoice ten days later. The independent-brand pitch this month is straightforward — buying direct removes the affiliate commission layer entirely. On a beauty product carrying a 25% creator commission, that is not a rounding error.

Buying Tips for This Week

1. Check the commission before you check the reviews. When something is everywhere in your feed, ask which category it sits in. Beauty and supplements at 15–30% will always be over-represented relative to their actual quality. Electronics at 5–13% will always be under-represented. Adjust your scepticism accordingly, in both directions.

2. Treat live shopping as the most expensive format. Live commissions run 20–30% against 10–15% for standard video. That gap is funded by urgency — the countdown, the “only forty left,” the bundle that expires when the stream ends. If you want the item, leave the stream, find it elsewhere, and buy it tomorrow. If you still want it tomorrow, it was real.

3. UK shoppers: assume the affordability check is live. Split-pay is regulated consumer credit from this month. Do not build a large purchase around an instalment plan you have not confirmed you qualify for, and be aware that these arrangements can now interact with your credit file in ways they previously did not.

4. Run the resale check before every durable purchase. Ninety seconds. Shoes, outerwear, cameras, phones, bags, furniture. Nearly half of consumers now say they will not buy new if they can find it secondhand, and the discovery tools have improved to the point that this is no longer a scavenger hunt. Certified refurbished electronics come with warranties.

5. Split your Summerween list into two piles. Statement and collectible pieces — buy now, they sell out. Generic seasonal filler — wait, it will be discounted in October and given away on November 1. Anything that functions outside the holiday, like a lamp or a candle or a doormat, buy now and stop thinking about it.

6. Buy direct where the commission is highest. For beauty, supplements, and apparel, the brand’s own store is frequently the better price precisely because the marketplace version has an affiliate payout baked into it. This is the opposite of the usual instinct, and it is worth checking.

7. Shopping cross-border? Use the local payment rail. Where instant-payment systems and instalment options exist locally, they clear faster, fail less, and cost less than international cards. Where checkout shows duty-inclusive pricing, take it — the alternative is a courier invoice arriving after the parcel.

8. Mid-July markdowns on summer apparel are the honest kind. Nobody is clearing linen in July as a trick. Demand genuinely falls off. This is one of the few weeks where the discount reflects the calendar rather than a merchandising strategy.

What’s Coming Next

A dated view of the next few months, so you can decide what to buy now and what to hold.

  • Late July — Full Halloween retail assortments land nationwide. Selection peaks; prices do not move. Candy portfolios are already shipping.
  • Early August — The giant yard figures and large statement decor hit stores. Historically the fastest sell-out of the season. Back-to-school pricing begins in earnest.
  • Mid-August — Back-to-school peak. Electronics is the largest line item for both K-12 and college households, and it is the category where waiting costs you the most.
  • Late August into September — Autumn apparel arrives, summer clearance bottoms out. Best week of the year for swimwear, linen, sandals, and outdoor furniture if you can store it.
  • September — Custom framing, personalization, and made-to-order turnaround times lengthen sharply. Anything bespoke for the holidays should be ordered by month end.
  • October — Generic Halloween decor discounts begin around the 20th. November 1 is a 50–75% clearance event on everything seasonal that did not sell.
  • November — EU rules bring split-pay under consumer credit supervision across the bloc, matching what the UK started this month. If you shop European stores with instalments, expect the same affordability checks.

The through-line: anything scarce, buy early; anything generic, buy late. July rewards patience on commodities and punishes it on anything with a fandom attached.

Bottom Line

Nothing important got cheaper this week. What changed is the machinery — who gets paid to recommend things to you, whether you are allowed to split the bill, and how early the seasonal calendar now starts.

The commission table is the most useful thing on this page. Once you know that a beauty recommendation pays a creator five times what an electronics recommendation pays, the shape of your feed stops looking like consensus and starts looking like arithmetic. That is not cynicism; it is just the correct denominator.

Everything else follows from it. Buy direct where commissions are fattest. Buy refurbished where the warranty makes risk boring. Buy the scarce Halloween thing in July and the generic Halloween thing in November. Leave the live stream before you buy, and see whether you still want it in the morning.

And if the instalment plan is the only reason a purchase feels possible — that is not a payment option. That is a warning.

Prices and availability shift constantly. Verify before you buy.
