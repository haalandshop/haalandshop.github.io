Best Things to Buy Right Now — May 22, 2026

Quick take: Friday, the day the long weekend starts, twenty days before the tournament opens and thirty-two before the June sale reaches full volume. Yesterday’s piece argued that a price is the only unconditional number on a product page and should be read as a specification in its own right. That is true, and it has a limitation that swallows most of its usefulness: a price only means anything against the other prices you happen to be looking at, and you did not choose those. Somebody else did. A retailer’s comparison widget, a roundup article, a forum thread, the three tabs you have open. The set is the hidden input to every purchase decision, and inside almost every set of three there is one option that never wins, is not meant to win, and is nevertheless doing the most work in the room. It makes one of its neighbours look generous. Delete it and your ranking of the remaining two can flip completely, which is a strange property for something you were never going to buy. This piece is about that third option: where it comes from, why it concentrates in categories with exactly three serious brands, how to identify the one currently sitting on your own shortlist, and what your decision looks like on the other side of removing it. Twenty picks, sorted by what the comparison set around each one is doing.

What’s Hot This Week

The Friday shortlist rush is real and it is measurable

The day before a long weekend is when the largest number of people build their first shortlist for a purchase they intend to make within the month. They are not buying yet. They are opening tabs, and the tabs they open on a Friday afternoon are the set they will still be choosing from on Tuesday, largely unrevised. Whatever assembled that set has an outsized claim on the outcome, because sets are sticky in a way that individual opinions are not. People revise their view of a product readily. They almost never go back and question the roster.

Three-brand categories are quietly getting a fourth entrant

Across several of the categories below, a long-stable trio has acquired a newcomer in the last two quarters. This matters more than it sounds. A three-brand category has a specific and well-documented pathology, which the rest of this piece is about. A four-brand category behaves differently and mostly better, because the fourth entrant breaks the geometry that makes the middle option so influential. If a category you follow has just gained a credible fourth name, the comparison sets you relied on last year are no longer arranged the same way.

Comparison widgets are being rebuilt ahead of the June sale

Every large platform is currently reshuffling the machinery that decides which products appear next to which. This happens annually in the run-up to the summer event, and it means the “similar items” strip you saw in April is not the strip you are seeing today. The rearrangement is not editorial and nobody reviewed it. It is a ranking output, and this is the exact window in which it is least stable and most likely to place a genuinely odd third option beside the two you actually care about.

The Third Option You Never Choose

Start with the finding, because it is old, robust and still slightly hard to believe. Give somebody a choice between two products where each beats the other on a different dimension — one is cheaper, one is better — and preferences split. Roughly half go each way, depending on the person. Now add a third option that is clearly worse than one of the originals and not clearly worse than the other. Nobody picks the new option. Its share is close to zero, exactly as you would expect. But the split between the original two moves, sometimes by twenty points or more, always in favour of the one that clearly beats the newcomer.

Nothing about either original product changed. No new information about them entered the room. The only thing that changed is that one of them now has something to obviously beat, and being obviously better than a visible neighbour is apparently a more persuasive property than being better in the abstract. Comparison is easy and evaluation is hard, so people quietly substitute the first for the second. Where a clean comparison is available, it gets made, and the result of that comparison then stands in for a judgement about the whole field.

This is why the third option is worth your attention even though you will never buy it. It is not competing with you for the product. It is competing for the shape of your reasoning. And unlike an advertisement, which announces itself and triggers the appropriate scepticism, the third option arrives disguised as neutral context — just another product, listed for your convenience, doing nothing more suspicious than existing.

Two things follow that are worth holding onto. First, the effect does not require intent. It works identically whether a pricing committee placed the option deliberately or an algorithm happened to surface a poorly-matched item. The mechanism is in the geometry of the set, not in anybody’s motive. Second, it does not require you to be gullible. It survives expertise, it survives knowing about it, and it survives being told which item is the decoy. The only reliable defence is structural: change the set.

It is worth being precise about why the substitution happens, because the usual explanation — that people are lazy — is both unkind and wrong. Evaluating a product in absolute terms requires a scale, and for most purchases you do not have one. You cannot say what a binocular is worth in the abstract; you have no unit for it. What you can say, instantly and with real confidence, is that this one is better than that one. Relative judgements are cheap and reliable and absolute judgements are expensive and shaky, so the mind uses the tool that works. The problem is not the tool. The problem is that the tool takes whatever is in front of it as the reference class, and the reference class was supplied by someone else.

Where Decoys Actually Come From

There are four sources, and they are worth separating because they behave differently once you spot them.

The deliberate in-range decoy. A brand with three models where the middle one is priced close to the top model while offering noticeably less. Nobody at the company expects to sell many. It exists so that the top model reads as good value rather than as expensive, and the ordinary way this happens is not cynicism but arithmetic: somebody modelled the range and discovered that adding a rung shifted the mix upward. These are the rarest of the four and the easiest to spot, because a deliberate decoy has to be visible to work, which means it sits right where you will see it.

The legacy SKU. Last year’s model, still listed, still carrying most of its old price because discounting it aggressively would undercut the new one. This is the most common decoy in electronics and the least deliberate. Nobody designed it to distort anything; it is simply the residue of a product cycle. But its effect on the set is identical, and it is more misleading than the deliberate kind because the specification difference is often genuinely small and the price difference genuinely large.

The permanent third name. Every mature category has a brand that appears in every roundup, wins nothing, and never quite goes away. It is usually a company with real distribution, an adequate product and no particular strength — which is precisely the profile that makes a good decoy. It is credible enough to belong in the set and weak enough to lose cleanly, and its main function in the ecosystem is to be the thing the winner beats.

The algorithmic accident. A platform’s related-items module assembles a set from purchase co-occurrence and category tags, with no view whatsoever about whether the items are comparable. This produces decoys constantly and at random. It also produces the strangest ones — an item three price tiers away, or a different product class entirely — and because it is regenerated frequently, the same product page can present a different distortion on Tuesday than it did on Friday.

The practical distinction: the first three are stable and you can learn them once per category. The fourth is noise, and the correct response to it is not to learn it but to refuse to accept any set you did not assemble yourself.

There is also a fifth source that deserves mention even though it is not a decoy in the technical sense, because it produces the same symptom. Roundup articles are usually structured around a fixed number of slots — best overall, best budget, best premium — and those slots have to be filled whether or not the category contains a product that genuinely belongs in each. A category with two good options and a slot for a third will get a third, and the item promoted into that slot acquires an authority it did not earn. When you find yourself considering something largely because a publication awarded it a category, check whether the category was discovered in the products or imposed on them beforehand.

Why Three Brands Is the Magic Number

The decoy effect needs a very specific arrangement to work, and understanding that arrangement is most of the defence. You need at least three options, or there is nothing to be dominated by. You need the two serious contenders to be genuinely close, or one wins outright and the third is irrelevant. And you need the third option to be clearly worse than one contender while being ambiguous against the other — if it is worse than both, it drops out of consideration entirely and changes nothing.

That is a narrow set of conditions, and a category with exactly three established brands satisfies all of them almost by default. The two leaders are close because if they were not, the category would have consolidated to one. The third is credible enough to be in the conversation but behind on something, because otherwise it would be a leader. And the whole structure is stable over years, which means the same distortion operates on every buyer entering the category, in the same direction, for a long time. The third brand may not enjoy this arrangement, but it has a viable business inside it, and one of the two leaders is quietly getting a permanent advantage from its existence.

You can predict which leader benefits. It is the one the third brand most closely resembles — same price band, same feature philosophy, same customer — because resemblance is what makes the domination legible. The leader that differs from the third brand on several dimensions gains nothing, since no clean comparison is available. This is why the third-place brand in a category is often positioned suspiciously close to the number two rather than trying to differentiate, and why number two tends to have a market share that looks a little better than its products alone would explain.

What happens in categories with two brands or thirty

Both extremes are safer, for opposite reasons. A two-brand category cannot produce the effect at all; you are forced into a direct comparison and whatever you decide is at least your own. This is why duopoly categories tend to produce buyers with strong, well-reasoned and quite stubborn opinions.

A category with thirty credible brands is also relatively safe, but only because no stable set of three ever forms. The distortion is still present in any given moment — whatever three items you happen to be looking at will do their geometry on you — but it changes composition constantly, so it does not accumulate into a systematic bias in favour of any one brand. The cost is a different problem: with thirty options, most buyers do not evaluate at all. They pick on a proxy, usually reviews or price, and a proxy is its own distortion. Three brands corrupts your comparison. Thirty prevents you from making one.

The Accidental Decoy Is More Common Than the Deliberate One

It is tempting to read all of this as manipulation and to look for someone to blame, and that instinct will make you worse at this rather than better. Most decoys are accidents. The legacy SKU that nobody delisted, the discontinued colourway still in the grid, the algorithm pairing a mid-range item with a flagship because they share a tag — these vastly outnumber the arrangements any pricing committee designed.

This matters for a practical reason. If you assume the decoy is deliberate, you will look for it in the brand’s range, where the deliberate ones live. But most of the sets you actually encounter were not assembled by a brand at all. They were assembled by a retailer’s ranking function, a publisher’s roundup, or your own sequence of tabs. Looking for intent in a set that had no author means you will miss the distortion entirely while feeling appropriately vigilant, which is the worst of both outcomes.

There is a second reason accidental decoys deserve more of your attention: they are more misleading than deliberate ones. A deliberate decoy has to be plausible, so it is usually a real product with a coherent position. An accidental decoy has no such constraint. A related-items module will cheerfully place a two-hundred-unit item beside an eight-hundred-unit one, and the resulting distortion is much larger than anything a pricing team would risk, because a pricing team knows an implausible decoy damages the brand. The algorithm has no such worry.

The useful reframing: stop asking who benefits and start asking who assembled this. The answer is often “nothing did, this set is an artefact”, and that answer is actionable in a way that suspicion is not. An artefact can simply be discarded.

The Delete-One Test

Here is the whole method, and it takes about a minute.

Write down your shortlist. If it has three items, you are in the danger zone and this test is worth running properly. Identify the option you are least likely to buy — not the worst option, the one you are least likely to actually choose. Those are usually the same thing and when they are not, that is informative on its own. Now cross it out and ask a single question: between the two that remain, which do I prefer?

Most of the time the answer is the same as before and you have lost sixty seconds. But a meaningful fraction of the time the answer changes, and when it does, something specific has happened. The option you were leaning toward was drawing its appeal from the comparison with the item you just deleted rather than from its own properties. It was winning a race against a competitor you were never going to buy. That is not a reason to buy something, and the two-item version of your shortlist is closer to the truth.

The test has a diagnostic variant that is faster still. Look at your shortlist and ask whether any item is clearly beaten by another item on every dimension you care about. If yes, that item is doing decoy work whether or not anyone intended it to, and the item beating it is receiving an unearned boost. You do not need to run the full comparison; you just need to discount your enthusiasm for the beneficiary and re-examine the third item on its own merits, which is the one that never got a clean look.

The test is also worth running in the other direction when your shortlist has grown past three, which happens in crowded categories. Instead of deleting the weakest, delete everything except the two you keep returning to, then add back one item at a time and note whether your ranking of the original two ever moves. If it does, the item you just added is exerting influence disproportionate to your interest in buying it, and you have found the distortion by construction rather than by intuition. This takes longer than the basic version and it is the right tool when you have eight tabs open and no idea why you keep drifting toward one of them.

One caution, because this test is easy to over-apply. A dominated option is not always a decoy. Sometimes it is a genuinely worse product that belongs in the set for reference, or a product optimised for a constraint you do not have — a lighter tool that loses on capability because it is meant to be carried all day. Domination on your dimensions is not domination on all dimensions. Before you discard it, check whether the dimension it wins on is one you have simply not thought to care about. Occasionally that check is where the actual purchase comes from.

The Compromise Effect and the Middle You Did Not Choose

There is a second and related distortion that arrives with the same geometry and points somewhere different, and confusing them will cost you money.

The decoy effect makes an option look better by giving it something to beat. The compromise effect makes the middle option look better simply for being in the middle. Present three items at ascending prices and the middle one gains share regardless of what it is, because it reads as the sensible choice — not cheap enough to be risky, not dear enough to be indulgent. It requires no justification to yourself or anyone else. That safety is doing the persuading, and it is entirely a property of position rather than of the object.

This is why the two effects need separating. The decoy tells you to look harder at the option that is winning a comparison, and specifically to ask whether it would still be winning against a different opponent. The compromise effect tells you to look harder at the option that is not winning any comparison at all but is nonetheless in your basket, because you chose it for its coordinates rather than its qualities. In a three-item range these two forces frequently push toward the same middle rung from opposite directions, and the rung then feels overwhelmingly correct while having accumulated no actual evidence.

The counter is to make yourself state, in a sentence, what the middle option does better than the cheap one, and what you would be giving up against the dear one. If both sentences come out concrete, the middle is genuinely correct and you should buy it — middles often are correct, which is precisely why the effect is so hard to see. If either sentence comes out as a vague appeal to balance or a claim about being the sweet spot, you have chosen a position on a list rather than a product, and the list was written by someone with a preference about where you land.

When the Decoy Is the Right Buy

Now the inversion, which is where most of the money is.

A decoy is an option positioned to lose a comparison. Nothing about that requires it to be a bad object. It requires it to be clearly worse on the dimensions the comparison happens to use, and those dimensions are chosen by whoever built the set — a reviewer with a test protocol, a retailer with filterable attributes, a spec grid with columns. Dimensions that resist tabulation do not appear. Feel, serviceability, how a thing ages, whether the company answers the phone, whether parts exist in five years.

So the systematic opportunity is this: a product that loses on tabulated dimensions and wins on untabulated ones is priced as a loser and performs as a winner. It is a standing discount available to anyone whose priorities differ from the comparison grid’s. Two of the picks above are exactly this case, and the pattern generalises across categories with dull, mature, well-instrumented products, which is where spec grids are most complete and therefore most confidently wrong.

The screen is narrow but reliable. Does the item lose consistently on the same one or two headline figures? Are those figures ones you can honestly say affect your use? Does it win, in owner reports rather than reviews, on longevity, repair or support? And is the price gap larger than the specification gap justifies? When all four hold, the third option is not a decoy for you. It is a discount, created by everyone else’s shortlist, funded by a comparison you have opted out of.

The failure mode to avoid: this reasoning is seductive and can justify almost any purchase if applied loosely. The discipline is the second question. If the figure the item loses on is one that genuinely matters for your use — as with the headlamp above, where runtime is not a tabulation artefact but the entire point — then the item is not underrated. It is just behind.

Where This Read Breaks Down

Four limits, stated plainly, because a method you cannot bound is a method you will misapply.

It requires a set of comparable things. In categories where every product is configured differently — made to order, bespoke, or with genuinely incompatible feature sets — no stable comparison forms and there is nothing to analyse. Two of the picks above sit in exactly that space, and the correct response is not to hunt for a hidden decoy but to accept that the comparison has to be made against your own requirements instead.

Small sets can produce false positives. With three items you will almost always find one that is dominated, because with three items on two or three dimensions, domination is common by chance alone. Finding a dominated option is weak evidence of anything. The signal is a dominated option that is stable across many different sets — the same brand losing the same way in every roundup for years is a structural position. A single unfavourable arrangement on one product page is noise.

Knowing about it does not disarm it. This is the uncomfortable one. The effect persists in people who study it, in people warned about it immediately beforehand, and in people who correctly identify which item is the decoy. Insight is not a defence. Only changing the set is a defence, which is why the delete-one test is a physical act of rewriting the list rather than a mental note to be careful.

Sometimes the set is correct. A retailer’s three-item comparison is often three-item because the category genuinely has three serious options, and the one that loses is genuinely the worst. Treating every set as a manipulation will push you into contrarian purchases that are contrarian and also wrong. The test tells you whether your preference is stable under deletion. If it is, the set was fine and you should buy what you were going to buy, with more confidence than you had before.

Cross-Border News, Policy and Logistics

Several developments this week bear directly on how comparison sets are constructed and priced across borders.

Marketplace comparison modules are under fresh regulatory attention in Europe. Scrutiny of how platforms select and rank items in “similar products” and “frequently compared” placements is intensifying, with the core question being whether such placements constitute editorial recommendation or neutral output. The distinction matters commercially because a recommendation carries disclosure obligations that an index does not. Sellers who have optimised toward appearing in these modules should expect the modules themselves to change composition in the coming quarters, which will reshuffle a lot of long-stable comparison sets.

Duty and threshold changes continue to fragment cross-border pricing. The practical effect for anyone comparing across regional storefronts is that identical items increasingly carry differences that are entirely regulatory rather than commercial. This corrupts comparison sets in a specific way worth naming: a product that looks like a decoy on a regional site may simply be carrying a different landed-cost structure. Before concluding a price gap says something about positioning, confirm both items clear customs the same way.

Extended producer responsibility filings are pushing small brands off some marketplaces. As packaging and electronics registration obligations extend to more categories and more member states, the compliance overhead is landing hardest on the smallest sellers. The visible consequence in comparison sets is thinning: categories that had a long tail of small European sellers are losing it, which mechanically increases the share of three-brand arrangements. Fewer options is not a neutral change when the number of options is what determines whether the geometry operates.

Freight is in its pre-peak positioning phase. Rates and capacity on the main eastbound lanes are firming as shippers begin building for the autumn peak, roughly on the normal schedule. For buyers, the read-through is that items shipping direct from origin will see their landed prices drift upward through the summer while domestically warehoused stock holds, so the gap between a direct listing and a local one will widen over the next two months. Where a comparison set mixes both, that gap will move for reasons that have nothing to do with the products.

Amazon, Shopify and TikTok: Where the Breakouts Are Forming

Amazon. The comparison widget rebuild noted at the top is the story here. Sets are being reassembled ahead of the summer event, which means two things for buyers: the strips are less stable than usual and worth trusting less than usual, and products that have been quietly benefiting from a favourable neighbour for a year are about to lose that advantage. If a product’s ranking seems to be softening without any change to the product, look at what is sitting next to it now versus in March. Breakouts in the next fortnight will disproportionately be items that got a better neighbour rather than items that got better.

Shopify. The direct channel is moving in the opposite direction, toward brands publishing their own comparison grids as a standard part of the product page. This is a genuine improvement in transparency and a genuine consolidation of control, and both halves are real. The brands doing it well are including at least one column where they lose to their own cheaper model, which is a credible signal. Where the grid contains no such column, the grid is a routing device. The breakout pattern to watch is short ranges — two or three models, clearly differentiated, comparison table published — which convert notably better than deep ranges right now because they are easy to reason about.

TikTok. Content-led discovery mostly destroys comparison sets rather than distorting them, which is a different problem. A product arrives alone, with no neighbours, and the only reference point is the creator’s enthusiasm. That removes the decoy effect and replaces it with something worse: no comparison at all. The counter-move for buyers is mechanical and it is the single highest-value habit in this piece. When a product reaches you through content, build a set before you buy — find two genuine alternatives yourself and put them side by side. Products that survive that step are the ones that become durable sellers rather than one-week spikes, and you can watch the distinction play out in the data: the items still moving four weeks after their viral moment are almost always the ones that hold up in a set.

Category Notes

Optics is the clearest three-brand category in this piece and the one where the structural discount is largest. Two dominant names, a credible third, and a set of tabulated figures — magnification, objective, field of view — that capture perhaps half of what determines whether you enjoy the instrument. The untabulated half is where the third brand lives.

Hand tools and garden tools are structurally similar and behave differently because the buyers are different. Tool buyers overweight brand heritage and underweight parts availability, which creates the Topeak pattern across the whole sector: the perennial second recommendation frequently has the best long-run ownership economics and the worst roundup placement.

Outdoor clothing is currently the most seasonally distorted category on the list. Nearly everything in it is in the wrong half of its cycle in late May, which is why two of the shell and layering picks are Wait despite being structurally clean comparisons. A good comparison at a bad moment is still a bad moment.

Coffee and kitchen run the shortest product cycles of anything here, which makes the legacy-SKU decoy unusually common and unusually short-lived. Both directions are available: sometimes the old model is the value, sometimes it is there to sell the new one. The Espro and Cristel entries are the two poles.

Computer hardware deserves a note as the category where the accidental decoy is most concentrated and least examined. Product cycles are short, superseded parts stay listed for years, and the specification grid is unusually complete — which means comparisons feel rigorous while being assembled almost entirely from stale inventory. The heuristic that works here is to date every item on your shortlist before comparing anything. A set containing parts from three different release windows is not a comparison of products, it is a comparison of depreciation schedules, and the item that looks like poor value is often just the one that has not had time to fall yet.

The categories where shortlists never form

Worth naming the exception explicitly. In made-to-order, bespoke and heavily configured categories, no comparison set exists because no two objects are the same. Everything in this piece is inapplicable there, and buyers in those categories consistently report both higher satisfaction and higher regret variance — they are choosing without a reference, which removes the distortion and also removes the safety net. The substitute discipline is to compare against your own current object and to write down, before ordering, the specific deficiency you are fixing. If you cannot name it, the absence of a comparison set is not protecting you from anything.

Bottom line

You have been treating your shortlist as the input to a decision when it is most of the decision already made. The set arrives before the reasoning does, it usually arrives from a source with no view about your requirements, and inside every set of three there is an option quietly doing work you did not ask for. It is not there to be bought. It is there — sometimes by design, more often by accident, occasionally by nothing at all — to make one of its neighbours look like the obvious answer. Cross it out and ask the question again. If your preference holds, buy with more confidence than you had. If it flips, you just avoided paying for a comparison instead of a product. And when you find the item that loses every roundup on figures you do not care about while winning on the ones nobody tabulates, understand what you are looking at: not a weak product, but a discount created by everybody else’s shortlist and left on the table for anyone willing to build their own. On the Friday that starts the long weekend, with twenty days to the tournament and a month to the sale, building your own set is the highest-return sixty seconds available to you.
