Best Things to Buy Right Now — February 20, 2027

Quick take: Yesterday’s piece was about the denominator nobody checks, and the arithmetic still holds: a price is a fraction, the page only shows the top of it, and the bottom — how many times you will actually use the thing — is invisible, unadvertised, and almost never estimated before checkout. What makes this particular week unusual is that the denominator is not hidden by neglect but by a calendar collision. Three separate clocks all strike at once. China’s Lunar New Year fell on February 6, and the two-week production shutdown that follows it ends right about now, which means the cross-border shelves are reloading with restocked bestsellers and a wave of new SKUs at once. Valentine’s Day, a week ago, has left behind a clearance shelf of dated and themed occasion goods whose value expired on the fourteenth. Winter is ending, and the season-end clearance on cold-weather and snow categories is at its steepest and its most mispriced. And the New Year’s resolution purchases — the treadmill, the studio bike, the standing desk, the language course bundled with a gadget — are now hitting the resale channels in volume, because the version of you who bought them in January has, statistically, stopped showing up. This is the single best week of the year to buy a durable object with a great denominator, and the single worst week to buy an object that depended on a version of yourself who does not exist. The same search results contain both. Sixty seconds and one division tells you which is which.

What’s Hot This Week

Four things are moving at once, and the common thread is that each of them is a deadline disguised as a trend. A deadline forces a date onto a purchase, and a date quietly rewrites the denominator to one.

The post-Lunar-New-Year supply restart is reloading the shelves this week

The Lunar New Year shutdown is the largest recurring pause in global cross-border supply, and its effects arrive on a delay. Manufacturing restarts around the sixth, but restocking a marketplace takes the better part of a fortnight: containers leave Shenzhen and Yiwu, clear customs, and hit FBA and overseas warehouses roughly two weeks later. That lag means the week of February 20 is when the reload actually lands. Two consequences follow. First, the bestsellers that were thin or sold out through late January and early February are back in depth, and the restock often arrives at the pre-holiday price rather than the scarcity price. Second, and more interestingly, this is when the new-SKU wave appears — the products Chinese factories tooled up for during the shutdown and are now shipping for the first time. For a buyer, the practical move is to separate the two: restocked staples are a “buy the thing you already wanted” opportunity, while brand-new SKUs are a “wait and let the review base form” situation, because a product three weeks off a container has no track record yet.

Valentine’s Day left a clearance shelf of pure occasion goods

The fourteenth is now six days gone, and the dated merchandise — the themed apparel, the printed mugs, the rose-red everything, the gift configurations that only make sense as a gesture on a specific date — has no residual demand whatsoever. This is the cleanest denominator lesson of the month, because the clearance is real and the product is genuinely not worth the clearance price to anyone who is not performing the gesture today. The instructive contrast is the gift that is also a tool: a good pen, a real knife, a quality flask. Those clear too, because they were merchandised as gifts, but their denominator is in the hundreds and the discount is free money for a buyer who ignores the occasion framing. The Valentine clearance separates the memento from the equipment more sharply than any other event in the first quarter, and almost nobody reads it that way.

Winter is hitting its deepest, most mispriced discount

Season-end clearance on cold-weather and snow categories peaks in the last two weeks of February, and it is mispriced in a specific direction. The clearance is deep on the things you should not buy — dated winter fashion, single-purpose holiday decor — and shallow on the things you should. Winter sports hardware, traction devices, thermal layers built to last, heated gear with real service lives: these clear, but the discount is modest, because the buyers for them are year-round and the sellers know it. The trap is symmetrical to the Valentine trap. People see “winter sale” and assume everything in the aisle is a steal, then either overpay for the durable hardware (it was never going to be cheap) or buy the disposable fashion (it was never going to be used). The correct read: the season-end aisle contains one good purchase and nine bad ones, and they are priced close enough to fool a quick glance.

The resolution-equipment resale flood has arrived

This is the quietest and most profitable signal of the week. New Year fitness and productivity equipment — bikes, treadmills, adjustable dumbbells, standing desks, language gadgets, art supplies, instruments — was bought in a January surge of intent. By late February, a predictable fraction of it is being listed, and the listings are written by people who have already admitted to themselves the thing was a mistake. That admission is reflected in the price: these are durable goods, often barely used, listed at a third to a half of retail by sellers with zero marketing budget and maximum motivation to be rid of them. For a buyer, this is the annual opening of a secondary market in excellent-denominator objects, sold by owners who have personally verified the denominator is one. The only risk is condition, and for bulky items the logistics of resale often mean the seller is local, which removes the cross-border return problem entirely.

The Denominator Nobody Checks

Ask someone why they bought something and they will tell you what it cost, what it replaced, or what it does. Ask them how many times they used the last three comparable things they bought and the answer is almost always a pause. That pause is the entire subject of this piece. We have a rich, well-developed vocabulary for evaluating the top of the fraction — discount depth, price history, competitive comparison, total landed cost, whether the coupon stacks — and essentially no vocabulary at all for the bottom of it.

This is not a moral point about consumption. It is an arithmetic one, and the arithmetic is unusually lopsided. Price differences between competing products in the same category are typically bounded: the sensible options cluster within a factor of two or three of each other, and shopping hard might save you thirty percent. Utilisation differences are unbounded. Two objects at identical prices can differ by three orders of magnitude in how often they get used, and no amount of price research detects that, because both listings look equally attractive and the difference lives entirely in your life rather than on the page.

The reason the gap persists is that utilisation is the one input a seller has no incentive to help you estimate. Every other variable in a purchase decision has an advocate. Price has comparison engines. Quality has reviews. Delivery has a countdown. Specifications have a table. Frequency of use has nobody at all, because a truthful frequency estimate suppresses roughly as many sales as it improves, and no merchandising system optimises for the purchases you correctly decline to make.

There is a second-order effect worth naming. Because utilisation is unmeasured, it becomes the dumping ground for wishful thinking. The purchase is justified by the version of yourself who uses the thing weekly, and that person is generated on demand at the checkout page and never heard from again. This is why the most reliable predictor of how often you will use something is not your intention but your history with the nearest comparable object you already own. If the last four items in that neighbourhood of your life averaged eleven uses, the fifth one will not average two hundred because this one is different. It is not different. You are the constant.

The useful move is to stop trying to predict the denominator from intention and start reading it off the product, because products disclose it constantly. Not in the marketing copy, which is uniformly optimistic, but in structural features that are expensive to fake: how it is packaged, how it is listed, what is sold alongside it, what the maker publishes about maintaining it, and whether the catalogue around it is built for repetition or for occasion. Those signals are the subject of the next section, and they are the reason the two tables in this piece are organised the way they are.

Five Ways a Product Tells You How Often It Expects to Be Used

The signals below are not equally strong and they are not all available on every product, but at least one of them is present on almost anything worth more than pocket money. They share a common property: each of them costs the seller something to establish, which is what makes them harder to fake than a claim in a bullet list.

Pack size is a frequency estimate written down. When something is only sold in twelves, or twenty-fours, or hundreds, somebody in a planning role has already modelled how fast it gets consumed and concluded that a single unit is not a coherent purchase. That is a genuine disclosure. It also cuts the other way, and this is the part people miss: if an item that logically ought to be consumable is only available singly, the seller’s own demand data says it does not get replaced often, whatever the marketing implies about a daily routine.

A published maintenance schedule is a bet on longevity. A maker who tells you to re-oil at six months, re-season after heavy use, or re-sharpen every season is describing a relationship measured in years, and is also accepting the support burden that comes with owners who follow the instructions and then complain. Nobody writes a maintenance page for an object they expect to be discarded after a season. The presence of the page is the signal; the specific interval is almost secondary.

Service life quoted in use-units rather than calendar time is the most honest number in retail. Kilometres, hours, cycles, impacts, wears. A brand that says a midsole is finished at eight hundred kilometres has handed you the denominator directly and voluntarily, and has accepted that a substantial fraction of readers will do the division and decide the product is too expensive. Calendar warranties are a different species entirely; they tell you about the manufacturer’s confidence, not about your usage rate.

Catalogue architecture reveals whether an object is a staple or an occasion. A brand that offers one silhouette in nineteen colours across a decade has built its business on the assumption that people wear the thing constantly and come back for another. A brand that names a product after an event has told you, in the product name, that it expects the object to be used on that event and possibly not otherwise. Both are legitimate businesses. They are not interchangeable purchases, and the price often fails to distinguish them.

What sits in the accessory slot tells you what the object is for. If the related items around a listing are all event-specific, the merchandising system has classified it as event-bound. If they are general-purpose — mounts, cases, adapters, spares that make sense in ten different contexts — the system has classified it as a permanent tool. That classification is built from actual co-purchase behaviour across a very large sample, which makes it one of the few signals on a marketplace page assembled from other people’s real utilisation rather than anyone’s marketing intent.

Best Direct-to-Consumer and Brand Picks

Every value in the marketplace table is an artefact of infrastructure. A subscription badge exists because a platform built a replenishment system. A pack configuration exists because a catalogue has a unit field. An annual listing lifecycle is visible only because a marketplace keeps a product identifier alive across years while stock comes and goes. A co-purchase carousel requires a large shared population of buyers. A department cross-listing requires a taxonomy imposed above many sellers. Not one of those signals is available to a brand selling from its own site, because a single-catalogue seller has no shared identifier space, no rival sellers, no aggregate co-purchase graph and no external taxonomy to be cross-filed into.

What a direct brand has instead is the one thing a marketplace structurally cannot provide: first-person knowledge of how the object behaves over its life, and the freedom to publish it. The brand knows the service interval because it designed the wear surfaces. It knows the replacement cadence because it processes the warranty claims. It knows whether the product is a staple or an occasion because it decided which one to build the catalogue around. Cross-Border News Worth Reading This Week

The post-Lunar-New-Year reload is the largest single supply event in cross-border retail, and it arrives on a delay. Most shoppers experience the shutdown as a stock-out in late January and assume it resolves in early February. It does not. The two-week manufacturing pause plus transit means the actual restock lands in the week of the twentieth, which is why the “out of stock” badges clear in a cluster rather than a trickle. For a buyer the implication is concrete: the products you wanted and could not get in January are back now, frequently at the pre-holiday price, and the new-tooling SKUs are arriving green, with no review base and no return history to read.

New-SKU launches are peaking exactly when review bases are thinnest. Factories use the shutdown to tool up for the year’s new products, and those ship in the late-February wave. The result is a marketplace full of listings that look finished but have dozens rather than thousands of reviews. Automated merchandising treats a new listing with strong early velocity as a breakout, which overweights inventory behind products that have not been proven through a return cycle. For the next month, the single most useful filter on any platform is review count dated before this month, not star average.

The resolution-resale flood is a free audit of the first-quarter denominator. A large population bought fitness and productivity hardware in January and is listing it in February. The prices are set by owners who have personally established the object’s usage rate, which makes the resale channel the only place in retail where the denominator is disclosed by the seller rather than hidden. The categories to watch are studio bikes, treadmills, adjustable dumbbells, standing desks and language gadgets — and the condition risk is real but locally mitigable, because bulky resale tends to be regional and pickup-based.

Winter clearance is deep on the wrong things. The season-end discount is steepest on dated winter fashion and single-purpose holiday decor, which have denominators of one and were never good buys. The durable winter hardware — traction, thermal layers, heated gear with real service lives — clears only modestly, because its buyers are year-round and the sellers know it. Reading “winter sale” as a uniform discount is the most common error in the aisle this week, and it pushes people toward the disposable and away from the durable.

Valentine’s occasion goods are now pure clearance, and the lesson is free. Themed and dated merchandise has no residual demand after the fourteenth, and it is being discounted to recover shelf space. The gift-configured tools sitting next to it — a real pen, a quality knife, a proper flask — clear for the same occasion reason but carry denominators in the hundreds. The contrast is the cleanest available illustration of the whole method, and it costs nothing to observe.

Amazon, Shopify and TikTok: Where the Breakouts Are Forming

The three channels are producing genuinely different kinds of breakout right now, and the utilisation lens separates them cleanly, because each platform’s discovery mechanism has a characteristic bias about how often a product will be used.

Amazon is where high-denominator products win and the ranking system knows it. Search-led discovery favours the purchase people already intended to make, and replenishable goods get the additional advantage of repeat-purchase behaviour feeding back into rank. The breakouts forming here this week are consumables and their adjacent hardware — filtration, protective disposables, coffee maintenance, anything with a subscription mechanism attached — and two forces are amplifying this in February specifically: the post-CNY restock has returned the bestsellers to depth after a thin January, and the resolution surge has pulled a different cohort of buyers into first-time purchases of replenishables they will now keep buying. The near-term breakout candidates are unglamorous and have excellent fractions, which is the correct combination and the reason they rarely appear on trend lists.

Shopify and the direct-brand layer are where the occasion economy concentrates, and that is a warning as much as an observation. A single-catalogue brand has no search-intent traffic to harvest, so it manufactures demand through narrative, and the most reliable narrative device is an occasion — a drop, a season, a race, a date. That produces genuinely strong launches and genuinely poor denominators. The exception, and it is where the durable direct-brand breakouts are forming, is the subset of brands whose entire pitch is repetition rather than novelty: single-silhouette catalogues, staple goods, products with published maintenance schedules. Those brands convert slowly and retain extremely well, and they are the ones worth watching over the next quarter as the occasion-led cohort runs into a post-event demand vacuum.

TikTok Shop is structurally biased toward the worst fractions in retail, and that is not a criticism of the platform so much as a description of its physics. Discovery is driven by demonstration, and the products that demonstrate best are the ones with a visible before-and-after in under twenty seconds. Visible transformation correlates strongly with novelty and weakly with daily utility, which means the breakout mechanism selects for objects that are compelling once. The interesting development this week is a second cohort emerging alongside it: creators building around replenishment and routine rather than reveal — coffee maintenance, microfibre systems, filter cadences — which demonstrates badly but retains far better. Where a product breaks out on both mechanisms at once — demonstrable and consumable — the growth is unusually durable, and that overlap is the single most useful thing to look for on the platform.

The synthesis across all three is a timing rule rather than a platform preference. Amazon breakouts are safest to buy into immediately because the ranking that produced them is built from repeat behaviour. Direct-brand breakouts are safest to buy after the launch window, when the occasion pressure has drained and the product either still exists in the catalogue or does not. TikTok breakouts are safest to buy roughly a month late, because the interval between a product going viral and the same product appearing in volume on resale channels is short, and that interval is the market publicly reporting the denominator. Right now, the resolution-resale flood is that report arriving on schedule.

The Sixty-Second Utilisation Check

This does not require research tools, price history or a spreadsheet. It requires four questions, asked in order, before the item goes in the basket.

First: name the last comparable thing you bought, and count its uses honestly. Not the category average, not the aspirational figure — the actual object nearest to this one in your house and roughly how many times you have used it. This single number is the best available estimator, and it is almost always lower than the one you were about to assume. If you cannot name a comparable object, that itself is information: you are entering a new category, and first purchases in new categories have the worst utilisation records of any kind of buying.

Second: divide, and say the result out loud. Cost per use is only powerful when it is spoken as a unit price, because that is the format the brain already knows how to evaluate. Thirty-two cents a use sounds like nothing. Eleven dollars a use sounds like what it is. The same purchase, the same number, and completely different intuitions depending on whether you leave it as a total or convert it. Do the conversion. It takes four seconds and it is the only step that reliably changes decisions.

Third: ask whether the occasion or the object is doing the work. If the deadline evaporated tomorrow, would you still want this? For a filter, a pan, a pair of garden shears, the answer is yes and the deadline merely supplied the prompt. For anything dated, themed or named after the event, the answer is no, and the purchase is best understood as buying a memento rather than equipment. Mementos are fine. They should just be priced and budgeted as mementos rather than smuggled through as gear.

Fourth: check whether renting, borrowing or simply going without is on the table. The denominator argument has a floor: if the honest count is one or two, the correct comparison is not against a cheaper version of the same object but against not owning one at all. This question is skipped almost universally because ownership is the default frame that shopping puts you in, and it is the question that saves the most money per second spent asking it.

When a Single Use Is Worth the Whole Price

The method above, applied without judgement, collapses into an argument that nobody should ever buy anything used fewer than twenty times. That would be wrong, and the exceptions are specific enough to be listed rather than hand-waved.

When the single use is irreplaceable. A total solar eclipse lasts a couple of minutes and does not recur in the same place for a very long time. An object used once, at an event that cannot be repeated, is not evaluated by cost per use at all — it is evaluated against the cost of missing the event, which for a genuinely unrepeatable occasion can be arbitrarily large. This exception is narrow and it is heavily abused by marketing, but it is real, and a wedding, a graduation or a once-in-a-lifetime trip is a legitimate instance of it.

When the failure mode is safety rather than disappointment. Cost-per-use reasoning is about value, and value reasoning has no business anywhere near eye protection, load-bearing equipment, electrical isolation or anything holding a child. For those categories, the correct denominator is one, deliberately, because the analysis you should be running is about certification and provenance rather than efficiency. The most expensive mistake available this week is applying the frugal frame to a helmet or a harness.

When the object is insurance against a low-probability, high-cost event. A spare, a backup, a redundant part. The whole point is that it may never be used, and a denominator of zero is the successful outcome. Applying utilisation logic here inverts the purpose of the purchase entirely, which is the mechanism behind most stories that begin with somebody deciding they did not really need the second one.

When the single use has an outsized downstream payoff. A tool bought for one repair that prevents a replacement, or a garment bought for one interview. The use count is one and the return is not measured in uses. The check here is simply whether the payoff is real or retrospectively invented, and the honest test is whether you can name the specific outcome before buying rather than after.

And the reverse case, which matters just as much: a high-utilisation object can still be the wrong purchase. Something used daily but which you would not miss, which displaces something you already own, or which you use frequently only because you bought it, has an excellent cost per use and no value. The fraction is a filter for the obvious errors, not a substitute for wanting the thing.

Where This Read Breaks Down

A method is only trustworthy if you know its failure modes, and this one has four that are worth stating plainly before anybody applies it to a serious purchase.

Utilisation is not additive across a household. A cost-per-use figure calculated for one person collapses when several people share the object, and it collapses in the opposite direction when the object is nominally shared but actually used by one person who then resents it. The fraction assumes a single, honest user. Anything bought on the theory that the family will all use it needs the estimate halved at minimum, because shared-use predictions are the least accurate predictions in the whole exercise.

Some objects have their value concentrated rather than distributed. A smoke alarm, a first aid kit, a good umbrella on the one day it matters. Dividing price by uses treats every use as equal, and for a whole class of goods the uses are wildly unequal, with essentially all the value sitting in a small number of instances that you cannot schedule. Where the value distribution is that skewed, the fraction is the wrong tool and should be replaced by a straightforward question about what the bad outcome costs.

The denominator is partly endogenous, and pretending otherwise causes real errors. Buying a better version of something genuinely does increase how often it gets used, because friction suppresses usage and quality removes friction. A pan that works, a bag that opens easily, a tool that does not fight you — these get used more than their mediocre equivalents, which means the cheap option’s denominator is not the same as the good option’s denominator. The honest way to handle this is to allow a modest uplift for the better item and to be suspicious of yourself when the uplift required to justify the purchase starts exceeding about a third.

And the method says nothing at all about whether you want the thing. Cost per use is a filter for waste, not a source of desire. Applied too aggressively it produces a house full of highly utilised objects and no pleasure in any of them, and it will confidently recommend against every book, record, ornament and souvenir ever bought. The fraction is there to catch the purchases you will regret. It is not there to adjudicate the ones you will love, and it is bad at telling the difference.

What the Coming Weeks Do to Today’s Prices

Three categories change state over the next fortnight, and they change in opposite directions.

Winter-linked goods keep softening through early March, and the depth of that softening depends almost entirely on how event-shaped the item is. Dated winter fashion and holiday decor have no residual demand whatsoever and will be discounted to whatever recovers the shelf space, which is the steepest price move available in retail this quarter. Durable winter hardware behaves completely differently: traction devices, thermal layers and heated gear return to their baseline demand, which is stable, year-round and unaffected by the season’s end. Expect a modest softening as the artificial pull unwinds, not a collapse, and expect it to be brief because the underlying buyers were always there. If you want the equipment rather than the memento, early March is the better week, and the discount will be smaller than the headline clearance in the same aisle — that gap is the entire distinction between an object with a denominator and an object without one.

The resolution-resale flood peaks in the next two to three weeks and then tapers. The volume of barely-used fitness and productivity hardware listed in late February is the highest of the year, and it thins as the sellers who were going to give up have given up. The practical read is that the best selection and the best prices coincide right now, and both degrade together through March. For a durable object with a great denominator, this is the annual window, and it closes without announcement.

The post-CNY new-SKU wave does the opposite: it builds. Listings that arrive green in late February accumulate reviews through March, and the ones that survive a return cycle earn the ranking that makes them visible. The buyer’s move is patience — let the review base form, let the first return cycle complete, and buy the survivors rather than the arrivals. A product that is three weeks off a container has told you nothing about itself yet.

Category Notes

Consumables and replenishables. The strongest denominators available and the least interesting decisions. Buy on unit price and storage space, ignore the promotional calendar entirely, and note that the subscribe-and-save mechanism is a genuine information source before it is a discount: its existence confirms a replenishment rate that somebody modelled. The post-CNY restock has returned these to depth after a thin January.

Garden and outdoor prep. Late February is the start of the pre-spring build, and the listings reappear on schedule. Seed trays, hand tools, soil systems and the like have denominators in the dozens to hundreds and a use season that begins in weeks, not months. This is one of the few categories where buying now is unambiguously correct rather than merely acceptable.

Fitness and productivity hardware. The category where the fraction is most brutal and most observable this week, because the resale channel is publishing the denominator for free. Studio bikes, treadmills, dumbbells and standing desks are available barely-used at a fraction of retail from owners who have verified the usage rate is one. Condition is the only real risk, and local pickup removes most of it.

Winter sports and thermal gear. Season-end clearance, but read it as two aisles. Durable hardware clears modestly and is worth buying if you actually use it year to year; disposable winter fashion clears deeply and is never worth buying. The discount depth is inversely correlated with the object’s value, which is the trick.

Occasion apparel and footwear. Espadrilles before summer and race-day kit before a spring event both look like sensible timing plays, and both are purchases with denominators in the single digits being priced as though they were staples. If you want the item, buy it deliberately as an occasion item and at an occasion price.

Cookware, tools and glassware. The categories where published maintenance schedules and deep single-silhouette catalogues cluster, and consequently where cost per use is most likely to be genuinely excellent. These are also the categories least affected by every deadline this week, which makes them the calm corner of an otherwise deadline-driven month.

Bulky low-cost goods and new arrivals. The post-CNY wave contains both restocked staples and unproven new SKUs. Treat the restock as a buy and the new arrival as a wait; the review base is the only reliable separator, and it forms in March.

Policy, Logistics and Cross-Border Notes

The post-Lunar-New-Year reload is the most consequential logistics event of the first quarter for anyone buying into or out of Asia, and its effects on shoppers are mostly delayed and mostly positive. Restocked bestsellers return to depth at pre-holiday prices, which quietly reverses the scarcity pricing of January. The new-SKU wave brings genuine novelty but also a month of thin review bases and untested return cycles, which is a quality-risk window rather than a price one.

For cross-border buyers specifically, the interaction of the reload with the resolution-resale flood creates one narrow practical point. The resale channel is regional and pickup-biased for bulky goods, which removes the cross-border return problem that makes buying unfamiliar hardware from a distant marketplace risky. A studio bike bought locally from an owner who has admitted it was a mistake carries none of the reverse-logistics uncertainty of the same bike bought new from a platform on another continent. Where the denominator is the only thing that matters and condition is verifiable in person, the secondary market beats the primary one this week.

The winter-clearance logistics story is the reverse of the reload: it is a composition story, not an availability story. Retailers are not running out of winter goods; they are deliberately clearing them to make room for spring, which means the discounts are decisions rather than shortages and they persist on a schedule rather than resolving when a shipment arrives. The durable hardware discount is shallow because the decision behind it is “clear some, keep the rest,” while the disposable discount is steep because the decision is “clear all of it.” Reading the two as one sale is the error, and the fix is to ask which decision produced the tag in front of you.

Returns capacity is the constraint nobody prices, and it is quietly tighter in late February because the resolution surge and the Valentine occasion both produced a wave of January and early-February purchases now meeting their return windows at once. The stated return window is unchanged; the practical time to resolution is not. For cross-border purchases this widens an already meaningful gap between the policy and the experience, and it is the strongest current argument for buying deliberately rather than buying on the theory that reversal is cheap.

What’s Coming Next

Early March is the resolution-resale taper and the winter-clearance floor. The secondary market in fitness hardware thins as the sellers who were going to quit have quit, and the winter aisle stops getting cheaper because the disposable stock is already gone and the durable stock never dropped much. The honest read: the best selection and the best prices on used durable goods are both available this week, and both are gone by the middle of March.

Mid-to-late March is when the new-SKU survivors earn their ranking. The post-CNY arrivals that cleared a return cycle accumulate reviews and rise in search, while the arrivals that did not quietly sink. This is the month to buy the survivors rather than the novelties, and the review-count filter dated before March is the tool that separates them.

Spring is the next real demand wave, and it starts with garden, outdoor and home-organisation rather than apparel. The pre-spring build is already visible in late February listings and accelerates through March. The categories to watch are the ones with high denominators and a use season that begins in weeks — exactly the opposite of the occasion goods clearing this week.

The strategic read from here: run the fraction before you run the price comparison, treat every deadline this week as narrow rather than general, buy the durable version of anything the season made you want, and let the dated merchandise go past. The next fortnight will present an unusual number of very good discounts on objects with very bad denominators, and the discounts will be real.

Bottom line

Yesterday the argument was that a price is a fraction, the page only shows you the top of it, and the bottom is where almost all of the variance lives. Today it is the same idea seen through a calendar collision. This is the week the New Year version of you collides with February, and the collision is visible in the market: Chinese factories are back at full output and the shelves are reloading, Valentine’s occasion goods are clearing to nothing, winter is hitting its deepest and most mispriced discount, and a flood of barely-used resolution equipment is landing on the resale channels priced by owners who have personally verified the denominator is one.

The tables above are organised around that single idea, and around the fact that the two halves of the market disclose it through completely separate channels: marketplaces leak utilisation through infrastructure — pack sizes, subscription badges, listing lifecycles, co-purchase carousels, department taxonomies — while direct brands disclose it deliberately, through maintenance schedules, service life quoted in kilometres and impacts, and catalogues built either for repetition or for occasion. Neither channel is available to the other, and both are more reliable than the product description, because both cost something to establish.

This week, more than almost any other, the memento and the equipment are sitting next to each other in the same search results at roughly the same prices. The Valentine clearance, the winter clearance and the resolution-resale flood are all happening at once, and only one of them is offering you a genuinely good object at a genuinely good price. Sixty seconds and one division tells you which is which.
