Best Things to Buy Right Now — May 23, 2026

Quick take: Saturday of the long weekend, nineteen days before the tournament opens and roughly a month before the June sale gets going properly. Yesterday’s piece argued that when a product has no history you are left with the spec sheet, and that a spec sheet has a grammar — what gets measured, what gets quietly omitted, whether a figure arrives with the conditions that produced it. That argument has a blind spot, and it is a large one. There is one number on every product page that has no conditions, no test standard, no asterisk and no room for interpretation, and it is the number almost nobody reads as information. The price. Not whether it is high or low against your budget, which is a fact about you, but what the specific figure chosen reveals about where the company believes this object sits — against the rest of its own catalogue, and against everything else competing for the same slot. A product priced at an awkward figure between two conventional tiers is making a claim. A price that has not moved in fourteen months and a price that moves every Tuesday are two completely different kinds of statement. The distance between a brand’s cheapest and dearest model in one category is a map of where the engineering actually went. And the second-cheapest option in almost any range is, with unnerving reliability, the one the company expects you to buy. This piece is how to read a price the way you would read a spec, why the number is more candid than the copy above it, and twenty picks sorted by what each price is claiming.

What’s Hot This Week

Three currents are running, and all three are about numbers rather than objects. The long weekend is the year’s second-densest promotional window, which means an unusual proportion of what you are looking at right now has had its price rewritten in the last seventy-two hours by somebody or something that did not think very hard about it.

Holiday weekend pricing is the loosest pricing of the year

Promotional weekends do something specific to price discipline: they suspend it. For four days, the number on the page stops being a considered position and becomes a tactical response, and it is being set by people working against a clock or by systems working against a competitor feed. That produces two exploitable failures. The first is over-correction, where a product that was already correctly priced gets cut anyway because the category needed a headline, and the result is a genuinely good number attached to a product nobody was trying to move. The second is the reverse and it is far more common: a small increase applied a fortnight ago so that a percentage can be printed against it this weekend. Both are visible if you know the number’s recent history, and neither is visible from the number alone. The practical consequence for this weekend specifically is that price is temporarily a much worse signal than usual, so this is the week to read the structure of a range rather than the level of any single member of it. Structure does not go on sale.

Ranges are getting wider at both ends

Across a striking number of categories, brands that used to sell three models now sell five or six, and the new rungs are not in the middle. They are being added at the bottom, to defend against cheaper entrants, and at the top, to establish that a ceiling exists. This matters because it changes what the middle of a range means. When a range spans three models, the middle one is a compromise. When it spans six, the middle two are usually the only ones designed as products rather than as arguments, and the outer four exist to make those two legible. If you learned to shop by avoiding the cheapest and the most expensive, that heuristic was calibrated on three-rung ranges and it is now under-selective. The wider the range, the more of it is rhetoric.

The tier grid is reasserting itself after a wobbly year

Retail prices cluster, and they cluster at conventional stopping points that vary by category but are always recognisable — the forty-nines, the ninety-nines, the round hundreds. For about eighteen months the grid was disrupted, because input costs were moving faster than anybody wanted to re-print packaging, and a lot of products ended up parked at odd figures for reasons that had nothing to do with positioning. That has largely resolved, and prices are snapping back onto the grid. Which means the products still sitting at odd numbers are, once again, doing so deliberately. The signal has been noisy since early last year and it is clean again as of roughly this spring. That is the single most useful thing to know about reading prices this month.

A Price Is the Only Unconditional Number on the Page

Consider what happens to every other figure on a listing before it reaches you. A battery life claim carries a usage profile, usually unstated. A waterproof rating carries a test protocol with a defined depth and duration. A weight carries an ambiguity about whether the accessories are included. A power figure carries a measurement window. A noise figure carries a distance. Every specification is a conditional statement dressed as a fact, and yesterday’s argument was that learning to see the conditions is most of the skill.

The price has none of this. It is not a measurement of the object, it is a commitment about the object, and the commitment is unconditional and enforceable. If a company publishes a figure, that is the figure at which it will part with the item today. There is no test standard under which the number would come out differently, no favourable interpretation, no charitable reading. It is the only field on the page where the company has no rhetorical room at all.

This has an underappreciated consequence: the price is the one place where a company cannot lie by omission, and omission is where nearly all product-page dishonesty lives. A brand can decline to publish a durability figure. It cannot decline to publish a price. A brand can measure something under conditions you would never encounter. It cannot price something under conditions you would never encounter. Whatever internal argument the company had about what this object is worth, and against which of its other objects it should sit, the outcome of that argument is printed on the page in a form the company had to accept.

What the number does not tell you is anything absolute. A price has no meaning outside a frame, and there are exactly two frames that matter. The vertical frame is the company’s own range: what does this cost relative to the thing one rung below and one rung above, and what does the step buy? The horizontal frame is the field: what does this cost relative to the other objects competing for the same purchase? Almost everyone reads the horizontal frame, because it is what a search results page shows you by default, sorted for your convenience. Almost nobody reads the vertical frame, because it requires leaving the results page and going to look at the manufacturer’s own catalogue. The vertical frame is where nearly all of the information is, precisely because so few people go and get it.

The Awkward Number Between Two Tiers

Every category has a price grid. It is not universal and it is not published anywhere, but spend twenty minutes in any category and it becomes obvious: the rungs are at roughly thirty, fifty, eighty, a hundred and twenty, two hundred, three fifty. The exact figures differ, the phenomenon does not. Products cluster on the rungs because the rungs are where the filter sliders default, where the mental arithmetic is easy, and where the competition already is.

So what does it mean when a product lands between two rungs?

There are three explanations and all three are worth knowing. The first is that the company designed to a specification and priced afterwards. This is the rarest and the most interesting. It means the engineering budget was set by what the object needed rather than by what a price point permitted, and the number came out where it came out. These products tend to be from companies with a narrow catalogue and an engineering culture, and they are frequently the best-value objects in a category despite looking awkward on a comparison table.

The second explanation is that the company tried to reach the rung below and failed. Something in the bill of materials would not compress, so instead of shipping a compromised version at the round number they shipped an honest version at the odd one. This is also good news, though of a weaker kind. You are looking at a company that declined to hit a price point by degrading the object, which is a real disclosure about priorities.

The third explanation is positioning against a specific rival. If a product sits a small, precise distance below a well-known competitor rather than on any grid point at all, the number is not describing the object. It is describing the competitor. This is fine, but understand that you are reading a fact about the market rather than about the product, and the number will move as soon as the competitor’s does.

The way to tell them apart is to look at the rest of the catalogue. If every product from that brand sits off-grid, you are looking at explanation one and the company simply does not price to the grid. If exactly one product does, look at what sits directly above it in the field — you have almost certainly found explanation three. And here is the corollary that surprises people: a number that lands exactly on a grid point is the less informative one. It was chosen for the filter slider. It tells you the company understands retail. It tells you nothing whatsoever about the object.

Best Direct-to-Consumer and Brand Picks

The direct channel inverts the whole instrument. There is no competitive surface, no repricing engine, no rival adjacent on the page. There is a price list, written by the party that also decided the specification, and a price list is a designed object in a way a search results page can never be. Every rung was placed by somebody who had a reason. The five reads below all describe the internal architecture of such a list, and none of them can exist on a marketplace, because a marketplace has no list — it has a field.

What the Shape of a Range Tells You

Take any brand’s price list in one category and write down just the numbers, ignoring the model names. The shape of that list is a document about the company’s engineering, and it is readable in about ninety seconds.

Evenly spaced rungs suggest a range that was planned as a range — somebody sat down and decided there would be four products at four positions, and then worked out what each would contain. These are usually well-behaved catalogues where the steps buy real things and the middle is trustworthy. Clustered rungs, where three models sit within a small band and then there is a large jump, suggest something else: the cluster is one platform with variations, and the jump is where a genuinely different design begins. In a clustered range the cluster members are near-substitutes and the choice between them is preference, while the jump is the only real decision in the catalogue.

A long tail at the bottom — one model priced far below the rest — is almost always a defensive product, added to occupy a search filter rather than to be sold. It shares a name and very little else with the range above it. A long tail at the top is the halo, discussed above. Both tails should be read as arguments rather than as offers.

The most informative shape is the one with a visible gap in the middle. A gap means the company has a hole in its own line, and holes are not accidents in mature catalogues — they mean a product was discontinued and not replaced, or a launch has slipped. If you find a gap, the products on either side of it are both mispriced relative to each other, and the cheaper one is usually the better buy because it is doing work that a missing model should be doing.

Why the second-cheapest keeps winning

The entry model in almost any range is built backwards. Somebody chooses the price first, because the price has to appear in an advertisement and has to survive a filter slider, and the engineering is then compressed until it fits. Every decision in that product is a subtraction. The second rung is usually built the other way round: the specification is set to what the object ought to be, and the price is calculated afterwards and then rounded. Those are fundamentally different design processes and they produce fundamentally different objects, and the difference is far larger than the price gap between them.

There is a test that resolves it in a minute. Look at what the step from rung one to rung two actually buys. If it buys a component change you can name — a different motor, a different bearing, a different steel, a different driver — then rung two is a designed product and rung one is a price point wearing its badge. If the step buys finish, colour, a longer cable, a carrying case or a warranty extension, then rung one is the product and rung two is margin. The heuristic is not “buy the second-cheapest”. It is “check whether the second step is physical”, and it happens that in most catalogues it is.

A Price With an Author and a Price Without One

Two products, same category, same shelf. One has sat at the same figure for fourteen months. The other has been at nine different figures since January. Most buyers read this as stable versus volatile and move on. It is a much sharper distinction than that: one of those numbers has an author and the other does not.

A price that holds is being held. Somebody is choosing, repeatedly, not to move it, and that choice costs them sales every time a competitor undercuts. People do that for exactly two reasons. Either the number is a reference they are protecting, because the product’s position in the market depends on it and a drop would be very hard to reverse, or the number is contractual and they have no discretion. Both mean the same thing operationally: there is no lower price coming, and the discount you are waiting for will arrive as a bundle or a gift card rather than as a change in the number.

A price that moves weekly is not being decided by anyone. It is the output of a system observing other systems, and the striking thing about such prices is that they have no memory of the object. The repricer does not know what the thing cost to make or what it is worth; it knows what the nearest offer is. The consequence for the buyer is straightforward and mildly cheering: a machine-set price has no floor that anybody is defending, so it will visit lower figures, and the only question is how often and how far. Watch it for two weeks and you will have the range. Buy at the bottom of the observed range, not at the average.

The distinction matters most when you are comparing across the two. A held price and a drifting price are not comparable numbers even when they are numerically identical, because one of them is the real price and the other is a sample from a distribution. Comparing today’s drifting number against a held number will systematically mislead you in whichever direction the machine happened to be pointing this morning.

The version of this that is worth internalising: before you compare two prices, ask whether each of them is a decision or a reading. A decision can be argued with, waited out, or trusted. A reading is a snapshot of a process and should be treated as one point in a series you have not yet collected.

The Span Between Cheapest and Dearest Is an Engineering Map

Divide the price of a brand’s most expensive model in a category by its cheapest. The ratio is one of the most compressed pieces of information available about where the variable cost in that category actually lives, and it takes ten seconds to compute.

A narrow span, say under two, means the brand does not believe there is much room between a competent version and an excellent one. In practice this is true of categories where the physics is settled and the materials are cheap — hand tools, basic cookware, cabling, most fasteners. In a narrow-span category the whole range is close to the same object and the correct move is to buy near the bottom, because the top of the range is buying you finish.

A wide span, five or more, means there is a technology break somewhere inside the range: a point at which the design changes rather than improves. Your job in a wide-span category is to find the break, because everything below it is one class of object and everything above it is another, and the price step across the break is the only step in the catalogue that buys a change in kind. Everything else buys degree. The break is usually findable by reading the specification sheets in sequence and looking for the rung where a component type changes rather than a number.

An extremely wide span, ten or more, usually means the category has two markets sharing a catalogue — a consumer market and a professional or enthusiast one — and the brand is serving both from one list. In these ranges the middle is genuinely the worst place to be, because it is too expensive for the consumer use case and too compromised for the professional one. Buy near the bottom or above the break, not between them.

There is one refinement worth carrying. Span tells you where the variable cost is, but it does not tell you whether that cost is materials, labour or precision. Materials-driven spans are visible in the spec sheet and they depreciate; a rarer wood or a better steel is a real difference that also shows up in resale. Precision-driven spans are invisible in the spec sheet and they do not depreciate, because tolerance does not degrade with use the way a surface finish does. If you can determine that a wide span in a category is driven by precision rather than materials, the top half of that range is much better value than it looks, and that is one of the few situations where paying up is defensible on arithmetic rather than on taste.

The Five-Minute Price Read

The whole method compresses to five steps, in order, and none of them requires a tool or a browser extension.

One: write down the grid before you look. Spend two minutes in the category and note where the clusters are. Do this before you have a candidate, so the candidate cannot anchor you. You are looking for the four or five figures that products keep landing on.

Two: locate your candidate against the grid. On a rung or between rungs? If between, go and check whether the rest of that brand’s catalogue is also off-grid. Whole-catalogue off-grid means the brand prices to the object. One product off-grid means it is aiming at a specific rival, and you should go and find out which one.

Three: open the brand’s own price list, not the marketplace listing. This is the step people skip and it is where most of the information is. Write down the numbers in order. Look at the shape: even, clustered, gapped, tailed. Identify the entry rung and check whether the step above it is physical or cosmetic.

Four: compute the span. Cheapest to dearest, as a ratio. Under two, buy low. Over five, find the break. Over ten, avoid the middle entirely.

Five: establish whether the number is a decision or a reading. Check the same product at two or three sellers. Identical everywhere means a held or contractual price, so there is nothing to wait for. Scattered means a machine is setting it, so collect a fortnight of observations before you transact and buy at the bottom of the range you observed rather than at whatever it says today.

That is it. Five steps, and the only one that takes real time is the fortnight of observation, which you only need for the machine-set case. Everything else is a single visit to a page most buyers never open.

When the Number Is Lying by Accident

Prices mislead far more often through inertia and administration than through intent, and it is worth being able to recognise the innocent cases so that you do not read malice into arithmetic.

The number is old. A price set three years ago and never revisited will look like a bargain today and is not making any claim at all. Small manufacturers in particular re-price infrequently, and a figure that has not been touched since before the last round of input cost increases is a fossil rather than an offer. It is still a good deal. It is just not a signal about positioning.

The number is a rounding artefact of the local grid. The same object sold into four markets will often carry four figures that do not convert into one another, because each was rounded onto that market’s own tier grid after conversion. The apparent regional price discrimination is frequently just four independent rounding operations. Do not infer a strategy from a discrepancy that a spreadsheet produced.

The number is a contract. Where a manufacturer sets a minimum advertised figure, the price you see is a legal artefact and contains no information about the object whatsoever. The tell is uniformity across unrelated sellers. Read nothing into it beyond the absence of a discount.

The number is mostly logistics. In heavy, bulky or certification-intensive categories, a substantial fraction of the price is freight and compliance rather than product, and two objects at the same price can contain very different amounts of object. This is why comparing a compact item against a bulky one on price alone is close to meaningless in furniture, appliances and anything with a battery.

The number is a launch guess. A price set before the product had any market feedback is a hypothesis, and hypotheses get revised. Launch prices in unfamiliar categories are the least informative numbers in retail, and they are also the ones most often presented as authoritative. Give a new product one full pricing cycle before treating its number as meaningful.

And the reverse case, which is rarer but real: a number can be deliberately uninformative. Some brands price everything in a category identically as a policy, specifically so that customers choose on fit rather than on cost. That is a design decision about the buying experience, not a failure of the price to carry information, and it is usually stated openly if you go looking.

Where This Read Breaks Down

Four limits, and they are worth stating plainly because the method is confident enough to be dangerous.

Single-product companies have no vertical frame. If a brand makes one thing, there is no range to read and half the method is unavailable. You are back to the horizontal frame alone, which is the weaker one. This is common among the most interesting small manufacturers, and it means the technique is least useful exactly where you might most want it.

Subsidised categories invert everything. Where the object is priced below cost because the money is made on consumables, refills, cartridges or a subscription, the price of the object is not the price of ownership and reading it as a specification will lead you directly into the worst purchase in the category. The tell is a proprietary consumable. When you see one, stop reading the price of the machine and go and read the price of the consumable per unit instead.

Fashion and status categories have no referent. In categories where the price is itself part of the product’s meaning, the number is not describing a position against engineering, it is describing a position against other people. The method still describes what the company is claiming, but the claim is no longer about the object, and the second-cheapest rule in particular stops working entirely.

None of this tells you whether to buy anything. The whole method answers “what is this number claiming and is the claim coherent”. It is silent on whether you want the object, whether you will use it, and whether the money is better spent elsewhere. A perfectly coherent price on a thing you do not need is still a bad purchase, and reading prices well makes it easier, not harder, to talk yourself into one.

Cross-Border News, Policy and Logistics

A few things worth having in view this week, none of which should change a purchase decision on its own.

Freight rates on the main east-west lanes have been drifting rather than spiking, which is the condition under which landed costs stay predictable and price lists stay stable. The practical consequence for a buyer is that prices set this spring are likely to hold through the summer, so the usual argument for buying early to get ahead of a cost pass-through is weaker than it has been in recent seasons. That cuts both ways: less urgency, but also less likelihood of a cost-driven discount.

Regional price divergence is running wider than usual across several electronics categories, which is mostly the rounding phenomenon described above compounded by currency movement that has not yet been passed through into local price lists. If you have the option to buy from more than one regional storefront, this is a good month to check both, and a bad month to assume that the difference reflects anything about the product or the seller.

On the compliance side, the general direction across major markets continues to be toward more information on the listing rather than less — more disclosure of materials, more disclosure of repairability, more disclosure of who the actual manufacturer is. For a price reader this is straightforwardly good news, because most of what the method above requires is exactly this kind of published detail, and the amount of it available on an average listing is meaningfully higher than it was two years ago.

Returns handling remains the largest hidden variable in cross-border purchasing and it is not improving. The gap between a domestic return and a cross-border one is measured in weeks and, frequently, in who pays the freight. This does not affect how you read a price but it should affect the size of purchase at which you are willing to buy across a border at all.

Amazon, Shopify and TikTok: Where the Breakouts Are Forming

Each of the three major surfaces is producing a different kind of breakout right now, and the differences are legible in exactly the pricing terms this piece has been developing.

On Amazon, the products breaking out are the ones sitting just below a well-known reference product from a category leader. This is the off-grid explanation three pattern operating at scale: a challenger prices a precise short distance under the incumbent, wins the comparison, and rides the incumbent’s search traffic. The categories where this is most visible right now are hand tools, small kitchen electrics and entry audio. The buying implication is that these products are genuinely good value while the incumbent holds its number, and they become poor value the moment the incumbent moves, because the challenger’s whole position was relative. Buy them, but do not expect the value proposition to survive a price war.

On Shopify and the direct-store ecosystem generally, the pattern is the opposite: the breakouts are brands publishing more pricing information rather than less. Cost breakdowns, published annual revisions, explicit no-discount policies. This works because a direct store has no competitive surface to be compared on, so the only way to justify a number is to explain it, and explanation turns out to convert better than discounting. For a buyer this is the most useful development on any platform, because a published breakdown collapses the entire vertical frame into a document you can read in two minutes.

On TikTok and content-led commerce, the breakout products are overwhelmingly the ones with a demonstrable single function and a price that fits inside an impulse threshold. The pricing signature is unmistakable: clustered hard onto the low grid points, almost never off-grid, because the number has to be instantly legible in a short video. That legibility requirement is a genuine constraint on what can be sold there, and it means the platform systematically under-represents products whose value is in precision or longevity, since neither can be demonstrated in the format. Read a viral price as a fact about the medium, not about the object.

Category Notes

Hand tools and garden. The narrowest spans in retail and therefore the category where buying near the bottom of a good brand’s range is most reliably correct. The exception is anything with a cutting geometry that has to be maintained, where the top of the range often buys serviceability rather than finish. Check whether replacement parts are listed at all; where they are, the upper rungs usually deserve their premium.

Audio. The widest spans and the clearest technology breaks, which makes it the best category to practise the method on. Almost every audio brand has one rung where the design changes rather than improves, and it is nearly always findable by reading the specification sheets in ascending order until a component type changes. Below that rung, buy the cheapest that meets your needs. Above it, the second-cheapest rule applies again within the upper sub-range.

Apparel. The category where the flat-price policy is most common and most honest, because size and colour genuinely do not correlate with cost in any way the customer should be made to absorb. Treat a brand that charges more for larger sizes as having told you something about itself.

Kitchen and coffee. Ranges here are usually clustered rather than even, with a large jump where domestic equipment becomes semi-professional. The jump is the only real decision. Everything below it is the same machine in different colours, and a surprising amount above it is the same machine with a larger boiler.

The categories where the grid barely exists

Art supplies, specialist stationery, sharpening equipment and most workshop consumables have almost no price grid, because the products are not comparable enough for one to form. In these categories the awkward-number read does not work, and you should fall back entirely on the vertical frame — the brand’s own list, in order, looking for what each step buys. It is slower but it is the only frame available, and these are also the categories where brands are most likely to price by input cost and least likely to price by position, which makes the list unusually honest.

Bottom line

The price is the only number on the page that was not written to persuade you of anything except itself, and it is therefore the only number that cannot be hedged, conditioned or quietly omitted. Read it as a specification. Note where the grid is and whether this product is on it. Open the brand’s own list and read the shape. Check whether the second step buys a component or a colour. Compute the span and find the break. Establish whether the figure in front of you is a decision somebody defends or a reading a machine took this morning. None of that takes longer than five minutes, and at the end of it you will know something the specification sheet cannot tell you: not what the object is, but what the company that made it believes the object is worth against everything else it knows how to make. This weekend, with prices looser than they will be at any other point before the June sale, that distinction is worth most of what you can know.
