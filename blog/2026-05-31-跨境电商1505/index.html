Best Things to Buy Right Now — May 31, 2026

Quick take: It is the last day of May, a Sunday, and there is almost nothing to buy. The tournament opens eleven days from now. The big June sale is twenty-three days out. Prices are frozen, the boxes are quietly filling up with accessories nobody asked for, and every listing you look at is in the holding pattern that always precedes an event. On a weekend like this the standard advice is to do nothing, and the standard advice is half right — you should not be buying. But there is one specific piece of research that is worth doing today rather than in three weeks, and the reason is structural: the warranty term is the only field on a product page that does not change during a sale. The price will move four times before the event closes. The shipping estimate will move. The coupon will appear, vanish and reappear. The stock counter will lie to you. The warranty will sit there, unchanged, saying exactly what it says today, because changing it requires a decision by someone in finance rather than someone in merchandising. Which means research you do on a dead Sunday in May is still valid on the busiest day in June, and nothing else on the page has that property. It gets better. A warranty is not a marketing claim, it is a booked liability. Before a company can print “five years” it has to reserve against five years of claims, which means somebody sat down with the returns data and estimated the failure curve. That estimate is private. The number printed on the page is the only place it leaks. Marketing copy costs nothing to write and therefore tells you nothing; a warranty costs real money and therefore cannot lie in the same direction for very long. Reviews describe the first thirty days, because that is when people write them. Warranties describe year three, because that is what the reserve has to cover. They are not competing sources about the same thing — they cover non-overlapping windows, and only one of them was written by people who have the failure data. What follows is how to read that number properly, why the deviation from the category norm matters far more than the number itself, why the exclusion list is the most honest paragraph any manufacturer publishes, an inversion that runs against everything this series has argued for a month — that warranties are most useful exactly where they are least valuable — and twenty picks sorted by what the coverage quietly admits.

What’s Hot This Week

The market is asleep and that is the whole point. Eleven days before a tournament and three weeks before a sale, the interesting activity has moved off the price field entirely, which frees you up to look at the parts of a listing that most people never read.

Nothing is moving, so look at what was never going to move

Across every category worth tracking, headline prices have been flat for a fortnight. That flatness is not evidence of a stable market, it is evidence of a market waiting — a seller who cuts now shrinks the discount they are allowed to advertise later, so the promotional impulse gets rerouted into the carton instead. We covered that mechanism at length and it is still running. What matters today is the consequence: if the price is uninformative, spend your attention on the fields that are informative, and the most informative field per word on the entire page is the warranty. It is also the field that will still be true in July. A price you research today is a price you have to re-check on June 23. A warranty term you research today is a warranty term you can act on in September.

The quiet pre-event warranty extension

There is a second reason this is timely. In the two to three weeks before a large sale event, a noticeable number of brands quietly lengthen coverage — one year becomes two, two becomes three, sometimes with a banner and often without. The logic is identical to the bundle logic: it is a concession that does not touch the price field. But it is cheaper than a bundle, because a bundle costs a real accessory out of real inventory this quarter, whereas a warranty extension costs an accounting reserve that lands in a future fiscal year. A warranty extension is a discount paid for by a future version of the company. That makes it the single cheapest sweetener available, which is exactly why you should be suspicious of one that appears three weeks before an event and no other time. A permanent term change is a statement about the product. A temporary one is a statement about the sale.

Categories where the coverage is currently doing the talking

Three areas are unusually legible right now. Power supplies and other passive electronics, where warranty length has drifted into a genuine tier system that maps almost perfectly onto component grade. Battery-containing anything — tools, personal care, outdoor equipment — where the gap between the headline term and the cell coverage has been widening for two seasons. And soft goods with lifetime claims, where a wave of new brands has adopted terms that were invented by companies with thirty-year balance sheets and adopted by companies with thirty-month ones. All three are readable in under a minute per listing.

Why the Warranty Is the Only Field That Cannot Lie

Every other claim on a product page is free. “Premium build quality” costs nothing to write. “Engineered for durability” costs nothing. “Tested to one hundred thousand cycles” costs the price of one test on one unit under conditions the manufacturer chose. A five-star average costs whatever the review-generation budget was. None of these carry a financial consequence for being wrong, which is precisely why they carry no information.

A warranty is different in kind, not degree. When a company offers coverage, an accounting entry follows: an estimated liability, sized by multiplying the expected claim rate by the expected cost per claim across the covered period. That number reduces reported margin today for products sold today. It gets reviewed. If actual claims come in above the reserve, someone has to explain the variance. This is a boring, unglamorous process and that is exactly what makes it trustworthy — the warranty term is the output of an argument between the marketing department, which wants a big number, and the finance department, which has the returns data and has to pay for it. What you see printed is where that argument settled.

This gives the number a property nothing else on the page has. It is a disclosure, in the strict sense: a piece of private information that the company would generally prefer to keep private, revealed because the act of offering coverage necessarily reveals it. The company knows its year-three failure rate. You do not, and no amount of review-reading will get you there, because the review population is dominated by people who bought in the last few months and wrote within a few weeks of unboxing. A sample of four thousand reviews can tell you almost nothing about what happens in month thirty-eight. The manufacturer’s warranty desk knows that number to two decimal places.

There is an obvious objection: companies offer generous warranties for marketing reasons all the time, and some of them get burned. True, and the exceptions are worth cataloguing, which we do further down. But notice the shape of the error. Companies that over-promise on coverage tend to discover the problem and quietly shorten the term at the next revision, or narrow the exclusions, or add a registration requirement. The system self-corrects, and it self-corrects in a direction you can observe. A brand that has held a long term across several product generations has survived several rounds of that correction. A brand that has just adopted one has not.

The practical upshot is a reordering of what you read first. Most people read the price, then the star rating, then the bullet points, then — if they are unusually diligent — the negative reviews. The warranty, if it is read at all, is read last, as a formality, as the thing you check after you have already decided. Read it second, right after the price. It takes forty seconds and it is the only paragraph on the page that someone had to pay for.

The age of a term matters as much as its length

This deserves separating out, because it is the fastest way to tell a real signal from an aspirational one. A warranty term is not a static fact; it is a position that gets re-argued every time a product is revised, and every revision is an opportunity for finance to claw it back if the claims came in hot. A term that has survived three product generations has survived three rounds of that argument. A term introduced this quarter has survived none.

You can usually date a term without much effort. Look at whether the previous model in the same line carried the same coverage, check whether the brand’s older products on the support page share it, and note whether the current term applies retroactively to earlier units or only to the new ones. When a long term appears suddenly across an entire catalogue at once, that is a marketing decision. When it has been quietly attached to the same product family for six years, that is data. Length tells you what the company claims; persistence tells you whether the claim held up.

Read the Deviation, Not the Number

Here is where most warranty-reading goes wrong: people treat the term as an absolute quantity. Five years good, one year bad. That is not how the signal works, because failure rates differ enormously by category and so do the conventions.

Two years on a cordless vacuum is generous. Two years on a cast iron skillet is close to an insult, because the honest expected failure rate of a cast iron skillet over two years, absent abuse, is approximately zero and everyone in the category knows it. Ten years on a mattress sounds extraordinary until you learn that ten years is the floor in that category and has been for a decade, at which point it stops being information and becomes wallpaper. Three years on a monitor is standard. Three years on a pair of earbuds would be remarkable. The signal is the deviation from the category norm, and a term that matches the norm carries almost no information at all.

This is why the first step is never “is this a long warranty” but “what does this category normally offer.” That takes one comparison across three or four competing listings, which you were going to open anyway. Once you have the local norm, three readings become available and each one means something specific.

Above the norm is the strongest positive signal available in consumer research, and it gets stronger the more crowded the category is. In a commodity category with thin margins, extending coverage is expensive and easily copied, so nobody does it unless the failure data supports it. When one manufacturer in a field of twenty offers double the standard term at a comparable price, the most economical explanation is that their unit genuinely fails less and they know it. They are converting private information into a competitive advantage, which is the only way to monetise it.

At the norm means the term was set by competitive matching rather than by actuarial analysis, and should be read as neutral. Not bad — just silent. Do your durability research elsewhere.

Below the norm is the reading people underuse, and it is the most reliable of the three. Nobody offers less coverage than their competitors by accident. Coverage is the cheapest visible differentiator in existence for a product that does not break; if you are undercutting the category standard, it is because the reserve maths does not work at the standard term. A brand offering one year where the category offers three has told you something about the failure curve that no review will, and they have told you against their own interest, which is what makes it credible. Short coverage in a category with long conventions is the closest thing to a confession you will find on a retail page.

One refinement that matters more than it sounds: compare terms at comparable price points, not across the whole category. A budget line and a flagship line from the same manufacturer will often carry different terms, and that difference is internal actuarial data made visible.

The Exclusions Are the Bill of Materials for the Failure

If the term is the headline, the exclusion list is the actual document, and it is routinely more informative. A warranty that covers “the product” for five years but explicitly carves out the battery, the pump, the heating element, the seals, the belt, the filter housing and “consumable components” has just handed you a ranked list of the parts most likely to fail, written by the only people who know.

Think about how an exclusion gets written. Nobody excludes a component speculatively; every carve-out is a small reduction in the attractiveness of the offer, so it has to be paid for by an expected reduction in claims. A part appears on the exclusion list because claims against that part were material enough to justify the marketing cost of naming it. The list is generated by the claims data, filtered by materiality. That is a remarkably clean signal and almost nobody reads it.

Batteries are the canonical case and worth being explicit about, because the pattern now spans tools, personal care, outdoor equipment, cleaning devices and a growing share of kitchen appliances. A “three-year warranty” on a cordless product where the cells carry twelve months is, for practical purposes, a twelve-month warranty, because the cells are the component with the shortest life and the highest replacement cost relative to the unit. On many cordless products the battery is thirty to fifty per cent of the replacement cost of the whole item. Covering the housing for three years while covering the expensive perishable part for one is not a three-year warranty in any meaningful sense. It is a one-year warranty with a long tail on the parts that were never going to fail.

The same structure appears with different vocabulary elsewhere. In espresso and other pumped appliances, watch for exclusions on the pump and the boiler. In anything with a compressor, watch the compressor term separately. In garments, watch for “wear and tear” carve-outs broad enough to cover the actual failure mode, since a down jacket fails by seam and baffle, not by fabric disintegrating. In footwear, watch whether the sole unit is covered, because that is what goes. In furniture, watch whether the mechanism is covered on the same clock as the frame, because on an office chair it is the gas lift and the tilt mechanism that fail, not the shell.

Two further tells belong here. First, the labour split. A five-year parts, one-year labour warranty is a one-year warranty for anything you cannot service yourself, and a five-year warranty for anything you can. Whether that is good news depends entirely on whether the product is openable, which is itself worth checking. Second, pro-rating. A ten-year pro-rated warranty that pays a declining share of replacement cost is the manufacturer publishing a depreciation schedule — the curve tells you the expected remaining life at each year, and if the curve drops fast in years two and three, that is the honest estimate, printed in a table, in public.

Why this matters so much: cross-brand warranty comparisons are always contaminated. Different companies have different margins, different marketing philosophies, different appetites for risk, different balance sheets. When Brand A offers more than Brand B, a dozen explanations compete. Intra-brand variation eliminates every one of them. Same company, same finance department, same marketing team, same competitive pressure, same fiscal year, same appetite for risk — and yet the number changes depending on which part of the product you are looking at. There is exactly one variable left that can explain the difference, and it is the expected failure rate. One Warranty, Four Price Tiers

Every guide in this series eventually finds the axis along which its main idea inverts, and for warranties the axis is price relative to return freight. The same warranty term means four completely different things depending on what the item costs, and the pattern runs opposite to intuition.

Under about fifty dollars, the warranty is economically void. Packing the unit, printing a label, paying carriage and waiting three weeks costs more in time and money than replacing the item. You will not claim. Nobody claims. The manufacturer knows nobody claims, which is part of why the term can be generous. The cash value of the coverage is approximately zero.

Between roughly fifty and three hundred dollars is the contested zone, and the entire question is who pays return freight. A two-year warranty where you pay shipping both ways on a hundred-and-twenty dollar item is worth a fraction of a two-year warranty where the brand sends a prepaid label. This is the band where reading the claims procedure matters more than reading the term.

Between three hundred and fifteen hundred dollars the coverage has real cash value, and the dominant variable becomes the location of the obligor. A genuine claim on an eight-hundred-dollar item is worth pursuing across a border; whether it is worth pursuing depends on where the repair happens.

Above fifteen hundred dollars the warranty starts to matter less again, because at that price point you are usually protected by statutory consumer rights that outlast the commercial warranty, and because a third-party service network typically exists. At the top end you are buying serviceability, not coverage. The right question stops being “how long is the warranty” and becomes “who can fix this in my country, and can they get parts.”

Now the payoff, and it genuinely reverses the advice this series has been giving for a month. Both of the last two guides concluded that their framework broke down at the cheap end — that below forty or fifty dollars the analysis was not worth the effort. This framework does the opposite. Its insurance value collapses at the cheap end, but its information value is highest exactly there. On a thirty-dollar item you will never file a claim, so the coverage is worth nothing as protection — but the durability question is the only real question about a thirty-dollar item, since the price is not in dispute and the features are all the same. The warranty is the only durability evidence available, and it costs nothing to read. Meanwhile on a two-thousand-dollar item the coverage is worth a great deal as protection but tells you comparatively little you could not learn from the service network, the parts catalogue and the second-hand market.

So: read warranties on cheap goods, and negotiate warranties on expensive ones. The cheap end is where the number is pure signal. The expensive end is where it is pure money. The middle is where you have to do both, which is why the middle is where people get it wrong.

Cost per covered year, the number nobody displays

There is a single calculation that makes all of this operational, and it takes about five seconds per listing: divide the price by the effective term. Not the printed term — the effective one, after you have applied the battery carve-out, discounted the registration gate to the default, and capped the result at the plausible remaining life of the obligor.

The reason to bother is that this number reorders shortlists in a way the price alone never does. An item at one hundred and eighty with a genuine five-year term costs thirty-six per covered year. A competitor at one hundred and forty with an effective one-year term costs one hundred and forty per covered year, and it looked like the cheaper option right up until you did the division. The gap is frequently large enough to invert a ranking entirely, and it is invisible on every comparison tool in existence because no retailer has any incentive to compute it for you.

Two cautions. This works within a category, not across categories, since expected lifespans differ wildly. And it flatters products whose coverage is long because failure is rare rather than because build quality is high — which is fine, because low failure is what you were buying anyway.

The Registration Gate and Other Ways to Shrink a Number You Already Printed

Once you start reading terms as liabilities, a whole family of devices becomes legible. Each one has the same purpose: advertise a large number while reserving against a smaller one.

Conditional extension is the most common. One year standard, extended to three if you register within thirty days. This is not generosity, it is claim-rate management, and it works because registration rates are low — often very low. The company advertises three, reserves against something much closer to one and a half, and the gap between those two numbers is pure margin. There is a useful corollary: the size of the gap between the default term and the extended term measures how much the brand doubts its own headline. A one-to-three jump is a big claim resting on a low take-up assumption. A two-to-three jump is close to honest.

There is also a second motive, which is data. Registration converts an anonymous marketplace buyer into a known contact, which is worth real money to a brand that sells mostly through third parties. That does not make the extension fake, but it does mean the extension exists for reasons other than confidence in the product, so it should be discounted as a signal even where it is honoured perfectly.

Proof-of-purchase strictness is the quiet one. Terms that require an original dated receipt from an authorised seller are doing real work on marketplaces, where a meaningful share of units move through channels the brand did not authorise. If the coverage requires a document you may not be able to produce, the effective term is shorter than the printed one by an amount you cannot calculate in advance.

Serial-number registration windows are stricter again, and they interact badly with cross-border shipping. A thirty-day window that starts at date of purchase rather than date of delivery can be half-consumed by transit before the box arrives.

Consumable definitions deserve attention because they expand. A filter is obviously consumable. A belt, arguably. A seal? A battery? A brush head, a gasket, a nozzle, a blade? Every one of those has appeared in a consumables carve-out somewhere, and the trend runs one way. Read the definition, not the word.

“Lifetime” is the weakest signal in the entire vocabulary, and this is the honest counter-case to everything above. Lifetime coverage costs nothing to offer on an item that does not fail — a stainless spork, a canvas tote, a machined aluminium bracket — because the reserve is negligible. It is therefore genuinely a marketing decision in a way that a five-year term on a motorised appliance is not. Worse, “lifetime” is usually defined as the lifetime of the product as determined by the manufacturer, not the lifetime of the buyer, which makes it terminable at the moment the model is discontinued. A specific number is almost always more informative than the word lifetime, and when you see both in one category, trust the number.

Who Actually Owes You the Repair

A warranty is a promise by a legal entity, and the identity of that entity is the part that cross-border buyers most consistently overlook. There are three questions and they take about a minute to answer.

Who is the obligor? The brand, a regional subsidiary, an importer, the marketplace seller, or a third-party administrator. These are very different counterparties with very different survival odds and very different incentives.

Where does the unit go? If a claim requires shipping to another continent, calculate that cost now, before you buy, because it does not get cheaper later. A five-year warranty administered by an entity with no presence in your country is a five-year warranty with a freight bill attached.

Who pays the carriage? Both ways, one way, or neither. This single line item determines the real value of coverage in the entire middle price band.

From those three answers falls a simple valuation rule that is worth memorising: a warranty is worth the smaller of the cost of the repair and the cost of claiming. If claiming costs more than replacing, the coverage is worth zero regardless of its term. A ten-year warranty on a twelve-kilogram appliance, where the service centre is across an ocean and you pay outbound freight, is nominally alive and economically dead. It still carries information — the ten years still tells you the manufacturer expects low failure rates — but it carries no money.

Two cross-border complications compound this. First, return freight on bulky low-density goods is priced on dimensional weight, so the items most likely to need a warranty repair are also the ones most expensive to send back. Second, a warranty repair crossing a border is an import, and depending on how it is declared it can attract duty on the way home. Goods returning after repair usually have relief available, but relief has to be claimed correctly, and a courier filing on your behalf may not do so. The result is an occasional surprise bill on a “free” repair.

There is one more, and it is the most easily avoided: a warranty term longer than the credible remaining life of the company is not a warranty. Discount every printed term by the probability the obligor still exists at the point you would need it. A five-year promise from a two-year-old marketplace brand with a single-product catalogue is a statement about the next twenty-four months at best. The right mental operation is not to read the term but to take the smaller of the term and the expected life of the business behind it, and for a great many new marketplace brands that second number is the binding one.

Category Notes

Batteries and anything containing them. Assume the cell term is the real term until proven otherwise. Check whether replacement cells are sold separately, at what price, and whether the pack is proprietary — a serviceable battery in an open format is worth more than two extra years of coverage on a sealed one.

Motors and pumps. These fail suddenly rather than gradually, which makes coverage disproportionately valuable, so a short motor term in a category where motors are the failure mode is a strong negative. Look for the motor to be listed separately; if it is not mentioned at all, it is covered by the general term, which is usually good news.

Textiles and soft goods. The failure mode is stitching, zips, coatings and hardware, in that order. A warranty covering “manufacturing defects” but excluding coating degradation excludes the actual failure mode of most waterproof garments. Down insulation is the interesting exception: down itself lasts decades, so a long term on a down product is mostly a statement about the shell and the baffles, and brands that know their construction is good will say so with the number.

Electronics without moving parts. Long terms are cheap here, which is why displays, supplies and passive audio have drifted long. Use the tier ladder, not the absolute number.

Furniture and mechanisms. Split the frame term from the mechanism term and ignore the frame term. Nobody’s chair frame breaks.

Kitchen appliances. Watch the heating element and the seals. Also watch for terms that vary between the domestic and the export version of the same model, which is more common than it should be and is worth a direct question before purchase.

Optics and precision instruments. These often carry very long or lifetime coverage that is genuinely meaningful, because the failure modes are rare and the brands are old. This is one of the few categories where the word lifetime is backed by an institution likely to outlive the buyer.

Where This Read Breaks Down

The framework has five real failure modes and pretending otherwise would make it a slogan rather than a tool.

Statutory minimums destroy the signal. In markets with a mandatory legal conformity period, a term equal to that period is not a choice and therefore not information — it is compliance. Where the statutory floor is two years, a two-year warranty tells you nothing whatsoever, and the first informative number is three. In markets without a floor, a one-year term is already a decision. The signal only exists above the statutory minimum in your jurisdiction, which means the same printed term carries different information depending on where you are standing. This is the single most important caveat here and it invalidates naive cross-market comparisons entirely.

Competitive escalation decouples the number from the data. In a handful of categories — mattresses, hand tools, some outdoor gear — everybody matched everybody years ago and the terms ratcheted to a level nobody would choose from first principles. Where the whole category sits at ten years or lifetime, the number has become a licence to compete rather than an estimate. You can spot these categories easily because the variance is near zero.

Insured warranties are the insurer’s estimate, not the maker’s. Where coverage is underwritten by a third party or sold as an add-on plan, the actuarial work is real but it belongs to someone else and reflects their pricing and risk appetite. Useful, but a different signal.

Channel-control terms. When coverage is restricted to authorised dealers, part of the term’s purpose is distribution enforcement rather than durability communication, and the length may be set by channel strategy.

Deliberate loss-leading on coverage. New entrants sometimes buy credibility with a term they cannot support, treating early claims as a customer-acquisition cost. This is real and it is exactly why the age of the term matters as much as its length: a term held across three product generations has survived the correction, a term introduced last quarter has not.

How to Read a Warranty in Ninety Seconds

A short procedure, in order, because the order is what makes it fast.

One: find the number, then find three competitors’ numbers. You need the local norm before the number means anything. Thirty seconds.

Two: find the exclusion list and read it as a parts list. What is named is what fails. If a battery, motor, pump, seal or element is named separately, that separate term is the real term. Twenty seconds.

Three: check for a registration gate. If the headline term requires action within a window, treat the default term as the real one and the extension as a bonus you will probably forget to claim. Ten seconds.

Four: identify the obligor and the freight direction. Brand, subsidiary, seller or administrator; and who pays to ship it. Twenty seconds.

Five: take the smaller of the term and the plausible remaining life of the company. Ten seconds, and it is the step that saves the most money.

Two habits make this stick. Do step one before you read the headline term, so that the category norm anchors you instead of the marketing number. And write the effective term down next to the price, because the comparison you actually want is cost per covered year, which is a number nobody displays and which reshuffles most shortlists the moment you calculate it.

Policy, Logistics and Cross-Border Notes

Several things sit in the background this week and none of them are urgent, which is precisely why they are worth reading now rather than in three weeks.

Ocean and air rates are in their usual pre-summer posture, which mostly means that whatever landed cost sits behind the June sale was fixed months ago. Prices you see during the event are insulated from current freight conditions, so freight commentary during a sale window is largely noise as far as your purchase is concerned.

Extended producer responsibility and repairability rules continue to widen, and they interact with this week’s topic directly. Where regulation obliges manufacturers to supply spare parts for a defined period, the practical durability of a product can exceed its warranty considerably, and the parts obligation becomes a better signal than the coverage. In those markets, check the spare-parts commitment alongside the term; occasionally it is the longer of the two.

Repair-versus-replace policy varies more than buyers expect. Some brands replace on first claim, some repair, some replace with refurbished stock. A term is worth less if claims are settled with a refurbished unit whose own coverage runs only to the end of the original period, which is the standard arrangement and which quietly caps the total value of a long warranty.

Cross-border warranty transfer remains the weakest link. International coverage, where it exists, is often narrower than domestic coverage from the same brand, and the difference is rarely displayed side by side. If a brand publishes separate domestic and international warranty pages, read both; the gap between them is a useful measure of how seriously they take export customers.

Customs treatment of warranty returns deserves one practical note. Declare a repair shipment as a repair, keep the original documentation, and use the correct procedure for goods returning after processing. Getting this wrong turns a free repair into a taxable import, and the amount involved can exceed the value of the repair on mid-priced goods.

What’s Coming Next

The next fortnight is mechanical and mostly predictable. Event inventory is landing now or has already landed; the tournament opens on June 11 and will start reallocating attention and advertising budget the moment it does; the June sale runs the 23rd to the 26th. Between now and then, expect the pattern already in progress to intensify — flat prices, fuller boxes, more generous-looking coverage that is worth checking against the default term.

The specific thing to watch, and the angle we will take up next, is the inventory build itself, as it happens. For a month this series has read the consequences of stocking decisions after the fact. The build is visible in real time right now, in variant completeness, in cart-quantity limits, in how fast a listing restocks after it briefly goes out, and in which SKUs suddenly acquire deep availability at a frozen price. That is a supply-side read rather than a listing-forensics read, and it is the last one available before the event turns everything into noise.

After that, two topics remain unused and both are ripe: review-gating, meaning the ways a review sample can be censored before you ever see it, which pairs naturally with the point made here about reviews and warranties describing different time windows; and the local retail effects around host cities during the tournament, which is a genuinely different kind of story and one that only makes sense while the event is running.

Bottom line

It is the last day of May and there is nothing to buy, which makes it the right day to do the one piece of research that does not expire. Prices will move a dozen times before the end of June. The warranty will not, because changing it requires a reserve, and a reserve requires a number, and that number is the manufacturer’s private estimate of how often the thing breaks.

Read the deviation, not the length. A term that matches the category norm is silent; a term above it is the strongest positive signal a listing can carry; a term below it, in a category where longer coverage is standard and cheap to offer, is a confession made against interest. Read the exclusion list as a parts list, because every carve-out was paid for by claims data. Discount registration gates to the default term. And take the smaller of the printed term and the plausible remaining life of the company printing it.

Two things tie the tables together. The first is that “obligor risk” appears in the marketplace table and cannot appear in the direct-brand one — on a marketplace the coverage tells you mostly about the seller, and direct from a brand it tells you about the product. Same number, different author, and the author is the thing to establish first. Check who wrote the warranty before you read what it says. The second is that the strongest evidence in this guide is not any cross-brand comparison but the intra-brand kind: Grivel giving metal one term and textiles another, Triobike giving the frame a decade and the battery two years. Same company, same everything, different numbers — and only one variable left that can explain it.

Finally, the inversion, because it cuts against a month of advice here: this framework is at its best on cheap things. On a thirty-dollar item you will never make a claim, so the coverage is worth nothing as insurance — but the only real question is whether the thing lasts, and the warranty is the only free evidence you have. On a two-thousand-dollar item the coverage is worth real money and tells you comparatively little. Read warranties on cheap goods. Negotiate them on expensive ones. And spend this quiet Sunday reading, because in eleven days nobody will be able to hear themselves think.
