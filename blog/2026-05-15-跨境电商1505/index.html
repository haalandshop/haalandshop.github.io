Best Things to Buy Right Now — May 15, 2026

Quick take: Friday, twenty-seven days from the tournament and thirty-nine from the June sale. Yesterday’s audit went after everything that happens after the box is open — consumables, wear parts, servicing, forward compatibility — and made the case that a published spare-parts list is the most honest disclosure in retail. That audit stopped one step short. It priced what the object costs you while you own it. It never priced the moment you stop owning it. And that moment has a number attached, whether or not you ever look at it, because every durable object you buy is eventually sold, given away, or paid to be taken off your hands, and those three outcomes are separated by real money. Here is the part almost nobody uses: the second-hand market is the largest longitudinal durability study ever conducted, and it is public. Nothing else comes close. Manufacturer testing runs a few hundred hours in a lab. Review corpora capture the first ninety days of ownership and then go silent. Warranty data is private and structurally biased toward the claims a company chose to honour. But the used market is populated by hundreds of thousands of units that have already survived five, ten, twenty years in the hands of strangers who have no incentive to flatter the maker, who describe faults in blunt language because a misrepresented item gets returned, and who set prices against each other with actual money. It tells you what breaks, at what age, in what way, how much a survivor is worth, and whether anyone wants one at all. It costs nothing to read and you can read it before you buy. Twenty picks, sorted by what this thing will be worth, to whom, and in what condition, on the day you are finished with it.

What’s Hot This Week

Pre-event listings are thin, and the used market is not

Twenty-seven days out, new-goods pricing is in its usual holding pattern: sellers who intend to discount in June have already stopped discounting now, and the promotional slots that do exist are being spent on overhang rather than on anything people actually want. That much is familiar. What is less familiar, and much more useful, is that the used market does not observe the same calendar. Second-hand supply runs on the seller’s life events — moving, upgrading, a hobby abandoned, an estate cleared — and none of those synchronise with a retail promotional window. The practical consequence is that for the next four weeks the gap between a new unit and a good used one is at its widest point of the first half of the year, because new prices are being held artificially firm while used prices continue drifting on their own schedule. If your purchase is a category with a liquid secondary market, this is a genuinely good month to buy used and a poor month to buy new. If your purchase is a category with no secondary market, none of this applies and you should simply wait for June.

Trade-in programmes are quietly expanding, and that is a signal worth reading

Several categories have seen brands add or widen first-party trade-in and certified-refurbished channels over the past few months. The consumer framing is always the same — sustainability, circularity, keeping things in use. The commercial reality is that a maker only builds a trade-in channel when it believes its own used units are worth handling, and that belief is expensive to hold, because it means staffing an inspection line, stocking parts for models you no longer sell, and accepting a unit back at a published number rather than a negotiated one. A published trade-in value is a maker putting a floor under its own resale market with its own balance sheet. That is a much stronger statement than a warranty, which costs nothing until something fails. When you see a brand publish trade-in numbers, note the ratio to original price and note how far back the eligible model list goes. A programme that only accepts units from the last two generations is a marketing exercise. One that accepts anything the company ever made is a durability claim you can bank.

Depreciation is finally being talked about in categories that used to ignore it

For a long time, only cars, watches and cameras had a mainstream conversation about residual value. That is broadening. Bicycles, power tools, kitchen equipment, furniture and even garden machinery now have active enough secondary channels that buyers arrive already knowing roughly what a three-year-old unit fetches. This changes the maths of a first purchase more than people realise. If you know a given item retains half its price after five years, its true five-year cost is half the sticker, and it may be cheaper to own than a competitor at sixty percent of the price that retains nothing. Residual value is a discount you receive later, and it is frequently larger than any discount you can negotiate now. The June sale might move a price by fifteen percent. The difference between a product that holds fifty percent and one that holds five percent is thirty-five points of the purchase price, and no promotional calendar can match that.

Sinn 556 A and Teenage Engineering OP-XY are the cleanest illustration in this table of what a sold-listing history actually tells you, because both are desirable, both are well made, and their exit curves point in opposite directions. Search completed listings for the Sinn and you will find a dense band of transactions clustered inside a fairly narrow range, with the oldest examples from well over a decade ago selling for numbers that are recognisably close to what the newest ones fetch. That density is the important part, not the price level. A tight band across many years and many units means the market has reached a stable opinion about what the object is worth, and a stable opinion is very hard to dislodge — it survives model updates, it survives the brand having a quiet year, and it means that if you need to exit you will find a buyer at a number you can predict today. The OP-XY sits at the other end. Portable synthesizers from this maker are objects of real affection and the build quality is not in question, but the sold-listing record shows something specific and repeated: a steep step down within weeks of each new model announcement, followed by a slow drift rather than a recovery. That is not a quality signal. It is a market that prices these instruments on desirability rather than on capability, and desirability is transferred to the newest object by design. The read for you is that the Sinn’s exit is a known number you can plan around, while the OP-XY’s exit is a function of an announcement you cannot see coming. Neither makes the object worse. It changes when you should buy: the watch is fine to buy at any point in its cycle, the synthesizer is much better bought used, six months after whatever the current model is stops being current.

Brompton C Line and Gazelle Ultimate C380 put the same question to two bicycles and get answers separated by a single component. Search the Japanese market for used Bromptons and the striking thing is not the prices, which are strong, but the ages — folding bikes from the early two-thousands are listed as working, ridden, serviceable machines, frequently with a note about which parts have been replaced along the way. Twenty-plus years of continuous service showing up as ordinary inventory is about as strong a durability finding as any dataset can produce, and it exists because the machine is mechanical throughout, every wear item is a replaceable part, and nothing in it expires on a clock. The Gazelle is a genuinely excellent bicycle by every measure that applies to the bicycle itself, and Dutch city e-bikes are built to a standard that most markets never see. But run the same search on used electric models and the listings simply stop being common somewhere around year five or six. The frames have not failed and the motors have not failed. The battery has aged into a range that no longer does what the buyer needs, replacement packs for older models get discontinued or priced close to a large fraction of a new bike, and a used buyer who cannot be sure of getting a pack will not bid. One expiring component sets the resale life of the entire object, regardless of how well everything attached to it was made. If you want a bike you can sell in a decade, the mechanical one is the answer. If you want the e-bike, buy it understanding you are buying a consumable with a frame attached, and price it as though the residual is zero.

Klein Tools insulated screwdrivers and Juki sewing machines both have deep used markets, and the deep used market means opposite things for each. For the Klein set, search results are full of individual drivers sold as singles, replacement handles, and small odd-lot groupings — the tools circulate as components rather than as sets. That is a healthy signal for a buyer of new ones. It means the design is stable enough across decades that a single driver from an old set is interchangeable and worth listing on its own, it means you can replace one damaged item without rebuying a set, and it means the insulation rating on these is the kind of thing tradespeople replace individually and often, which is exactly how safety-rated tools should be treated. Buy the new set; you are entering a parts ecosystem, not just buying a box. The Juki reads differently precisely because its used market is so deep and so good. Domestic machines from this maker survive a very long time, the mechanism is serviceable by a wide network of independent technicians, and the consequence is a large standing inventory of serviced used heads at roughly half the price of new, often with a technician’s recent service note attached. A new machine has to justify itself against that, and for most home sewing use it does not — the feature gap between a current model and a well-kept older one is small, while the price gap is not. A category where used supply is abundant and genuinely good is a category where buying new is the expensive way in. That is not a criticism of the machines. It is the arithmetic of their own longevity working against the new unit.

Gitzo Systematic tripods and Vitra ID Mesh chairs are both premium objects with long service lives, and their used markets diverge because of who is selling. Search for used Systematic legs and you will find relatively few at any given moment, spread across a wide age range, and priced with surprising firmness. Thin supply plus steady demand is the classic profile of something owners do not part with — a tripod has no electronics to date it, no format to make it obsolete, and the only real wear items are the leg locks and feet, both replaceable. People keep them for thirty years and then their children sell them. Buying new here is defensible because the exit is strong and the object will outlast several cameras. The Vitra chair inverts this through no fault of its design. Premium task chairs enter the second-hand market in waves, from office clearances, downsizings and refits, and a wave means dozens of near-identical units hitting one region at once from a single seller who wants them gone by a deadline. The result is that excellent chairs trade for a small fraction of new, not because they are worn out — most have had light use and full service records — but because the supply curve is lumpy and the sellers are not price-sensitive individuals. For a buyer, this is an opportunity rather than a warning: wait for a clearance lot near you. It does mean that buying new, at full price, in a category whose used market is periodically flooded, is the one purchase pattern that captures none of the value.

Buck Knives 110 and Staub cocottes close the table with the two cases where the resale read leads somewhere counterintuitive. The Buck has essentially no meaningful resale market — it has been made in enormous numbers for decades, used examples are everywhere, and nobody is going to pay you much for yours. Under a strict residual-value framework that looks like a mark against it. It is not, and this is the limit of the framework worth stating plainly: when the purchase price is low enough, the exit value stops mattering, and optimising for it becomes a way of talking yourself out of good cheap things. The knife is inexpensive, repairable, backed by a service programme that will re-edge and rebuild it, and will very likely outlive your interest in it. Buy it and never think about its residual again. The Staub is the opposite error. Enamelled cast iron is superb cookware and lasts more or less indefinitely, which over several decades of high production has produced a used market with far more good units in it than there are buyers looking. Prices for second-hand pieces sit well under new, chips and crazing are cosmetic rather than functional in most cases, and the objects do not wear out fast enough to clear the inventory. Paying full retail for new enamelled cast iron means paying a large premium over an identical-performing used piece in a market that is not short of them. Wait, and buy second-hand — or wait for June, when this is one of the categories that reliably discounts.

Best Direct-to-Consumer and Brand Picks

The marketplace table read the second-hand market’s own data. This table reads something a marketplace structurally cannot show you: whether the maker participates in its own resale market. Does a serial number get you a service history? Will they service a model they stopped making? Does the warranty follow the object to its second owner, or die with the receipt? Is there a factory refurbishment path, a published trade-in number, a certified-used channel? Will they sell a wear part to someone who did not buy the item new? These are all disclosures — they exist only if the company chooses to make and keep them, they cost real money every year, and no reseller can answer them on the maker’s behalf. Red Oxx and Autodromo are both small, design-conscious, direct-selling brands, and the difference between them is entirely in what happens when an old unit comes back. Red Oxx will repair a bag it made at any point in its history, for whoever currently owns it, and the practical significance of “whoever currently owns it” is easy to underrate. A warranty tied to the original purchaser is a benefit that evaporates at the moment of resale, which means it adds nothing to what a second-hand buyer will pay, which means it adds nothing to your exit. A repair commitment that follows the object instead of the receipt does the opposite: it makes a fifteen-year-old bag a rational thing for a stranger to buy, because the stranger inherits the same recourse you had. That is why old examples of this luggage trade briskly. Autodromo makes genuinely lovely watches with a coherent point of view, and this is not a comment on the design or the assembly. It is a structural observation about small watch brands generally: limited runs, movements sourced from suppliers, and no standing factory service department mean that a piece from an older run is difficult to get serviced through the maker, and a watch that cannot be reliably serviced is a watch the second-hand market discounts heavily. The exit here is soft, and it is soft for reasons the brand would need considerable scale to fix. Buy one because you want to wear it for a long time, not because you expect to recover much.

Ophidian and Hasselblad pose the same question to two very different price points and reveal that the split runs along the analogue-digital line rather than the cheap-expensive one. Ophidian is a small British speaker maker that will sell you a replacement driver directly, including to someone who bought the speakers second-hand. Loudspeakers are close to an ideal resale object to begin with — the cabinet is inert, the crossover components age slowly and are replaceable, and the drivers are the only real wear item — so a maker that keeps drivers available converts a pair of speakers into something with an indefinite service life and, consequently, a real used value. Hasselblad’s medium format bodies are extraordinary instruments and the optics are the kind of thing people keep for careers. The problem is the body, and it is a problem shared across all digital imaging: the sensor, processor and firmware age on a technology curve rather than a wear curve, support for a discontinued body winds down long before the shutter is tired, and the used market prices that reality in with unusual brutality. The glass holds its value. The body does not, and it is the expensive half. Nothing about this is unique to the brand — it is what digital capture does to residuals everywhere — but if you are buying into the system, the exit-aware version of the purchase is to spend on lenses and buy the body a generation behind.

Seek Outside and Sixpenny are both direct sellers making things intended to last, and they diverge on whether there is any route back to the maker. Seek Outside will take in a shelter that is years old, repair it, re-seam it, replace zips and panels, and send it back, and that is why used examples of their tipis are traded actively among people who know exactly what they are getting. A repairable fabric shelter with a factory that still repairs it does not really depreciate so much as it changes hands. Sixpenny makes comfortable, well-regarded sofas sold direct with slipcovers that can be replaced, which is a real advantage over most of the category, and the point here is not about build quality. It is that upholstered furniture as a class has almost no organised route back to its maker: no reupholstery programme, no transferable coverage, no frame-only service, and a shipping cost profile that makes sending anything back economically absurd. Combine that with a used market where buyers are reasonably wary of second-hand upholstery, and the exit is close to nil for essentially every brand in the category, including the good ones. The honest framing is that you should buy a sofa expecting to consume it entirely, and choose on comfort and slipcover availability rather than on any hope of residual value.

Council Tool and Kirby both live in categories with vigorous rebuild cultures, and the question is who is doing the rebuilding. Council Tool sells handles, wedges and hardware separately, which means an axe that has been hung for thirty years and finally cracked a handle is a twenty-minute job rather than a replacement purchase, and it means the head — the part that carries the value — has an indefinite life. Heads from this maker turn up used and get rehung routinely, which is exactly the behaviour that supports a resale price. Kirby is a more interesting case and deserves care, because the machines themselves are famously durable and the rebuild market around them is enormous. That is the issue. The rebuild economy exists almost entirely outside the maker, run by independent shops who acquire old units, replace bearings, belts and cosmetics, and sell them at a fraction of new. A buyer’s realistic alternative to a new machine is therefore a professionally rebuilt one at a small share of the price, doing the same job, from a shop that will service it again later. That is a compliment to how the machine was engineered and not a criticism of it — the durability is precisely what created the competing supply. It still means the new purchase is hard to justify on numbers, and it means your own exit will be into a market already saturated with refurbished units.

La Pavoni and Joolz close on the two clearest cases of a maker either extending or capping the life of its own object. La Pavoni still stocks parts for lever machines made in the nineteen-seventies. Gaskets, pistons, group components, seals — the consumable set for a fifty-year-old machine is orderable. The consequence is visible in the used market, where those fifty-year-old machines are not curiosities but working espresso makers changing hands at prices that would embarrass most five-year-old electronics, because a buyer knows the thing can be kept alive indefinitely. That is the strongest exit story in either table, and it comes from a parts shelf rather than from any marketing claim. Joolz makes a well-designed stroller and this verdict is not about quality either. Strollers as a category run into something no maker can engineer around: guidance and retailer policy limit the useful second-hand life of child-safety equipment, harnesses and fabrics have service-life expectations, and the buyer pool for a used unit is precisely the group most cautious about used safety gear. The residual is capped by the category, not the product. The rational response is to buy the stroller you actually want for the two or three years you need it, treat the exit as a small bonus rather than a plan, and check whether the brand publishes a service-life figure before assuming a long one.

How to Read a Resale Market in Ten Minutes

The whole exercise above takes far less time than it sounds, because you are not doing research so much as looking at a page and noticing its shape. Three reads, in order, and you can do all three on a phone while standing in a shop.

Read the sold listings, not the asking prices

Active listings tell you what optimistic sellers hope for. Sold and completed listings tell you what buyers actually paid, and the difference between the two is frequently enormous — in slow categories the asking prices can sit at double the clearing price for months. Every major marketplace exposes a completed or sold filter somewhere, and it is the single most valuable free data source in consumer buying. What you want from it is not one number but the shape of the distribution. A tight cluster means a settled market and a predictable exit. A wide scatter means condition and provenance dominate, so your exit will depend heavily on how well you look after the thing and how well you document it. A cluster with a long tail of very low sales usually means there is a common failure mode producing broken units, and it is worth finding out what it is before you buy new.

Read the age of the oldest working unit on offer

Filter for the category and sort or scan for the oldest examples described as functional. This is the closest thing to a survival curve that a consumer can access. If a category routinely shows working twenty-year-old units, the design is durable and the parts supply held. If listings thin out sharply after year five, something expires — a battery, a proprietary consumable, a firmware dependency, a discontinued part — and you should find out which before assuming the product is a long-term purchase. The absence of old units is as informative as their presence, with one caveat worth holding: a product line that is genuinely new cannot show you old examples, so check the age of the category or the maker’s earlier models rather than concluding the worst about something that has only existed for three years.

Read the condition language sellers choose

Second-hand descriptions are unpolished failure-mode documentation. Sellers describe the specific fault in front of them in plain words, because overstating condition gets an item returned and a rating damaged. Scan twenty listings in a category and the same phrases start repeating — a particular joint that loosens, a coating that wears through at one spot, a switch that gets intermittent, a seal that hardens. Those repeated phrases are the real reliability report, assembled at no cost by people with no relationship to the manufacturer and no reason to be diplomatic. They will tell you more about what to inspect, what to baby and which spare to buy in advance than any published specification. It is worth noting the one bias: sellers are disproportionately people who had a reason to part with the item, so faults are over-represented relative to the whole population. Read the frequency of a phrase, not its mere presence.

The Two Shapes of a Depreciation Curve

Almost every consumer object depreciates in one of two shapes, and telling them apart at purchase time is worth more than any other single piece of buying analysis. The first shape is a cliff followed by a plateau: a steep loss in the first year or two, after which the price stops falling and stays roughly flat for a very long time. This is what mechanical, repairable, format-independent objects do — hand tools, mechanical watches, acoustic instruments, cast iron, quality bicycles. The plateau exists because the object has reached its floor as a useful thing, and utility does not decay further. The second shape is a glide to zero: a gentle, relentless decline that never flattens, ending at scrap. This is what anything on a technology or consumable clock does — computing hardware, battery-powered anything, appliances with proprietary electronics, devices tied to a service. The glide never flattens because the object’s utility is genuinely draining away rather than merely being fashionable.

The practical rule that falls out of this is clean. If the curve is a cliff-and-plateau, buy used and hold, because someone else has already paid the cliff and the plateau is where the value lives. You will own it for a decade and sell it for close to what you paid. If the curve is a glide to zero, buy the cheapest thing that meets the requirement, buy it as late in its cycle as your patience allows, and plan for a residual of nothing. The catastrophic purchase pattern is to buy a glide-to-zero object at full price on the assumption that quality confers value retention. It does not. Quality determines how long the object works, which is a different property from what someone will pay you for it, and only the plateau shape converts one into the other.

There is a diagnostic that separates the two shapes before you have any price history to look at, and it takes one question: can this object become obsolete while still working perfectly? If yes, it is on the glide. If the only thing that can end its life is physical wear, and physical wear can be repaired, it is heading for a plateau. Notice that this question has nothing to do with materials, price, brand reputation or country of manufacture, which are the four things buyers usually reach for when guessing at durability. A beautifully made object with a firmware dependency is on the glide. A crude, ugly, cheaply finished mechanical thing with standard fasteners is on the plateau. The market is not making an aesthetic judgement when it prices these; it is pricing the answer to that one question, and it happens to be much better at asking it than most buyers are.

Categories With No Exit at All

Some things simply cannot be resold, and being clear-eyed about which is a kindness to your own budget. Mattresses and upholstered furniture have hygiene resistance and shipping economics that kill the market. Child safety equipment has service-life limits. Anything with a personalised fit — custom orthotics, prescription optics, bespoke tailoring — has a buyer pool of one. Consumables and part-used goods are obvious. Built-in appliances carry removal and reinstallation costs that frequently exceed the unit’s used value. Large low-value items of any kind fail on freight: if it costs more to ship than it is worth, its market is whoever lives within driving distance this month, which is close to nobody.

The right response is not to avoid these categories, which would be absurd, but to stop applying resale logic to them and switch to a pure cost-per-year calculation instead. For a no-exit category, the entire purchase price is consumed over the service life, so the only question is price divided by expected years of use, adjusted for how much you will enjoy those years. That reframing changes decisions. It usually argues for spending more on the things you touch daily and use for a decade, and rather less on things you will use occasionally, because with no residual to recover, frequency of use is the only lever that improves the ratio. It also means the June sale matters much more in these categories than in the plateau ones, since a discount at purchase is the only discount you will ever get.

Worth adding a fourth outcome to the three at the top of this piece, because it is the one people forget to budget for: some objects cost money to leave. Bulky furniture, old appliances, mattresses and anything containing a battery or refrigerant frequently carry a collection or recycling charge at end of life, and in several markets that charge is now formalised through producer-responsibility schemes rather than left to the local tip. A disposal fee is a negative residual, and it belongs in the cost-per-year calculation with a minus sign in front of it. It is rarely large enough to change a decision on its own, but it does close the gap between two options more often than you would expect, and it quietly punishes the strategy of buying the biggest version of something on the grounds that bigger is better value. Bigger is also heavier to get rid of.

The Battery Clock and Other Residual Killers

A handful of specific components have an outsized ability to destroy the resale value of an otherwise excellent object, and they are worth knowing by name. The battery is the worst offender, because it ages whether used or not, its replacement is often priced as a large fraction of the whole product, and pack availability for older models tends to be discontinued while the host is still perfectly good. The proprietary consumable is second: a filter, cartridge, bag or pod that only one company makes gives that company the unilateral ability to end your object’s life by discontinuing a small plastic part. Third is the account or service dependency, where the object requires a working server to function; a used buyer cannot verify how long that will be true and will discount accordingly. Fourth is the discontinued proprietary fastener or connector, which sounds trivial until a single unavailable part makes an entire assembly unserviceable.

The read at purchase time is to ask a simple question about each: who else is allowed to supply this? If the answer is more than one company, the risk is manageable and the residual survives. If the answer is exactly one, the object’s resale life is whatever that one company decides it will be, and you should assume the shorter end of the range. This is the argument for open standards restated in financial terms rather than philosophical ones — an open consumable does not just save you money while you own the thing, it is the difference between an exit and a disposal fee.

The version of this that catches careful buyers out is the partially proprietary design, where most of the object is standard and one small component is not. A machine built almost entirely from generic bearings, belts and fasteners still dies permanently if its one custom-moulded housing is unavailable, and the ninety-five percent that was thoughtfully specified provides no protection whatsoever. Resale markets are good at finding these single points of failure, which is why you will sometimes see two superficially similar products with wildly different used prices. The cheaper one usually has a well-known unobtainable part, and the used listings will name it. That is another reason to read condition descriptions before buying new: the fault the second-hand market complains about most is the component that decides how long your unit gets to exist.

Does the Maker Show Up in Its Own Second-Hand Market

The single strongest predictor of whether an object holds value is whether its manufacturer engages with used units at all, and there are four escalating levels of engagement worth distinguishing. Level one is selling parts to anyone, without requiring proof of original purchase. This is the minimum and it is not universal. Level two is servicing discontinued models, which requires keeping technical knowledge and part stock alive for products that generate no new revenue. Level three is transferable coverage, where a warranty or repair commitment follows the object rather than the receipt, directly adding to what a second owner will pay. Level four is a first-party used channel — certified refurbishment, published trade-in values, a factory-restored line sold alongside new.

Each level costs the maker more than the last and each adds more to the residual than the last. What makes this a useful buying test is that all four are checkable in about three minutes on the company’s own site, and none of them can be faked, because they all require ongoing operational commitment. A brand that talks about heirloom quality but will not sell a gasket to a second owner has told you exactly where its commitment ends. A brand that publishes what it will pay you for a fifteen-year-old unit has told you the opposite, in numbers, with money behind it.

What Selling Actually Nets You After Fees and Freight

A residual value you cannot realise is not a residual value, and the gap between the headline used price and what lands in your account is larger than most people expect. Marketplace selling fees typically take a meaningful slice of the sale price, payment processing takes another, and if the platform requires or strongly encourages free shipping, you are absorbing the freight as well. For a physically large or heavy item, that freight can consume most of the margin — which is precisely why heavy things depreciate faster than light things of the same quality, and why the used market for anything awkward is stubbornly local.

Two practical implications. First, when you are estimating an exit value at purchase time, apply a haircut to the observed sold prices rather than using them directly, and make it a bigger haircut for anything bulky. Second, this asymmetry is an argument for buying used in heavy categories and buying new in light ones. The seller of a heavy item is fighting freight economics and will accept less, so the buyer captures an unusually large discount; the seller of a small dense valuable item nets most of the sale price, so used prices stay firm and the discount for buying second-hand is thinner. Freight cost per unit of value is an underrated variable in both directions, and it explains most of the cases where a used market looks irrationally cheap or irrationally expensive.

Where the Resale Read Breaks Down

This framework has real limits and it is worth being honest about them before anyone over-applies it. It fails on genuinely new products, which have no secondary history to read and therefore look bad by default despite possibly being excellent. It fails in categories where the used market is thin for reasons of geography rather than quality — a superb regional brand may have no visible resale market simply because it never sold in volume where the marketplaces are. It is distorted by fashion, which can hold prices up on objects that are not durable and depress them on objects that are. It over-weights the experience of people who sold, who are by definition not the people who kept theirs happily.

Most importantly, it can quietly turn into a reason to buy the wrong thing. Optimising for residual value pushes you toward whatever the market has decided is collectible, and collectibility and usefulness are only loosely correlated. If you find yourself choosing a product you like less because its resale is stronger, the framework has stopped serving you. The exit number is a tiebreaker between things you would be happy to own, not a criterion for choosing what to own. Used properly it tells you when to buy new versus used, when to wait for a model cycle, and which categories deserve a bigger up-front budget. Used improperly it turns shopping into speculation, which is a considerably worse hobby.

Cross-Border News, Policy and Logistics

The logistics backdrop into June is mostly about capacity discipline rather than any single rule change. Carriers have been managing space to hold rates rather than chasing volume, which keeps freight costs from falling even as demand sits in its pre-event lull, and that firmness passes through to landed cost on anything bulky. The practical effect for a buyer is that heavy goods will discount less than light goods in the June event, because the seller’s cost floor is higher and less flexible. Expect the best percentage discounts on small, dense, high-value items and comparatively thin ones on furniture, appliances and anything shipped as freight.

On the returns side, the pre-event period is when the mismatch between order date and return window is most likely to catch people. Buying now against a June price-protection expectation means your return clock starts today while the event sits inside or beyond the window, so verify the policy’s return anchor before treating a pre-event purchase as risk-free. For cross-border orders specifically, the practical advice is to build in a buffer: customs timing is the variable you cannot control, and a delivery that lands a week late against a thirty-day window leaves considerably less room to inspect and decide than the headline number suggests. Where a marketplace offers extended holiday-style windows around major events, that extension is usually the single most valuable term in the transaction and it is worth timing a purchase around.

Amazon, Shopify and TikTok: Where the Breakouts Are Forming

The breakout pattern most visible this month is products that come with an explicit second-life story, and it is showing up differently on each channel. On the large marketplaces, the winners are items whose listings surface refurbishment, certified-used or open-box variants alongside the new one — buyers increasingly click through to compare, and a listing that offers both captures the buyer who would otherwise leave to search elsewhere. On merchant-owned storefronts, the movement is in trade-in and buy-back widgets appearing directly on product pages, quoting a number for what the brand will pay you later. That is a conversion tool, but it is also a commitment, and the brands willing to publish it are seeing it work as a differentiator against competitors who cannot.

On short-form video and livestream commerce, the strongest-performing content in durable categories has shifted toward longevity demonstrations — the multi-year-old unit still working, the repair performed on camera, the parts drawer opened. This plays well because it is the one claim that cannot be staged in a thirty-second product shot, and audiences have become fluent at spotting the difference. For anyone buying rather than selling, the useful inference is that a brand investing in this kind of content is exposing itself to scrutiny it would avoid if the product did not hold up. It is weak evidence on its own and strong evidence in combination with a parts list and a service policy. Treat the video as the prompt to go and check the two things that are actually verifiable.

Category Notes

Watches and small mechanical goods. The plateau shape in its purest form. Steel sports watches from established makers with real service networks are close to the best residual performers in consumer goods, and the reason is entirely structural: serviceable indefinitely, no format dependency, no battery, and a global market of buyers. Microbrands are a different proposition — often better value new, considerably weaker on exit, and dependent on a service story that small companies find expensive to maintain.

Bicycles and mobility. Split hard along the electrification line. Mechanical bicycles hold value for decades and every wear part is standard. Electric ones are governed by pack availability, and the honest way to buy one is as a five-to-seven-year consumable. This is not an argument against e-bikes, which solve problems no mechanical bike can; it is an argument against paying a premium for one on durability grounds.

Audio. Passive loudspeakers are among the quietest good performers in resale, because the failure surface is small and drivers are replaceable. Anything with a streaming service dependency or a proprietary app is on the glide curve regardless of how good it sounds. If you want audio equipment with an exit, prefer passive, prefer analogue interfaces, and prefer makers who sell drivers.

Kitchen and cookware. Enamelled cast iron, carbon steel and stainless are all so durable that their own used markets undercut new pricing substantially. Buy these second-hand without hesitation. Small electrical kitchen appliances are the opposite: sealed, cheap to make, expensive to repair, no used market worth the freight.

Tools and outdoor equipment. Consistently strong, because buyers are experienced, condition is easy to assess, and parts culture is deep. Hand tools in particular hold value so well that the used market is where most professionals shop. Fabric goods depend almost entirely on whether the maker repairs them.

Furniture and large household goods. Freight economics dominate everything else. Design pieces with recognised names hold value in metropolitan areas and nowhere else; the same object two hours from a city is worth what someone with a van will pay. Upholstery has no exit. Solid timber does, if it can be moved.

Photography and imaging. The sharpest internal split of any category. Lenses behave like mechanical goods and hold value for decades because optical formulas do not become obsolete and mounts can be adapted. Bodies behave like computers. Support windows are the binding constraint rather than shutter life, and a body two generations old is usually worth a fraction of a lens bought at the same time for the same money. Buy glass first, buy bodies late and cheap, and treat tripods, heads and cases as near-permanent purchases.

Appliances, large and small. Large appliances suffer from installation and removal costs that swamp any residual, and from control boards that fail long after the mechanical parts are fine. Small appliances are usually sealed. The exception in both sizes is anything with a commercial or semi-professional lineage, where the parts supply is maintained for business customers and consumers get to free-ride on it. If a model number appears in a commercial catalogue, its used market will be noticeably healthier than its domestic siblings.

What’s Coming Next

Seven audits now, and each has taken a different layer of the purchase apart: the comparison set, the reviews, the specifications, the photographs, the counterparty, the record itself, the ownership costs, and now the exit. Together they cover the full financial arc of an object from the moment it enters your consideration to the moment it leaves your possession. There is one thing all eight have in common, and it is time to say it out loud: every single one of them assumed you had already decided to buy something. Every audit optimised a purchase that was treated as given. Not one of them asked the prior question, which is whether the object should be bought at all, and that question has a real methodology behind it rather than being a moral lecture about consumption.

Next: the audit of the decision itself. How to tell a requirement from a preference from an anticipated identity, why the strongest predictor of regret is the reason for buying rather than the thing bought, what the borrow-rent-buy ladder actually costs at each rung once you include the exit numbers established today, and how to price the option value of waiting when the item is not going anywhere. The reading axis for the tables will be the one that comes before all the others: what would happen if you simply did not buy this, and what is that outcome worth.

Bottom line

The purchase price is the number you negotiate, the ownership cost is the number that accumulates, and the exit value is the number that quietly decides whether the whole thing was expensive or cheap. It is the only one of the three you can research using a large sample of real outcomes from strangers, for free, before spending anything — and almost nobody looks. Three reads get you most of the way: the sold-listing distribution, the age of the oldest working unit, and the vocabulary sellers use to describe faults. Then one question to the maker’s own site: will you sell a part, service an old model, honour coverage for a second owner, or publish what you would pay me back. Objects that depreciate to a plateau should be bought used and kept; objects that glide to zero should be bought cheap and late and written off entirely. Getting that single distinction right will save you more money over a decade than every promotional calendar combined — and it will do it while you own better things, for longer, and hand them on to someone who wanted them.
