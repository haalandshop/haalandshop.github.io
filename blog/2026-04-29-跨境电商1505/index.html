Best Things to Buy Right Now — April 29, 2026

Quick take: Last week the argument was that the best-documented object is usually the one being discontinued, and that the fix is to ask one question before you click — can this thing be kept alive without the maker that is about to stop standing behind it. That audit still holds, and it points directly at the thing it kept dodging: the answer to whether an object survives its maker is almost never the object, it is the parts. A discontinued amplifier is safe because its capacitors are catalogued and standard; a discontinued camera is a tombstone because its electronics are proprietary and gone. So the real unit of analysis is not the whole, it is the component. The component catalogue you were told to open is the actual product you are buying, and most shoppers never read it, because it lives one tab past the review they did read, and the review felt complete. This week the question rotates off the maker and onto the part: which individual components end this object’s life, are those components named, are they in stock, and could a stranger source them if the maker vanished. The answers sort the entire catalogue into two piles that no star average can show you. On one side are objects whose fatal parts are open, standard, and stocked — a food processor whose bowl and motor are listed individually, a table saw whose brake cartridge and fence are orderable, a drivetrain built from parts any shop carries, a lens with no firmware to expire. On the other side are objects whose fatal part is a lock only the maker holds — a proprietary battery pack, a cloud-bound module, a young brand whose spare supply has not been built yet, a value import whose parts sit on a boat you cannot track. The whole may look identical across the two piles. The part is the difference, and the part is what you will be hunting for at 2 a.m. in year four, which is exactly why nobody shops for it at minute two. The discipline this week is to buy the component before you buy the object, because the object is only as good as the part you can still get when the maker has moved on.

What’s Hot This Week

Three movements are converging on the same shelf, and the thread joining them is that each one makes the individual component more visible, more contested, or more decisive than it was a quarter ago. The late-spring restock is landing, the pre-summer launch season is opening, and the makers who want your trust are increasingly publishing what used to be hidden — the parts list. Together they produce a storefront where the question of whether a thing can be kept alive has quietly moved from a footnote to a feature, and where the shopper who learns to read a component catalogue outperforms the one who reads only the spec sheet, because the spec sheet describes the object on day one and the parts list describes the object in year five.

Makers are publishing component catalogues as a trust signal

The most interesting shift this season is not a discount, it is a disclosure. A growing set of makers — across appliances, tools, optics, and bikes — are surfacing individual components with part numbers, prices, and stock status, where two years ago that information lived behind a service desk or did not exist at all. The motive is partly reputational: in a market where durability is a selling point, naming the part you can replace is cheaper than claiming the thing lasts forever. The effect on the shopper is real. A parts page turns a vague promise of longevity into a yes-or-no question you can answer in five minutes, and the maker who publishes it is, whether intentionally or not, confessing how long the object was meant to live. The interesting second-order move is that the catalogue is becoming the differentiator: two near-identical blenders sit side by side, and the one whose seals, blades, and coupling are individually orderable is a different purchase than the one sold only as a sealed unit, even when the spec sheets match. This week the disclosure is the product, and the shopper who reads it first shops a different catalogue than the one who does not.

The pre-summer launch wave is burying the spare-part story under the new-model story

The other force is the opposite of disclosure. The run-up to summer is when the new models arrive, and the launch machinery is built to tell you what is new, not what is serviceable. A refreshed laptop, a redesigned appliance, a new bike group — each is marketed on the feature that changed, never on the part that will eventually fail, which means the most important document about the object is the one the launch deliberately does not show you. A new model and its outgoing sibling often share the same fatal component, so the upgrade you are being sold may change nothing about the part you will hunt for later, while the price you pay for the newness buys you no extra years. The platforms compound this by sorting on newness and price, which structurally hides the parts page three tabs deep behind the hero shot. The workaround is unglamorous: treat any object priced as a launch as a parts suspect by default, then go find the component catalogue before you trust the feature list, because the feature list was written to keep you from looking.

Right-to-repair pressure is quietly widening the open-part pool

The third movement is slower but more structural. The spread of right-to-repair expectations, and the practical pressure it puts on makers to supply components and manuals, is gradually widening the pool of objects whose fatal parts are obtainable from someone other than the original maker. This does not flip every category overnight, and it does not help the cloud-dependent gadget at all, but it does change the baseline for the mechanical and analogue durables that dominate a sane shopping list. A standard seal, a named bearing, a stocked circuit board, a reusable sole — these are drifting from maker-only scarcity toward a normal supply you can plan around. The practical upshot for this week: the open-part object is becoming easier to verify and the proprietary-lock object is becoming easier to spot, because the contrast between a maker who lists parts and a maker who will not is sharper than it was, and the shopper who reads both sides sees the lock immediately. The spare-part audit is the lens this week hides best precisely because the disclosure is new, partial, and buried, which is exactly why it is the one worth applying before the launch noise drowns it.

The Component Catalogue Is the Real Object

There is a reason the parts list, not the spec sheet, deserves to be the first document you open, and it is not sentiment. The spec sheet describes the object as it leaves the factory; the parts list describes the object as it fails, which is the only version of the object you will actually own for long. A product is not a single thing you buy once. It is a small system of components, most of which will outlast the warranty and one or two of which will end the object’s useful life the moment they fail and cannot be replaced. The object you receive is the sum of those parts; the object you keep is the subset you can still source. So the real purchase is not the assembled unit, it is the catalogue of components that lets you keep it, and learning to read that catalogue turns the vague hope that something lasts into a question with an answer.

The mistake almost everyone makes is to evaluate the whole and ignore the part. A blender is reviewed on power, a saw on cut, a bike on ride, a lens on sharpness — all properties of the object on day one, none of them properties of the object in year four. The review that matters most is the one nobody writes, which is the owner who reports that the coupling failed at month thirty and the seal was a five-dollar order, or that the proprietary module died and the maker had moved on. The first owner is describing the object you see; the second owner is describing the catalogue you are actually buying. The spare-part audit simply insists that the second owner is the more useful one, because you will become the second owner whether the reviewer did or not, and the difference between a five-dollar fix and a landfill is entirely contained in the parts list the first owner never mentioned.

The reason this reframes the whole purchase is that it moves the decision from the showroom to the service desk without your leaving the page. You do not need to know how to repair anything. You need to know, before you click, whether the thing that will eventually break can be replaced by anyone, and the parts page answers that in minutes. A maker that lists individual components with numbers and prices is selling you the right to keep the object; a maker that sells the object but not its internals is selling you a timer you cannot reset.

Owning the Part Changes the Ownership

The single most useful reframe available to a shopper this week is to stop thinking of a purchase as owning an object and start thinking of it as owning the right to the parts that keep the object alive. Those are not the same thing. An object can be yours in full and still be unrecoverable, because ownership of the assembly means nothing if the component that ends it is rented from the maker. Conversely, an object can be old, discounted, and out of warranty and still be fully yours, because every part that matters is open and stocked and the maker’s involvement ended the day you bought it. The distinction lives entirely in the parts, and almost none of it appears on the listing.

A part you can own changes the ownership in four concrete ways. It changes the timeline, because a stocked component turns a failure from a dead end into a Tuesday errand. It changes the leverage, because a maker who knows you can source the part elsewhere cannot charge you a hostage fee for the one they control. It changes the resale, because an object with a known, obtainable fatal part is worth more used than an identical object whose failure dooms it, and the used market prices this whether the listing admits it or not. And it changes the psychology, because owning the part removes the quiet dread that accompanies every proprietary-lock object — the knowledge that the thing you paid for can be ended by a component you were never allowed to hold. None of these four properties is in the spec table. All four are in the parts catalogue, which is why the catalogue, not the object, is the document that decides what you own.

The reason this matters more for a cross-border shopper is that the part is where the border lives twice. A component sourced domestically arrives on a normal shipment with a normal return; a component sourced only from the maker’s home market may sit in customs, in a language you cannot read, on a lead time the maker will not quote. So the open-part object is not only more survivable, it is more survivable across a border, because the third-party supply that saves it domestically is the same supply that saves it internationally. The proprietary-lock object is the opposite: its only source is the maker, and the maker’s home-market parts desk is exactly the desk that is hardest to reach from another continent.

The Spare-Part Test Decides the Verdict

If the audit reduces to one procedure, it is the spare-part test, and it is boring enough that almost nobody does it and decisive enough that it should be mandatory. Before trusting any long-term review or any launch feature, name the single component most likely to end the object’s life, then try to buy that part on its own. Not the whole, not a service plan, not a promise — the specific component, with a part number, a price, and a stock status. If you can find it and order it, the object is a buy on durability grounds regardless of what the review said. If you cannot, the object is a wait, regardless of how glowing the review is, because the review describes the object on day one and the missing part describes the object in year four, and year four is the only version you will own for long.

The test has a sharp second half that most shoppers skip. Finding the part is not enough; you must also check who sells it. A component listed only on the maker’s site, with no third party and no cross-compatible equivalent, is a part you can buy today and may not be able to buy in three years, which is to say it is a temporary lock dressed up as a catalogue. A component listed by the maker, stocked by third parties, and cross-compatible with equivalents from other makers is a part you effectively own forever, because the supply does not depend on any single company’s interest. The test therefore grades on a curve: stocked-and-open beats stocked-and-maker-only beats listed-but-out-of-stock beats absent. Most objects land in the bottom two, which is why the test weeds out so much of what looks safe, and why the small set that lands in the top band is worth the premium it sometimes costs.

There is a timing dimension worth naming, because it changes which verdict you land on. The spare-part test is most punishing at the launch and most forgiving at the clearance, for the simple reason that a new object’s parts supply has not been proven over time while an old object’s has. A just-released gadget may list parts that the maker has not actually stocked at volume yet; a discontinued tool has either proven its parts flow or exposed its absence over years of owner reports. So the test is not a single click but a habit: run it on the new thing you are tempted by, and run it again on the old thing you are wary of, and you will often find the wary old object is the safer buy precisely because its parts story is the one that has been lived. The launch hides the part; the clearance reveals it; the test is how you read both honestly.

Standard Parts Versus Proprietary Locks

The cleanest split in the entire audit is between the standard part and the proprietary lock, and learning to see it turns a confusing catalogue into a binary. A standard part is built to a specification the industry shares: a seal size, a bearing grade, a bolt pattern, a lens mount, a battery format, a coupling thread. Anyone can make it, anyone can stock it, and the maker’s exit changes nothing because the supply never depended on the maker. A proprietary lock is a part the maker designed to be unique: a custom module, a cloud-bound chip, a battery that fits nothing else, a sole only the maker sells, a firmware that expires. The maker’s exit ends the supply, because the supply was the maker. The object may be identical on the spec sheet; the part is the whole difference, and the part is what you will be hunting for when the warranty is a memory.

The trap is that proprietary locks are frequently marketed as features. A custom battery that fits only this device is sold as integration; a cloud module that enables a function is sold as intelligence; a sole available only from the maker is sold as authenticity. Each of those may be a real benefit on day one. None of them is a benefit in year four, when the custom battery is discontinued, the cloud function is retired, and the sole is out of stock, and the object you liked is now a paperweight the maker no longer supports. The audit does not argue that proprietary design is evil — only that it converts a property of the object into a rental from the maker, and that the rental ends when the maker’s interest ends, which is a fact no feature list discloses. The shopper who separates the day-one feature from the year-four lock owns the first and rents the second, and deserves to know which is which before paying.

The practical upshot is that the open-part object is almost always the better long-term buy even when it costs more up front, because the premium you did not pay is the premium you will pay later to replace the whole when one standard part would have fixed it. A food processor whose coupling is a five-dollar order outlives a cheaper sealed unit by years; a saw whose brake cartridge is stocked beats a cheaper saw whose proprietary cartridge is gone; a drivetrain built from standard parts beats a prettier integrated one whose unique link is unavailable. The spare-part audit is not anti-premium and not pro-cheap. It is pro-open, because the open part is the one component that lets the object outlive the transaction, and the closed part is the one component that guarantees it will not.

The Part Economy That Outlives the Maker

The deepest version of the audit is to notice that the object’s survival is rarely the maker’s achievement — it is the part economy’s. A welted boot outlives its maker because cobblers and sole suppliers exist independently of any boot company. A manual lens outlives its maker because the mount is a shared standard and the glass is serviceable anywhere. A standard drivetrain outlives its maker because the bearing, the link, the cable are catalogued by hundreds of sellers. In each case the maker built the object, but the part economy keeps it, and the part economy is the durable institution the shopper is actually buying into when they buy the open-part object. The maker can close; the economy does not, because it was never one company.

This is why the audit rewards objects whose fatal parts sit inside a large, boring, durable supply chain rather than inside a single ambitious one. A common seal lives in a warehouse full of seals; a proprietary module lives only in the maker’s. A standard battery format is made by a dozen firms; a custom cell is made by one, and only while the product is current. The shopper who buys the seal is buying into the seal industry, which will outlast the product and the maker both. The shopper who buys the module is buying into the maker’s continuation, which is a bet no listing invites you to price. The part economy is the quiet underwriter of every durable object, and the objects worth keeping are the ones it underwrites, because when the maker leaves, the economy stays, and the object stays with it.

The useful habit this builds is to read the parts list as a map of dependencies, not a menu of replacements. Every component on the page is a line of dependence: on the maker, on a third party, on a standard, on a sole source. The object worth keeping is the one whose map is short and whose dependencies point outward to durable supplies rather than inward to a single company. A simple blade has almost no map and almost no dependence, which is why it lasts generations. A cloud gadget has a map of one — the maker — and a dependence that ends when the maker’s server does. The audit does not require you to repair anything. It requires you to look at the map once, before you pay, and notice whether the lines lead to an economy that will outlive you or to a company that may not outlive the warranty. The part economy that outlives the maker is the only insurance the object ever really has.

Where This Read Breaks Down

This lens is not universal, and applying it everywhere would be its own error. It breaks down first on genuinely consumable goods, where there is no part worth stocking because the object is meant to be used up — a serum, a cartridge, a filter you replace by design. Buying the discontinued formula of a consumable is a reformulation risk, not a parts risk, and the two should not be confused; the spare-part audit simply does not apply, because the only component that matters is the one you are meant to throw away. It breaks down on fast-improving categories, where the newer object is genuinely better and the old part’s availability is a trap, not a virtue — a discontinued laptop with stocked parts may still be a worse buy than the current one, because the part that matters is the silicon, and the silicon moved on. It breaks down hardest on objects whose value is the maker’s live service itself — a subscription, a hosted platform, a curated feed — where owning the part is a contradiction, because the only part is the maker, and the honest verdict is to treat the whole purchase as a rental with an unknown end date. So use the spare-part audit where durability and a replaceable fatal part both matter, and set it down where the object is designed to be consumed, replaced, or enjoyed now. The audit is a tool for the categories where the component actually decides the lifespan, and this week that is most of the mechanical and analogue hardware on the shelf and nearly all of the spring-restock tools.

Pair one — the food processor with a parts catalogue versus the bandsaw with an overseas parts lead. Magimix lists its blades, bowls, seals, and couplings individually with part numbers, so the components that actually wear out are orderable from the maker and from third parties, and a ten-year-old unit is serviceable because the supply was never the maker’s to withdraw. Grizzly makes genuinely capable bandsaws at a value price, but the replacement parts for a given model sit on long overseas lead times, and when an SKU retires the specific components can go quiet with no domestic equivalent to fall back on. The generalizable test this pair teaches: open the parts page before you read the reviews. A long ownership story is only an asset if the object it reviewed can still be repaired, and a value import converts your best evidence into a wait the day the overseas supply lapses.

Pair two — the saw with a stocked brake versus the power station with a proprietary cell. The test: is the component that ends the object stocked and open, or is it a maker-only lock on a model clock? Where the answer is stocked and open, the object outlives the transaction. Where the answer is a proprietary pack on a model year, you are renting the life of the unit from the maker’s refresh cycle, and the rental ends whether you finished using it or not.

Pair three — the open drivetrain versus the young base-layer supply. A Shimano drivetrain is built from standard chains, cassettes, and cables carried by hundreds of sellers, so no single maker controls the parts that end its life, and the supply will outlast any one component’s warranty by decades. That is not a criticism of the maker; the layers may well outlast the warranty and then some. It simply means the parts story has not aged, and a young brand’s long-term read is always provisional until the first owners cross the wear interval. The test: does the object’s survival depend on a supply the industry shares, or on a maker too new to have built one? Buy where the supply is shared. Wait where the supply is still being built.

Pair four — the serviceable lens versus the young outerwear cohort. Where the answer is no, old evidence is current evidence. Where the answer depends on a young maker’s supply, wait for the seasons to do their work.

Pair five — the standard camera mount versus the young resole cohort. An OM System camera sits on the Micro Four Thirds mount, a shared standard with maker and third-party service, so the body is maintainable without the original maker and a discontinued reference is merely the cheapest entry into a parts universe that will outlive the model year. The test, and it generalises across every category here: does the object’s design let another person’s years apply to yours? When it does, borrow their age. When every unit is young or the maker’s supply is unproven, you are the cohort, and the wait for evidence is a wait you cannot outsource.

That is a legitimate growth strategy, not a flaw. The test: does the maker sell the part, or only the whole? A spare-part store is cohort evidence cheaper to verify than any review, and its absence on a young brand is a reason to wait, not to walk away.

Pair four — the open board versus the fast-turnover accessory. A Pine64 board ships with open documentation and standard components sourced from many sellers, so no single maker controls the parts that keep it running and the supply outlives any one board’s production. Twelve South makes attractive accessories that many people prefer to generic ones, but the catalogue turns over on the Apple release cycle, and a specific piece is often superseded before owners report on long-term hardware wear such as hinge slack or connector fatigue. The cohort is simply young, which is what happens to any product whose catalogue moves quicker than its failure window. The test: is the design stable enough that old owners’ experience applies to the current unit? Where the catalogue churns on someone else’s clock, you are always in the launch cohort, and a Wait is the honest verdict until the supply proves it can age.

Pair five — the established welt versus the young resole cohort. A Thorogood work boot is built on Goodyear-welted construction that any cobbler can rebuild, and the maker stocks heels and soles for older builds, so the fatal wear item is replaceable and the object outlives the warranty by design. Wildling Shoes makes thoughtful barefoot footwear and does something refreshing in a crowded category, but the US presence is young and the resole cohort that would prove the construction holds through its first rebuild has not yet accumulated. Again, not a criticism: the shoe may well outlast the warranty. The test, and it closes the audit: identify the one component whose replacement ends the object’s useful life, then ask whether that component is stocked and open or whether the maker’s supply is still being proven. If the answer is stocked and open, the reviews may be few while the future is already safe.

How to Read the Spare Part Before You Click

The audit only matters if it changes what you do on the page, so here is the practical method. Three moves, none requiring expertise, all requiring you to look past the hero shot and the star average. The interface is optimized to show you the newest arrival and the lowest price; you are looking for the parts status and the stock, which means every one of these moves is a deliberate act against the grain of the page.

Name the component most likely to end the object’s life

Change your question from “is this good” to “what breaks first, and can I buy it,” and name the single part before you read a single review. A blender’s coupling, a saw’s brake, a bike’s chain, a lens’s mount, a phone’s battery, a boot’s sole — each is the component that will eventually end the object, and each is the only component that decides the verdict. Naming it first converts the rest of the research from a mystery into a hunt, because you now know exactly what to look for on the parts page, and you can ignore the ninety percent of the review that describes the object on day one. This single move, done before you read a five-star review, turns the listing from a spec sheet into a question with an answer, because it tells you which document actually matters.

Open the parts catalogue and check stock and source

Before trusting any long-term claim, open the parts page and ask whether the fatal component is listed individually, in stock, and obtainable from a third party or only from the maker. A parts catalogue with named, stocked, cross-compatible components is an object designed to be kept alive; a maker that sells the object but not its internals is telling you the object dies when its first irreplaceable part fails. Five minutes on the parts page answers the question the entire review section cannot, because reviews describe how the object failed while the parts page describes whether you can do anything about it, and the source column tells you whether the supply will outlive the maker or vanish with it.

Decide whether the part outlives the maker

The whole audit reduces to one comparison: is the fatal component open and stocked, or is it a lock the maker holds. If the part is standard and carried by many sellers, buy with confidence — that is the food processor with orderable seals, the saw with a stocked brake, the drivetrain built from shared parts, the lens with no firmware. If the part is proprietary and tied to the current model, wait — that is the power station with a maker-only cell, the young brand whose supply has not formed, the cloud gadget whose module expires. The verdict is never in the object alone; it is in the relationship between the object and its parts, and naming both before you click is the entire discipline.

What the Platforms Are Quietly Changing

Underneath the interface the platforms are reorganising how parts information is presented, and most of the changes make this audit harder. The biggest is the sorting by newness and price, where a refreshed model and a stocked-part veteran sit in the same rail priced within a few dollars, with the only difference — that one can be kept alive and the other cannot — buried deep in a specification tab. A sort that privileges the lowest price is a sort that structurally cannot tell you which object still has a parts supply. A second change is the softening of parts language: makers say genuine components or authorised service rather than we list every part, which reads as assurance rather than admission and hides the lock behind a reassuring word. A third is more encouraging: some marketplaces now surface a used and refurbished market beside the new one, and a healthy supply of decade-old units in working condition is a durability signal no review can fake, because it proves the object outlived its first owner and someone still stocks what fixes it. For cross-border buyers the change that matters most is regional parts divergence, where a component stocked in the home market is back-ordered in yours, so the object arrives serviceable on paper and unserviceable in practice, and the parts page you trusted described a shelf you cannot reach.

Category Notes: Where the Spare-Part Audit Pays Off

The audit pays off very unevenly, so spend it where the fatal part actually runs. Mechanical and analogue durables are the highest-yield category by a wide margin: food processors with orderable seals, table saws with stocked brakes, drivetrains built from standard parts, manual lenses, welted boots, and forged blades all survive because their fatal parts are open and stocked, and buying the well-reviewed unit is the single best move on the page. Furniture and seating sit in the middle: a welted or all-metal piece outlives its maker, while upholstered and fast-turnover systems need a mature parts cohort you often cannot yet read, and a young direct sofa is a wait until the first owners cross the sag interval. Consumer electronics are the awkward case, because genuine year-over-year improvement means a new model may be genuinely better, so weight the part story against the silicon and treat the launch as a possible upgrade rather than a steal — but a modular phone with a parts store is the rare electronics buy that ages. Skincare and consumables sit at the bottom of this particular audit, not because reviews do not matter but because there is no fatal part worth stocking — the only component is the one you are meant to use up, and a discontinued formula is a reformulation risk rather than a parts risk. Power stations and battery platforms deserve a special warning this week, because the cell is the component that ends the product’s life and its decay is invisible for a year and a half, so a unit whose battery is proprietary and tied to the model year is a wait even when the object itself is superb — the parts clock for the part that matters has already been set by the maker. Appliances sit in the same zone: a discontinued blender with a stocked seal catalogue is a buy, while one whose coupling was proprietary and is now gone is a wait regardless of how complete the cohort looks. Match the effort to whether the object has a maker-dependent fatal part, and the audit stops feeling like work.

Cross-Border Logistics and the Parts Gap

The spare-part audit has a cross-border dimension the domestic buyer never encounters, and it is the reason a well-reviewed import can disappoint in ways nobody warned about. A product whose parts are stocked in its home market may list those parts on a page you cannot read and ship them on a lead time you cannot quote, so the component that saves the object domestically is the component that strands it internationally. The fix is to read the home-market parts page and the local one as separate documents: use the foreign catalogue for the mechanical question of what wears out and when, and the domestic status for the logistical question of what happens when it does. There is also a plain timing effect worth naming this week. An import restocked under a different freight and duty regime carries a landed cost the current reviewer never paid, so old praise of the value may describe a price that no longer exists, and the part that was cheap at home may be expensive or back-ordered here. And cross-border return windows close long before any failure window opens, which is the structural asymmetry at the centre of importing an object whose fatal part is maker-only: you must decide whether to keep the thing months before the parts story could tell you whether keeping it was wise. There is also a documentation gap specific to imported parts: the manual, the firmware, and the parts diagram for a model are often only in the home-market language, and the maker’s support portal may have already archived them, so the independent repair that would save the object domestically is harder to execute when the instructions vanished with the product. That asymmetry is the strongest possible argument for buying imports only where the fatal part is open and stocked in your market, and for treating a young or maker-only supply as a cross-border wait regardless of how complete the home-market cohort looks.

Bottom Line

The component catalogue you opened last week is the real object you are buying, and that is not a metaphor but the literal unit of analysis, because an object is only as good as the part you can still get when the maker has moved on. This week that matters more than usual, because makers are publishing parts lists as a trust signal, the pre-summer launch wave is burying the spare-part story under the new-model story, and right-to-repair pressure is quietly widening the open-part pool. The fix is one procedure run before you click: name the component most likely to end the object’s life, open the parts catalogue and check stock and source, and decide whether that part outlives the maker. Buy where the fatal part is open and stocked, as with the food processor whose seals are orderable, the saw whose brake is catalogued, the drivetrain built from shared parts, the lens with no firmware, the modular phone with a spare-part store, and the welted boot any cobbler can rebuild. Wait where the part is a lock the maker holds, as with the value import on an overseas parts lead, the power station with a proprietary cell, the young apparel and outerwear brands whose supply has not formed, and the firmware-dependent phone whose life is rented from the maker. The ten marketplace buys above are listings whose fatal parts are standard or stocked by the maker and third parties alike; the ten direct buys are makers whose designs stayed still long enough for a parts story to accumulate, with repair and rebuild programmes as corroboration. The ten waits on each side are not verdicts against the products, several of which are excellent — they are notes that the part clock has not run yet, or that the part that matters is already locked. Let it run, or buy knowing which lock you accepted. A stocked, open, obtainable fatal part is the cheapest insurance available, and the only cost of checking whether it exists is five minutes on the parts page.
