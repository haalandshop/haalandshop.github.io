Best Things to Buy Right Now — June 30, 2026

Quick take: Two things happen at midnight tonight. The financial first half of the year closes, which means every retailer with stock physically sitting in a domestic warehouse has a reason to discount it before the books shut. And a stack of new cross-border cost rules switch on in Europe, Russia, and Southeast Asia, which means anything still overseas gets a new price floor tomorrow. Put those together and you get the cleanest shopping rule of the year so far: buy the box that’s already in the country, and buy it today. Below, what changed, why it matters at the checkout, and twenty things genuinely worth your money this week.

What’s Hot This Week

Tonight is the last night of duty-free small parcels in Europe. From tomorrow, the long-standing relief that let imported parcels under €150 come in without customs duty is gone, replaced by an interim flat charge of €3 per item while the full customs overhaul is built out. Three euros sounds trivial until you do the division. On a €12 phone case it is a 25 percent price increase. On a €9 pack of hair clips it is a third. On a €140 pair of trainers it is noise. The charge is flat, so it lands hardest on exactly the category that made cheap cross-border shopping addictive — the small, light, single-item order. Forwarders have been running hard cut-offs to beat it, with the last clean window closing at 09:00 on Monday and emergency air-capacity surcharges of roughly 20 to 25 yuan per kilo charged to sellers who wanted their inventory across the line first. Those surcharges are already in the price of whatever landed last week. Nobody is absorbing them out of kindness.

The United States quietly turned a temporary suspension into permanent law. On June 24 the customs authority published two interim final rules — one covering commercial channels, one covering international post — that write the suspension of the $800 low-value duty exemption into regulation rather than leaving it resting on an executive order that could lapse. The public comment window runs to July 24, but the operative change took effect immediately. In practice: every inbound shipment, regardless of value, now needs a formal or informal entry, a ten-digit tariff classification, and a declared country of origin. For postal parcels there is now a tiered flat charge per item — roughly $80, $160, or $200 depending on which duty band the origin country falls into, with the highest band covering the origins most small parcels actually come from. That is a $200 charge on a $15 item, which is not a tax so much as a closed door. The practical consequence for you as a shopper is that the postal channel is finished for cheap direct imports, and everything reputable has moved to commercial courier with duty prepaid.

Which makes three letters the most important thing on any listing page: DDP. Delivered duty paid means the seller or carrier has already settled the import charges and the price you see is the price you pay. The alternative, delivered at place, means the parcel stops at customs, you get a notice, and you pay before it is released — and if you don’t, it gets returned or destroyed. Refusal rates on that second model have gone through the roof this year for the obvious reason that nobody enjoys being invoiced by a government for a t-shirt. Before you buy anything from an overseas seller this week, find the words “import charges included,” “duties prepaid,” or “DDP.” If you can’t find them, assume the worst and budget another 20 to 35 percent.

A single blank field becomes a kill switch at midnight on the largest European marketplace. From June 30, any listing being shipped cross-border into the EU or the UK without a valid country-of-origin value gets restricted — the listing stays visible, but the buy button stops working. This applies to both platform-fulfilled and seller-fulfilled inventory, across all the major European storefronts, and there is no grace period and no manual review queue. New listings can’t even be created without the field populated. What you will see over the next few days is a visible thinning of the long tail: the unbranded, multi-variant, drop-shipped listings whose sellers genuinely do not know where their product was made are the ones that go dark first. If there is something sitting in your basket from an overseas seller shipping into Europe, finish the order today. If it’s still there on Thursday, it may simply not be purchasable.

The Russian marketplace fee reset lands tomorrow, and it inverts the usual advice. The dominant platform there is collapsing six price tiers into three: everything at or below 100 roubles and everything from 101 to 300 roubles keeps its existing rate, and everything above 300 roubles moves to a flat category rate with all the old sliding-scale discounts removed. The 12 percent commission cap that protected items under 1,500 roubles disappears entirely. For cross-border sellers the change is even blunter — the price-tier logic is abolished outright, so a 1,000-rouble item and a 10,000-rouble item in the same category now pay exactly the same percentage. Modelled across the main categories, a 10,000-rouble item goes from roughly 9 to 15 percent in fashion, roughly 11 to 22 percent in beauty, 8.5 to 12 percent in home goods, and 7 to 10 percent in consumer electronics. But here is the twist worth knowing: bulky categories — furniture, large appliances, bicycles, big outdoor items — actually get a small commission cut. Everywhere else in the world, big things are getting more expensive to ship and small things stay cheap. In this one market, for the next quarter, the opposite is true. Also changing: the outbound inventory request window shrinks from 65 to 60 days, and a cross-region surcharge of up to 8 percent appears for the two largest cities.

Freight went the wrong way through June, and that bill arrives in September. Transpacific container rates climbed through the peak-season push, with forty-foot high-cube boxes running somewhere in the $6,000 to $9,800 range depending on carrier, origin, and destination coast — and East Coast routings sitting above West Coast on the back of longer transits and canal-linked charges. Containers loaded this month are the goods on shelves in late August and September. Which means the imported, bulky, low-margin things you can buy today out of existing domestic stock are, right now, cheaper than their own replacements will be. Televisions, furniture, mattresses, grills, large kitchen appliances, anything sold by volume rather than by weight — the current shelf price reflects a cheaper container than the next one.

And that’s where quarter-end does the rest of the work. Tonight closes the first half of the financial year. Every unit of inventory physically sitting in a domestic warehouse is a number on a balance sheet at midnight, and the fastest way to improve that number is to convert it into cash. This is why the last three days of June are consistently one of the two or three best non-event discount windows of the year, and almost nobody shops them, because there is no branded sale name attached. What gets cleared: spring apparel that didn’t move, patio and grill inventory bought in February, fans and cooling gear ordered before anyone knew what the summer would look like, last-generation televisions, and every seasonal SKU with an autumn replacement already on a ship. None of it is advertised. All of it is discounted.

Southeast Asia adds a tax and a stopwatch. From July 1, marketplace sellers operating in Vietnam face a 1 percent corporate income tax withheld at source by the platform, which is small enough to be absorbed by anyone with real margin and large enough to be passed straight through by anyone without. Separately, from July 6, the biggest video-commerce platform starts scoring sellers in Thailand on their response rate to buyer messages within 12 hours, with an 85 percent floor and tiered penalty points below it. The consumer effect of the second one is genuinely positive and shows up fast: when a platform makes message response a scored metric with teeth, the median reply time collapses within a month. If you have ever sent a pre-purchase question into the void, the void is about to start answering.

One fast-fashion giant closes its US self-ship channel today. Third-party sellers on that platform can no longer fulfil American orders from their own facilities and must route through the platform’s managed warehouses. Sellers are unhappy; shoppers should not be. Self-ship was the source of nearly every horror story in that ecosystem — the mystery tracking number, the fourteen-day estimate that became forty, the return address that turned out to be a different continent. Centralised fulfilment means slower onboarding of new products and a narrower catalogue, but a delivery date you can actually plan around.

One thing not to do: panic-buy small imported goods tonight. The instinct is understandable and mostly wrong. A €3 charge or a prepaid courier duty line is an annoyance, not a catastrophe, and the classic mistake in a week like this is paying an express-shipping premium larger than the duty you were trying to dodge. The genuine urgency is narrow and specific: European baskets from overseas sellers that will be blocked by the country-of-origin cutoff, anything you were going to buy from a postal-channel seller into the US, and heavy imported goods where the domestic-stock price advantage is real and measurable. Everything else can wait a week, and some of it will be cheaper in a week.

Buying Tips

Run the “where is the box” test before every purchase this week. It takes four seconds. Look at the estimated delivery date. Two to five days means the item is sitting in a domestic warehouse, its landed cost is already paid, and quarter-end pressure is working in your favour. Ten to twenty-five days means it hasn’t crossed a border yet and every rule that starts tomorrow applies to it. Those two listings can be the same product at the same headline price and represent completely different deals.

Read the shipping terms, not the shipping price. A listing quoting $4 shipping with duties unpaid is more expensive than one quoting $12 with duties included. The second one is a price. The first one is an estimate with a government-shaped hole in it. On any cross-border order above about $40, “import charges included” is worth more than any discount code you’ll find.

Split your basket by origin. If half your cart ships domestically and half comes from overseas, checking out as one order often routes everything through the slower, riskier channel. Two separate orders means the domestic half arrives this week and the imported half can’t hold it hostage.

Prefer the local-stock variant even at a visible premium. Most large marketplaces now list the same product twice — once from an overseas seller at a lower headline price, once from in-country stock at a higher one. Once you add import charges, the wait, and the return difficulty, the local listing wins more often than not. It also wins the argument if something arrives broken, because a domestic return label costs a few dollars and an international one costs more than the product.

On anything heavy and imported, buy the current generation rather than waiting for the next. This is the opposite of the usual advice and it is specific to this quarter. The freight cost of the next inbound wave is already locked in and it is higher than the last one. A television, a mattress, a piece of flat-pack furniture, a large appliance — the model on the shelf today reflects a cheaper container than its successor will.

Don’t confuse a quarter-end markdown with a sale. Quarter-end discounts are quiet, unadvertised, and inconsistent — they appear on individual SKUs where a specific seller has a specific problem, not across a category. Which means the only way to find them is to have a list and check it, rather than to browse a landing page. If you’ve been circling something for a month, check it tonight.

Verify the seller can tell you where a thing was made. This has stopped being a philosophical question and become a functional one. Sellers who can answer it stay purchasable in Europe from Tuesday; sellers who can’t, don’t. It is also a decent proxy for everything else — a seller who knows their supply chain generally also knows their defect rate.

What’s Coming Next

Tomorrow through the end of next week is the noisy part. The European charge, the Russian commission reset, and the Vietnamese withholding all switch on July 1. Expect a chaotic fortnight of sellers re-pricing, some of it in both directions as they discover their old margin models don’t hold. The reliable pattern after a fee change is that prices overshoot upward for two to three weeks and then settle slightly below the panic level. If you can wait until mid-July on non-urgent cross-border purchases, you’ll usually do better than buying into the reset week.

The big summer event is close enough to affect your planning. The flagship mid-year sale is days away, which is why some sellers are clearing quietly right now — they’d rather move stock at a decent margin today than fight a discount war next week. Two implications. First, anything genuinely deep tonight is probably deeper than what you’ll see in the event, because event pricing is engineered rather than desperate. Second, if you want the headline electronics deals, those do come in the event, so hold those specific items.

Freight-driven price increases land in late August and September. The containers moving at this month’s elevated rates put goods on shelves in eight to ten weeks. That’s when you’ll see quiet price rises on bulky imported categories — not dramatic, but real, in the five to ten percent range. Anything large you know you’ll want before winter is better bought in July than in September.

The compliance thinning continues all summer. Between the country-of-origin enforcement starting tomorrow, the certification filing requirements landing in the following week, and the general tightening of who can ship what into which market, the cheapest unbranded end of several categories will keep contracting. This is mildly bad for prices and clearly good for whatever ends up in your house. Plan on paying slightly more for slightly better for the rest of the year.

And one to watch rather than act on: the second-half discount calendar is starting to firm up, with the major autumn event now expected in early October. That’s fourteen weeks out — the right horizon for a durable, deferrable purchase, and completely irrelevant to anything you’d use this summer.

Bottom Line

June 30 is the least glamorous good shopping day of the year. There’s no branded event, no countdown clock, and no landing page, which is exactly why the discounts that exist tonight are real ones — a seller trying to close a quarter is more motivated than a seller running a campaign.

The framework for this week is a single question: where is the box right now? If it’s in a warehouse near you, its price reflects an old, cheaper cost base and a balance sheet that closes at midnight. Buy it. If it’s still overseas, tomorrow it inherits a new charge, a new commission, or a new compliance requirement — so either buy it today with duties prepaid, or wait three weeks for the re-pricing dust to settle.

Concretely: televisions, large kitchen appliances, and anything heavy and imported are at a local minimum tonight. Peak-summer gear from domestic-stock brands is fully sized and starting to discount. Small imported goods are worth grabbing today only if you were already going to buy them. And everything else — the deferrable, durable, big-ticket stuff — goes on the October list.

Buy what’s near you. Wait on what isn’t.
