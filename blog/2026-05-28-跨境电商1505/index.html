Best Things to Buy Right Now — May 28, 2026

Quick take: Fourteen days to the tournament, twenty-six to the June sale, and the shelves have gone still. Nobody is launching, nobody is discounting meaningfully, and the standard advice is to sit on your hands until something happens. That advice treats the pre-event fortnight as dead time, which is a category error. It is not dead, it is structured, and the structure is unusually legible precisely because so little attention is pointed at it. Three things are true in this window and in almost no other. First, today’s price is the reference price — the number that next month’s percentage will be calculated against — which makes right now a measurement opportunity even when it is not a buying one. Second, the merchandising decisions for the event are already locked, which means every product on every shelf has already been sorted into a bucket you can infer: in the event, dumped before the event, or never in the event at all. Third, the only people transacting while nobody is watching are the ones who have a reason to, and their reasons are readable. The practical payoff is a rule that sounds backwards and is not: the dead fortnight is the correct time to buy the things that no sale is coming for, and the correct time to do nothing about everything else. What follows is why the pre-event calendar has a shape, how reference prices get set and hardened, the three populations of stock currently sitting on shelves, who is motivated to sell into silence, where the whole read collapses, and twenty picks sorted by whether an event is coming for them at all.

What’s Hot This Week

Late May in a tournament year is about as quiet as retail gets, and the quiet is the story. Nothing is hot in the conventional sense — no category is spiking, no price is collapsing, no product is selling out. What is moving is entirely structural, and three of those movements are visible from the outside if you know where to point.

Prices are flat, and flat is the data

Across every category worth tracking, price dispersion has compressed to near nothing over the past ten days. The same air fryer costs the same at four retailers. This is not coincidence and it is not competition working well. It is what happens when every merchandising team has finished setting their event grid and frozen the pre-event baseline so the discount maths comes out clean. A flat price two weeks before an event is a declared price, not a negotiated one, and it is the most reliable single number you will see all quarter. Write it down. In twenty-six days it becomes the denominator of every percentage you are shown.

Launch calendars have gone quiet on purpose

Product announcements have essentially stopped, and the stoppage is deliberate rather than seasonal. Launching into the fortnight before a tentpole event wastes the launch — attention is being hoarded for the event, review coverage is being scheduled around it, and any early momentum gets buried the moment the sale opens. The consequence is that anything launching now is doing so for a reason, and there are only two reasons: the product does not need event traffic, or it could not get an event slot. Those two possibilities point in opposite directions and are usually distinguishable within a minute of looking.

Freight and last-mile capacity has slack for the first time since March

The quiet extends backwards through the supply chain. Ocean and air capacity out of the major origin lanes is looser than it has been since the spring restock, inbound appointment slots at the big fulfilment networks are available at short notice, and last-mile networks are running below peak. Practically, this means a cross-border order placed this week arrives faster and with fewer exceptions than the same order placed in three weeks, when everybody’s event inventory is trying to move through the same doors at once. Delivery speed is a form of discount, and right now it is the only one on offer.

The Dead Fortnight Is Structured, Not Empty

The instinct to skip the pre-event window comes from a reasonable place: if a sale is coming, buying before it is a mistake. That logic is sound and it is also incomplete, because it silently assumes that the sale is coming for the thing you want. Tentpole events do not discount catalogues, they discount selections — a curated subset assembled months in advance by people optimising for margin mix, inventory position and vendor funding. Most of what a retailer sells is not in that subset and never was. For those products, waiting buys you nothing except twenty-six days of not owning the thing, and quite often a worse price, because event periods pull inventory and attention away from the long tail and stockouts on non-featured items are common.

So the first move in the dead fortnight is not to decide whether to buy. It is to decide whether an event is coming for this specific product at all, which is a much more answerable question than it sounds.

The window has an internal shape worth understanding. Roughly three weeks out, merchandising grids lock and pricing teams set the pre-event baseline. Roughly two weeks out — where we are now — that baseline goes live and stops moving, which is why dispersion has compressed. Somewhere in the final ten days, a second wave begins: the quiet clearance of inventory that did not make the grid and needs to be gone before event stock lands. That wave is the single best buying opportunity in the entire cycle and almost nobody is looking at it, because everyone who is paying attention is waiting for the headline event that starts two weeks later.

This is also the period when the calendar exerts maximum pressure on sellers without exerting any on buyers. A merchant with capital tied up in stock that has no event slot is watching working capital sit still while the warehouse fills with product that does. A seller who did not get accepted into the event grid is looking at four weeks of suppressed organic traffic, because platforms reallocate impressions toward promoted inventory during event periods. Every one of those parties has a reason to transact now and no reason to advertise that they do. That asymmetry — motivated sellers, unmotivated buyers, frozen prices — is the entire opportunity, and it exists for about ten more days.

The last structural point is what makes the window trustworthy. Prices set during the dead fortnight are set to be defensible. A retailer knows whatever number sits on the page now will be screenshotted, indexed by price trackers, and used as the comparison basis when the discount is announced. That creates a real incentive toward an honest price rather than a theatrical one, at least among sellers large enough to care about scrutiny. The number you see this week is, for once, roughly what the seller thinks the thing is worth.

Today’s Price Is the Reference Price

Every discount is a fraction, and fractions have denominators. The percentage is the part that gets printed in large type; the denominator is the part that determines whether the percentage means anything. During the dead fortnight, you get to observe the denominator directly, in the open, before anyone has a reason to dress it up. This is the single most useful thing about the window and it costs nothing but the discipline to write numbers down.

The practical version is unglamorous. Pick the ten to fifteen things you might plausibly buy in the next two months. Record today’s price for each, the exact configuration or variant, the seller, and the date. That is the whole exercise, and it takes about twenty minutes. In twenty-six days, when the event copy announces a percentage off “list”, you will be one of a very small number of people who can evaluate the claim rather than react to it. Most of the time the discount will be real and your list will simply confirm it, which is fine — the list is cheap insurance, not a conspiracy detector.

The value shows up in the minority of cases where the reference price has been engineered. There are three common shapes. The first is the restored list price, where a product that has been selling at a persistent street discount for months gets quietly returned to its official list a week or two before the event, so that the event price — which is roughly the street price you could have had all along — reads as a substantial cut. Nothing here is false. The list price is the list price. It is simply that the comparison has been made against a number nobody was actually paying. The second is the variant swap, where the discounted configuration at event time is not the one that was available at the reference price, usually differing in capacity, colour, bundled accessory or model year. The third is the bundle wrap, where the item is discounted only as part of a package whose components have never been individually priced, which makes the percentage formally unverifiable.

All three are detectable with a price history, and the dead fortnight is when you should be building one. Third-party price trackers do most of this automatically for large marketplaces and are worth using, but they have a blind spot that matters: they track the listing, not the product. When a listing’s underlying variant changes, the tracker’s history follows the listing and quietly becomes a history of two different objects. Your own note, which records the configuration alongside the number, does not have that failure mode.

There is a second-order use for the list that is arguably worth more than the first. Prices recorded now, compared against prices during the event, tell you something durable about each seller that generalises well beyond this cycle. A retailer whose discounts consistently measure against a stable pre-event baseline is one whose future claims you can take at face value with far less checking. A retailer whose baseline moves conveniently upward every time an event approaches has told you something permanent. You are not just measuring a price. You are calibrating a source.

Three Kinds of Stock Are Sitting on the Shelf

Right now, every physical item in every warehouse serving the June event has already been sorted, and the sorting is finished. Merchandising grids locked weeks ago. Vendor funding is committed. Promotional allocations are assigned. The consequence is that the shelf you are looking at is not one population but three, blended together and visually indistinguishable, each with a completely different correct action. Separating them is the core skill of the dead fortnight, and it is mostly inference rather than information — you will not be told which bucket a product is in, but the buckets leave traces.

Event stock, already spoken for

This is inventory with a confirmed promotional slot. It is typically high-volume, high-recognition product in categories the platform uses to drive event traffic, and it is characterised by a suspiciously well-behaved price: stable, round, and frequently a touch higher than it was in April. Availability is excellent because stock has been built for the event. Coupon stacking, which was probably available a month ago, has quietly disappeared. The correct action is simple and boring: wait. The discount is coming, it will be real, and there is no meaningful risk of the product disappearing.

Pre-event clearance, moving now on purpose

This is inventory that did not make the grid and needs to be gone before event stock arrives — usually superseded model years, odd configurations, overweight colourways, or product from vendors who declined to fund a promotion. It shows up as an oddly specific discount on an oddly specific variant, applied quietly, with no campaign around it. Availability is patchy and declining. This is the highest-value bucket in the entire cycle, because the discount is driven by warehouse physics rather than by marketing, and warehouse physics does not care whether you noticed. The correct action is buy, and buy now, because this stock is defined by the fact that it will not exist in three weeks.

Orphan stock, which no event will ever touch

This is the long tail: specialist categories, small vendors, product that is simply not the kind of thing tentpole events feature. Its price has been flat for months and will be flat for months more. There is no discount coming, not in June, not in July, and waiting costs you the use of the thing plus a real risk of thinning availability as event logistics crowd out the tail. The correct action is buy when you need it, and the dead fortnight is as good a moment as any — arguably better than most, because delivery networks currently have slack.

Anchor Hardening and How to Catch It

Anchor hardening is the quiet upward drift of a reference price in the weeks before an event, so that the event discount measures against a larger number. It is the most common form of pre-event price manipulation and also the mildest, because in most cases it involves nothing more aggressive than allowing a product to return to its official list price. That is worth stating plainly, because the point of catching it is not moral outrage — it is that a hardened anchor turns a genuine-looking percentage into a much smaller real saving, and you would like to know which one you are being offered.

The mechanism is simple. Most products in competitive categories sell persistently below list, through some combination of clipped coupons, promotional codes, seller-funded reductions and platform subsidies. That street price is the real price in any meaningful sense — it is what people pay. In the weeks before an event, those mechanisms get withdrawn one at a time, and the listing drifts up toward list. When the event opens, the headline discount is calculated against list, and the resulting price is frequently within a few percent of the street price that was available all through April.

There are three signatures, in increasing order of reliability. The weakest is the price itself moving up, which is suggestive but has innocent explanations — input costs, currency, a genuine repricing. The middle signature is promotional tooling disappearing: the clippable coupon vanishes, the multi-buy discount lapses, the code that worked last month stops working. This is much harder to explain innocently, because those tools are cheap to run and there is no reason to withdraw them all simultaneously two weeks before an event except to clear the runway for the headline number. The strongest signature is the same product holding its street price at a different retailer, which eliminates cost, currency and repricing as explanations in one move, because those would affect everyone. When one seller’s price rises while three others hold, you are watching event preparation.

Catching it requires almost nothing. Note the price. Note whether a coupon or code is currently live. Check one competing retailer. Three data points, ninety seconds, and you have a reference that no amount of event-week urgency can talk you out of. The point is not to catch anyone out. It is that on event day you will be deciding under time pressure with a countdown timer on the page, and the only defence against that environment is a number you wrote down while calm.

One qualification. Anchor hardening is much less common at the high end than most people assume, and reading it into every price rise will make you miss real opportunities. Premium and specialist categories tend to have stable pricing because their buyers research over long horizons and would notice, and because those categories rarely join tentpole events anyway. The technique concentrates almost entirely in high-volume, low-consideration goods sold on discount theatre. If you find yourself suspecting it on a hand-built object from a small workshop, you have almost certainly misread a genuine price change.

Who Transacts While Nobody Is Watching

The dead fortnight has thin demand, which means the sellers who are actively trying to move product right now are a self-selected group. Understanding who they are and what pressure they are under is most of the work, because their motivations determine whether the thing being offered is an opportunity or a problem.

The first group is capital-constrained. Working capital in physical goods is mostly inventory, and inventory that is not selling is capital that is not working. A merchant committed to a large event order has to pay for it before the event generates revenue, and the gap gets bridged by clearing whatever is on hand. This is the healthiest form of dead-fortnight selling, because the product is fine and the discount is purely financial. It shows up as broad, unadvertised reductions across a catalogue rather than targeted markdowns.

The second group is space-constrained, overlapping heavily with the pre-event dump described earlier. Fulfilment capacity is finite, storage fees escalate for slow-moving stock, and event inventory has to physically go somewhere. Anything occupying a shelf that event stock needs has to move, and the discount is whatever it takes. Also a healthy signal, with the caveat that this clearance concentrates on the variants that were hardest to sell.

The third group is excluded, and this one requires care. These are sellers who did not get a slot, because they were not accepted, could not fund the discount, or failed a performance threshold. They face four to six weeks of suppressed organic visibility and are trying to bank revenue before the lights go out. Exclusion is not itself a red flag — plenty of good sellers skip events on margin grounds — but it correlates with things that are. A seller excluded for performance reasons has fulfilment or defect metrics you would want to know about. The distinguishing question is whether they chose not to participate or failed to, and the account history usually answers it.

The fourth group is seasonal and the most benign. Some categories simply have their moment now regardless of the retail calendar: end-of-cold-season technical clothing, spring garden equipment, anything tied to a use window opening or closing. These sellers are responding to weather, not to the event, and their discounts have no relationship to what happens in June.

The practical filter is to ask which of the four you are looking at before you evaluate the price. Capital and space constraints produce real bargains on ordinary product. Seasonal timing produces real bargains with a use-window caveat. Exclusion produces a discount that may be compensating you for something. The discount looks identical in all four cases, and the first three questions cost about a minute of looking at the seller rather than the listing.

Orphan Stock Is the Actual Opportunity

If the dead fortnight has one actionable conclusion, it is this: the correct time to buy the things no event will ever discount is right now, and the reason is not price but everything else.

Orphan stock — the long tail of specialist categories, small vendors and products that tentpole events structurally ignore — is defined by price stability. It does not go on sale in June, it does not go on sale in November, and the number on the page today is very close to the number on the page in six months. For these products, the entire waiting calculus collapses. There is no discount to be patient for, which means patience has no payoff and several costs.

The costs are worth enumerating because they are usually invisible. The first is simply not having the thing, a real cost that people systematically undervalue when a hypothetical future discount is on the table. Twenty-six days of not owning a tool you need is twenty-six days of working around its absence. The second is availability erosion, specific to this window and genuinely underappreciated. Event periods consume fulfilment capacity, warehouse space and vendor attention, and the long tail is what gets squeezed. Specialist inventory comfortably in stock today is meaningfully more likely to be backordered in three weeks, not because demand rose but because the supply chain reprioritised.

The third cost argues most strongly for acting now: logistics quality is currently at its annual peak and about to deteriorate. Shipping lanes have slack, customs is running at normal speed, last-mile networks are below capacity, and exception rates are low. In three weeks all four reverse simultaneously as event volume lands. An order placed today arrives faster, with fewer handling incidents and less tail risk of the multi-week delivery failure that event periods reliably produce.

Identifying orphan stock is mostly a matter of asking whether the product is the kind of thing a mass-market event would feature. Categories with small addressable audiences, products requiring explanation to buy, brands without the volume to fund a discount, and anything sold primarily through specialist retail are all orphans. The fastest test is whether the product appeared in last year’s June event, which is usually a search away and almost perfectly predictive, because event grids are built substantially from previous grids.

The one caution is not to mistake a temporary orphan for a permanent one. A product can be excluded from a specific event and still be heading for a markdown for other reasons — end of model year, distribution change, brand exit. Those markdowns are real and can be large, but they are not calendar-driven and therefore cannot be waited for reliably. If a discount is not on a schedule, waiting for it is not a strategy, it is a hope.

Where This Read Breaks Down

Every framework has boundaries and this one has four worth naming, because applying it outside them produces confident mistakes rather than useful caution.

It assumes the event is real and its shape is known. The approach rests on knowing a specific promotional period is coming, roughly when, and roughly what it will cover. That is safe for major recurring events on established calendars. It is not safe for one-off promotions, regionally specific sales, or platform events with shifting formats. If you cannot say with reasonable confidence what a product’s discount will look like in four weeks, you are guessing, and the framework will simply lend structure to the guess. That failure mode is nasty because it feels rigorous.

It does not extend to categories with genuine supply constraints. The waiting logic assumes stock will be there when the discount arrives. That fails wherever supply is limited by manufacturing capacity, materials, or a small number of production runs per year. There, waiting for a discount that may be real can cost you the ability to buy at all, and the correct behaviour inverts: buy when available, ignore price. Anything hand-made, anything with a queue, anything where you have seen the phrase “next batch” is outside this framework.

Second-hand and collectible markets have no pre-event calendar and never will. Used prices are set by what individual sellers happen to want on a given week. There is no merchandising grid, no vendor funding, no promotional slot, and consequently no reference price to harden and no dump to catch. These markets are quiet in the dead fortnight, but that reflects attention being elsewhere, which produces thin liquidity in both directions. Sometimes that is an opportunity and sometimes nothing good is listed.

The window closes, and it closes earlier than the event starts. Everything here has a shelf life of roughly ten days. Pre-event clearance concentrates in the final stretch before event stock lands and then stops abruptly. Logistics slack disappears as inbound volume ramps. Anchor hardening completes and becomes unobservable, because a higher price that has sat there for two weeks just looks like the normal price.

The honest summary is that this is a framework for a narrow window in a specific market structure. Used correctly it turns two dead weeks into a genuine advantage. Used indiscriminately it is an elaborate way to talk yourself into buying things.

Working the Dead Fortnight in One Evening

The entire method fits into a single sitting, and doing it once produces something you will use twice — now, and again during the event. Here is the sequence in the order that wastes the least effort.

Start with the list, not the research. Write down everything you might plausibly buy in the next two months, without filtering for whether it is a good idea. Ten to twenty items is typical. Filtering works much better once you can see the whole set, because the decision is usually comparative — most people discover that three of the fifteen actually matter and the rest were browsing dressed up as intent.

Bucket each item before you look at any prices. Answer one question per item: will a major event discount this specific product in the next month? Not the category, the product. The fastest reliable check is whether it or a close sibling appeared in last year’s equivalent event — thirty seconds each, close to definitive. Sort into event stock, possible pre-event clearance, and orphan. Do not do this after looking at prices, because a price you like will reliably corrupt the classification.

Now record numbers, but only for the event-stock bucket. Price, exact configuration, seller, whether a coupon or code is currently live, and one competing retailer’s price for the same thing. This is the reference data that makes event week evaluable rather than reactive. It is genuinely the only part of the evening that has to be done with any precision, and it applies to maybe a third of the list.

Then act on the two buckets that are actionable today. Orphan stock: buy the things you actually need, because no discount is coming and the logistics window is currently as good as it gets. Pre-event clearance: check availability carefully, confirm you are getting the variant you want rather than the one that would not sell, and buy if it holds up. These two buckets are where the entire value of the exercise lands.

Finally, set a single reminder for two days after the event opens, not for the opening itself. Opening-day pricing is the noisiest and most theatrical part of any event, the countdown timers are doing psychological work, and the genuinely good offers are frequently not the headline ones. Forty-eight hours in, the theatre has subsided, price trackers have caught up, and your reference list does its job in about ten minutes of comparison. The two-day delay costs almost nothing in availability and removes almost all of the pressure that makes event shopping expensive.

Category Notes

Consumer electronics accessories are where anchor hardening concentrates most heavily and where the dead fortnight read pays for itself. Charging hardware, cables, storage, peripherals and small audio all sell on near-permanent discount, which means list price is nominal and the pre-event drift toward it is both easy to execute and easy to catch. Note current prices and available coupons in this category above all others.

Small kitchen appliances split cleanly into the two marketplace buckets and the split is unusually visible. Current-model, high-recognition units are event stock and should be waited for. Superseded model years and unpopular colourways are pre-event clearance and should be bought now, with a careful look at exactly which configuration is discounted. The tell is discount breadth: a whole line reduced is promotion, a single variant reduced is clearance.

Outdoor and seasonal goods are complicated by the fact that the use window and the event window are both open. Anything you need for the next four weeks should be bought now regardless of what June holds, because a discount you cannot use in time is not a discount. Anything for later in the summer can wait comfortably, since this category is heavily featured in early-summer events.

Enthusiast and specialist gear — audio, photography, cycling, tools, cooking equipment above the mass market — is almost entirely orphan stock. These categories do not appear in tentpole events in any meaningful volume, prices are stable year-round, and the availability erosion risk during event periods is real. This is the clearest buy-now category on the list and the one where waiting has the worst expected value.

Apparel and footwear run on their own seasonal calendar that only loosely intersects with retail events. End-of-season markdowns on cold-weather goods are at their annual deepest and will not improve. New-season product is at full price and will stay there. This is the category where the dead fortnight framework matters least — season position dominates event position completely.

Furniture and large home goods deserve a specific warning. These are heavily featured in summer events and the discounts are genuine, but delivery lead times extend dramatically during event periods and installation scheduling becomes difficult. Wait for the discount, but measure, decide and confirm the delivery constraints now, so that event week is a single click rather than a research project under time pressure.

Policy, Logistics and Cross-Border Notes

Shipping lanes are as good as they get right now and this is the least-discussed argument for buying during the dead fortnight. Ocean and air capacity out of the major manufacturing origins has slack, inbound appointment availability at the large fulfilment networks is genuinely open, and last-mile networks are running below peak. Exception rates — lost parcels, multi-week customs holds, failed delivery attempts — are at their seasonal low. All four of those reverse within about three weeks as event inventory floods the same infrastructure. If you are ordering anything that crosses a border, the difference between ordering this week and ordering in mid-June is measured in days of transit and a materially different tail risk.

Duty and tax thresholds deserve a check before any cross-border order. Low-value import allowances vary considerably by destination and have trended downward across several major markets over the past two years. An order sized to sit just under a threshold can become one that triggers formal clearance, brokerage fees and a delay if the threshold moved and you did not notice. This is a two-minute check that fails expensively when skipped, and it fails most often on repeat orders where the buyer works from a remembered number.

Marketplace event rules have a compliance dimension that affects buyers indirectly. Platforms running promotional events generally impose reference-price rules on participating sellers — a discount must typically measure against a price genuinely charged for some minimum period beforehand. This is precisely why anchor hardening takes the form it does: restoring a list price weeks in advance is compliant, inventing one during the event is not. That makes the pattern legible rather than sinister, and it means the drift you observe now is largely finished by the time the event opens.

Returns processing slows sharply during event periods. A return initiated in the dead fortnight moves at normal speed. The same return during or shortly after a major event can take substantially longer to be received, inspected and refunded, because returns are the lowest-priority operation in a warehouse running at capacity. For any purchase where return is a realistic possibility, buying now has an operational advantage that no discount reflects.

Warranty and service routing is worth confirming before the volume arrives, particularly on cross-border purchases where the servicing entity may not be in your country. Support queues lengthen during and after event periods for the obvious reason. Establishing now that a product has a serviceable path in your market costs one search and occasionally saves the entire value of the purchase.

What’s Coming Next

The dead fortnight has now been taken seriously as a window in its own right, and the next question follows directly from the last note above. When the event does open, the number that decides whether an offer is good is not the discount. It is what happens if the thing is wrong. Return windows, restocking fees, who pays return freight, how a cross-border return is routed, and how long a refund actually takes are all part of the price, and none of them appear in the price. Two products at identical discounts can differ enormously in what it costs to be wrong about them, and that difference is knowable in advance and almost never checked. The next guide prices the return policy explicitly — how to read a returns page as a financial instrument, why the option to be wrong is worth real money on expensive and uncertain purchases, and how to compare two offers when one of them quietly makes reversal impossible.

Bottom line

It is the last Thursday in May, fourteen days before a tournament and twenty-six before a sale, and the shelves have gone still. The still period is not a gap in the calendar. It is the most legible part of it.

Three things follow. First, sort before you shop. Every product in front of you has already been assigned to a bucket by someone else — in the event, dumped before the event, or never in the event at all — and the correct action differs completely by bucket while the shelf looks identical across all three. Checking whether a product appeared in last year’s equivalent event takes thirty seconds and answers the question better than any amount of price staring.

Second, buy the orphans now. Specialist gear, small-vendor product, anything a mass-market event structurally ignores — no discount is coming, waiting has no payoff, and the two costs of waiting are both real and currently rising. Availability in the long tail thins as event logistics crowd it out, and shipping conditions are at their annual best this week and will not be in three.

Third, write the numbers down. Twenty minutes today produces the only defence that works against event-week pricing, which is a reference price recorded when you were calm and nothing was counting down. Record the price, the exact configuration, and whether a coupon is currently live. That last detail is the one that catches anchor hardening, because promotional tooling disappearing simultaneously across a category two weeks before an event has no innocent explanation, while a price rise on its own has several.

And keep the asymmetry that makes the whole window worth the effort: during the event you will be one buyer among millions, competing for attention with a timer running. Right now you are the only person in the room. That is worth more than most of the discounts you are waiting for.
