Best Things to Buy Right Now — July 11, 2026

Quick take: Today is a double quarter-final Saturday. Norway play England in Miami at 5pm Eastern, Argentina play Switzerland in Kansas City at 9pm. By tomorrow morning, forty-four of the forty-eight countries that started this tournament on 11 June will be finished, and all three host nations — the United States, Mexico and Canada — have already been out for the best part of a week. That is not a sports observation. It is the largest and fastest-moving inventory event in consumer goods this year, and it is happening on a compressed eight-day timetable that ends on 19 July. Separately, and much more quietly, the US postal service changes how it handles perfume, nail polish, hairspray, hand sanitiser and anything containing a lithium cell tomorrow, and it changes how it prices light bulky parcels at the same time. Underneath both sits a storage-fee clock that turns over this month and will force a very specific set of products onto clearance in August. Three different timers, all readable, all actionable. Here is what to do with them, plus twenty things worth buying.

What actually changed this week

1. Twenty-eight countries’ worth of merchandise died in eleven days

The knockout rounds of a forty-eight-team tournament are an unusually violent thing to run through a retail supply chain. On 1 July, thirty-two teams were still alive. Tonight six are, and two of those will be gone by midnight. Every exit converts a pile of licensed apparel from full-price seasonal goods into dead stock, and it does it inside about seventy-two hours.

The mechanics are consistent and worth knowing, because they repeat with each round. When Germany went out on penalties in the round of thirty-two, the player-issue shirt went from roughly 1,099 to 549 in local currency within days, and the fan version from 699 to 349 — a straight fifty percent, applied almost immediately. When Brazil lost to Norway on 6 July, the Seleção kit moved to forty percent off across major channels. The pattern held in 2018 as well, when Germany, Argentina and Spain were all eliminated by the quarter-finals and their shirts settled at an average of more than forty percent off on the big marketplaces, with some listings quietly running buy-one-get-one to clear the last of it.

What makes this year different is scale and location. This is the first forty-eight-team edition, it is being played across three countries that between them have the highest per-capita sportswear spend on earth, and the sponsor investment is enormous — total commercial activity around the tournament runs to roughly eleven billion dollars, with two and a half to three billion of that in sponsorship and marketing alone. One brand alone reports national-team shirt sales running at two and a half times the pace of the last tournament, across twelve sponsored teams. Its main competitor has fourteen. Both over-ordered, because you have to over-order: you cannot manufacture a champion’s shirt after the whistle.

And then all three hosts went out inside seventy-two hours. Canada lost to Morocco on 5 July. Mexico lost to England on 6 July. The United States lost to Belgium on 7 July. That is the largest single demand pocket in the tournament — the domestic one, the one everyone stocked most heavily for — evaporating in the middle of the event with twelve days still to run. The tell was in the restocking: US shirts were sold out at the peak of demand and only came back into stock after the team had been eliminated, at which point nobody wanted them. That inventory is now sitting in warehouses in the country it was made for.

2. Tomorrow, posting a bottle of perfume in America changes

From 12 July, the US postal service formally reclassifies a long list of ordinary consumer goods as restricted hazardous materials. The list is broader than most people expect:

  • Lithium cells: power banks, wireless earbuds, smart watches, cordless tools, e-readers, almost any rechargeable gadget
  • Liquids: perfume, cologne, essential oils, hand sanitiser, nail polish and nail polish remover, ink, stain remover
  • Aerosols and sprays: hairspray, air freshener, varnish
  • Other: dry ice, certain temperature-controlled goods, compressed gas items

Correctly declared, those parcels now carry a 7.50 dollar handling fee on the air services. Incorrectly declared or unlabelled, the penalty is up to 50 dollars per parcel across all commercial services, with no exemption. At the same time, the dimensional weight divisor drops from 166 to 139 for anything over a cubic foot, and the sub-one-pound ounce pricing on the cheapest ground tier disappears entirely — which works out at roughly twelve percent more on the smallest parcels, or somewhere between seventy cents and two dollars each depending on distance.

Translated into shopping terms: two categories get more expensive to send inside the United States from tomorrow. Anything with a battery or a liquid in it, and anything that is large but light. Pillows, duvets, plush toys, pool floats, packing foam, lampshades, laundry baskets — the classic light-and-bulky set — all get repriced by the divisor change. Sellers absorb the first round of this and pass on the second, usually within three to five weeks.

3. The clearance clock nobody shows you turns over this month

Here is the most useful thing in this article, and it has nothing to do with football.

The largest marketplace charges sellers an aged-inventory surcharge on anything sitting in its warehouses. The surcharge now begins at 181 days. It escalates steeply, and at 365 days it can reach roughly thirteen times the ordinary monthly storage rate for the same item. On top of that, oversized goods that are not enrolled in the ship-in-own-packaging scheme now carry an extra packaging fee of between 1.51 and 4.04 dollars per unit, and standard fulfilment fees have crept up again — with the heaviest impact on home and outdoor categories, which is to say exactly the heavy, slow-turning things people buy in summer.

Work backwards from that and you get a genuinely predictive tool. Stock that arrived in a warehouse in January crosses the 181-day line in July. Stock that arrived in the autumn of 2025 is approaching the twelve-month cliff right now. A seller facing a thirteen-fold storage multiplier does not run a clever promotion. They dump.

Now stack the two timers. Tournament merchandise was manufactured and inbounded through January and February for a June kick-off. That inventory crosses 181 days in July and August. Its demand curve ends abruptly on 19 July. The seller is holding a product with no future demand and an escalating fee. There is exactly one thing they can do, and they will start doing it the week of 20 July.

4. Two marketplaces moved their fees in opposite directions on the same day

On 7 July, Russia’s largest marketplace raised seller commissions — five percentage points for merchants using platform warehouses, and up to twenty points for those shipping themselves. Growth across the top three Russian platforms has decelerated sharply, from fifty-six percent to thirty-two percent year on year, and in a decelerating market a commission rise goes almost entirely onto the shelf price. Expect around nine in ten listings there to reprice upward.

On the same day, in Bangkok, a new domestic Thai marketplace launched with the exact opposite proposition: zero commission for the first year, capped at five percent in year two, eight in year three and ten in year four, plus free delivery on the first hundred thousand orders and a free choice of courier. Fifteen thousand merchants signed up before launch. The pitch was blunt — it exists because sellers in that market have been paying platform fees that can reach half of their revenue once advertising, storage and returns are counted.

That number is the one to hold on to. The marketplace fee load is the invisible component of every price you see, and it is now large enough that entire national platforms are being founded specifically to undercut it. When a seller lists on a zero-commission channel and a forty-percent-load channel simultaneously, the price difference is not a deal. It is arithmetic.

Two refinements worth knowing.

Official versus lifestyle matters more than the result. After a shock exit, officially licensed match shirts lose value dramatically faster than softer replica or lifestyle pieces carrying the same colours. In one well-documented case, official shirts shed around sixty-three percent of resale value inside seventy-two hours while the lifestyle versions of the same collection lost about twenty-eight. If you are buying to wear rather than to commemorate, the lifestyle piece is the better purchase and always was.

The unlicensed flag tee is where the actual volume is. The clearest case this tournament involves a country making its first ever knockout appearance. Its official kit supplier saw shirt turnover jump roughly sixtyfold in a matter of days, sold out of the goalkeeper shirt entirely, and had to open a global pre-order. Meanwhile, on the largest marketplace, a plain 19.99 dollar flag jersey with no licensing at all shifted close to three thousand units in thirty days, worth about fifty-eight thousand dollars, and pushed into the top twenty of its novelty apparel subcategory. More than two thousand storefronts piled into flag shirts, scarves and stickers for that one country. The licensed product sold out. The unlicensed product printed money. That is the tournament merchandise business in one sentence.

How to read where a listing sits without any special tools: check the date first listed if the platform shows it, look at whether the review timestamps cluster in one season, and watch for the tell-tale switch from percentage-off promotions to outright list-price reductions. A seller running a fifteen percent coupon is optimising. A seller who has cut the list price is paying a surcharge.

Categories most exposed to this in the next six weeks: seasonal outdoor furniture, paddling pools and water toys, cooling gear, barbecues and outdoor cooking, anything tournament-branded, spring apparel, and oversized home goods caught by the new packaging fee. If you want any of those and can wait until the second half of August, wait.

The hazmat list: what to buy before Sunday

Not a panic list. A short, specific one. These are the things where the US postal change lands directly on the shelf price over the next month or so, in rough order of impact.

  1. Power banks and portable chargers. Lithium cells, awkward weight, high volume. Buy the one you were going to buy for August travel now.
  2. Perfume and cologne. Both a restricted liquid and a fragile shipping problem. Discounters that rely on the cheapest postal tier will move first.
  3. Nail polish, remover, and gel kits. Flammable liquids, small parcels, thin margins — the worst possible combination for a per-parcel fee.
  4. Hairspray, dry shampoo, aerosol deodorant. Same logic, plus these are high-repurchase, so a small increase compounds.
  5. Essential oils and diffuser refills. Small, light, liquid, frequently sold in multipacks that now each carry the handling fee.
  6. Cordless tools and spare battery packs. Heavy lithium, and the seasonal DIY window closes in September anyway.
  7. Wireless earbuds and smart watches. Less exposed because most ship through carriers rather than post, but the budget end of the market lives on postal tiers.
  8. Anything large and light. Duvets, pillows, plush toys, pool floats, storage bins, laundry hampers. These are hit by the dimensional divisor change rather than the hazmat rule, and the increase is a straight cost pass-through with no offsetting benefit.

The practical version of all this: if you have a saved cart with a fragrance, a battery or a bulky-but-light item sitting in it, today is a better day to check out than next month.

Platform and category watch

The largest marketplace. The story here is fees, not features. Aged-inventory surcharges from 181 days, escalating to roughly thirteen times monthly storage at a year. Oversize packaging surcharges of 1.51 to 4.04 dollars for sellers not enrolled in the own-packaging scheme. Standard fulfilment fees nudged up, with home and outdoor bearing the worst of it. Separately, from 24 August the seller agreement is being amended so that store accounts cannot be transferred or pledged as security without written permission. That sounds like pure back-office news, but the consumer consequence is real: it makes a marketplace storefront permanently tied to one registered entity. Over the next year that should slowly improve accountability on the platform, because the account can no longer be quietly sold on to somebody else after a bad year.

The short-video marketplace. Beauty continues to dominate outright. On the most recent weekly US chart, a single collagen eye-patch product from a Korean skincare brand topped the list with estimated turnover in the thirteen to sixteen million dollar range, and skincare took more than half the top ten. Around it: electrolyte drink powders, colour cosmetics, apparel, and — unusually — home cleaning tools, fitness gear, die-cast car models, portable pressure washers and waist trainers. In the UK the mix skews to women’s health gummies, multi-brand skincare bundles, baby appliances, ceiling fan lights, folding treadmills and lip products, at roughly a tenth of the US turnover. In Indonesia the top of the chart is baby gift sets and bundled kits, with agricultural film, turbo fans and spring mattresses selling steadily underneath.

The forward-looking piece: the platform’s own fulfilment arm is running a ten percent fulfilment-fee rebate on incremental volume through 30 September, has cut rates on sixteen to fifty pound items by up to five dollars a unit, and from this month is unlocking items over fifty pounds or twenty-six inches on a side. Furniture, large appliances and gym equipment are about to arrive on a platform that had effectively been closed to them. Early entrants into a newly-opened category are usually cheap. Expect genuinely aggressive pricing on bulky goods there through the autumn.

Independent stores and the low-price platforms. European small-parcel economics changed on 1 July and the effects are now visible on shelves. A three-euro charge applies per tariff classification per parcel, with a further two-euro clearance handling fee arriving in November, which puts five to eight euros of new cost on a directly-mailed small item. A nine-ninety-nine listing now bills over thirteen. Sellers across the European sites have either raised prices or delisted the unprofitable end of their range, and conversion on the cheapest links has roughly halved. The structural answer everyone has landed on is the same: hold stock inside the destination country. Outbound orders from overseas warehouses ran up about a hundred and ninety-three percent in the first half of this year, which tells you how fast that shift is happening.

For a shopper, this has one clean implication. The gap between the cheapest direct-mail price and the local-warehouse price has closed to almost nothing in Europe, while the delivery time gap is still five to fifteen days. Choose the local option; you are no longer paying a premium for it.

Sectors moving right now. Sportswear and fan apparel: at absolute peak, with a cliff on 20 July. Beauty and skincare: structurally the strongest global category, with Korean formulation brands taking the top slots in three separate markets. Health supplements: electrolytes and women’s health gummies both accelerating. Large home goods: about to get a new distribution channel. Small-parcel logistics: cost rising in the US, the EU and Russia simultaneously, for three unrelated reasons. Collectible IP: a Chinese designer-toy character appeared at the tournament opening ceremony, and that brand’s overseas store sales rose about two hundred and seventy percent month on month afterwards — a reminder that character licensing is currently outperforming most apparel.

One supply-side note for the next few weeks. A severe typhoon is due to make landfall on the Zhejiang and Fujian coast tonight and into tomorrow morning, and the two largest container ports serving that region are already restricting operations. Historically a disruption of that size shows up as a three to ten day delay on direct-from-Asia shipments about two to four weeks later — so late July and early August. If you have a direct-mail order you actually need by a date, order it this week rather than next.

Most likely to break out on Amazon, Shopify and TikTok Shop

Amazon. Unlicensed flag apparel is the sleeper. The licensing pipeline is too slow to react to a tournament in progress, so plain flag shirts, scarves and novelty tees for whichever countries reach the final will out-sell the official product by volume between now and 19 July. Behind that: portable projectors and outdoor screens for the semi-finals and final, countertop ice makers, insulated party coolers and wireless outdoor speakers — the entire watch-party set, all of which fall off a cliff on 20 July and are worth buying on the other side if you can wait a fortnight.

Shopify and independent stores. The winners here are the challenger kit brands. The clearest case of the tournament is a mid-sized sportswear label whose only sponsored nation reached the knockout stage for the first time and whose turnover jumped roughly sixtyfold on the back of it, with the goalkeeper shirt selling out worldwide and going to pre-order. That brand did not buy the exposure; it inherited it. Expect a wave of copycat direct-to-consumer kit launches through the autumn. Separately, independent European brands are quietly having a good quarter, because the two largest low-price platforms cut their European advertising budgets sharply this year and the auction those brands compete in got cheaper as a result.

TikTok Shop. Korean skincare, specifically the patch and ampoule formats, remains the most reliable breakout category and has been for months. Electrolyte and hydration powders are the strongest non-beauty line. Watch the new oversize category from this month for the genuinely novel opportunity — the first sub-500 dollar sofas, treadmills and full-size fridges on a short-video platform will be aggressively priced, because the platform is subsidising the category open.

Buying tips for this week

  1. Do not buy any tournament merchandise for a team still in it. Champion stock gets reordered rather than discounted, and stays at full price for six to nine months. If your team wins on 19 July, the shirt you want will be reprinted and available at the same price in September. There is no scarcity to beat.

  2. Do buy tournament merchandise for teams already out — but wait for the second markdown. The first cut lands within seventy-two hours of elimination and runs forty to fifty percent. The second cut lands after the final, when the whole category dies at once, and it is consistently deeper. The exception is your exact size in a popular shirt, which goes in the first wave.

  3. Buy the lifestyle version, not the official match shirt. After an exit, official licensed shirts lose value roughly twice as fast as the softer replica pieces in the same colours. If you intend to wear it rather than frame it, the replica is both cheaper and holds its value better.

  4. If you are in the United States and have a fragrance, a battery or a bulky-but-light item in your cart, check out today. The postal reclassification lands tomorrow, and the cost shows up on shelves in three to five weeks. This is a small saving executed reliably, which is the only kind worth acting on.

  5. Learn to read the storage clock before you wait for a sale. A seller running percentage-off coupons is optimising and will not go deeper. A seller who has cut the list price is paying an escalating storage surcharge and will go deeper. That single distinction is worth more than any deal alert.

  6. Buy the watch-party equipment before Tuesday or after the 20th, never in between. Projectors, ice makers, coolers and outdoor speakers are at their annual demand peak for eight more days and then fall off a cliff. The gap between those two prices is usually thirty to forty percent.

  7. In Europe, stop comparing direct-mail prices to local-warehouse prices. Since 1 July the per-parcel charges have closed the gap to almost nothing, while the delivery difference is still one to two weeks. The local option is now the default correct answer, not the premium one.

  8. Order anything coming direct from Asia this week rather than next. A severe typhoon is hitting the two biggest container ports serving eastern China tonight. It will not affect anything already in a local warehouse, but direct shipments placed in the next fortnight are the ones that will absorb the delay.

  9. Treat the new oversize listings on social commerce with curiosity, not caution. Furniture and large appliances arriving on a short-video platform for the first time will be subsidised into the category. The prices are real. The thing to verify is the returns path on a two-hundred-pound object, because that is where a new category is always weakest.

Bottom line

Three clocks are running and all three are readable.

The first is public and ends on 19 July. Every country eliminated between now and then converts a warehouse of licensed goods into dead stock inside seventy-two hours, and the discount curve that follows is so consistent you can plan around it. Buy nothing for a team still playing. Buy for teams already out, and prefer the second markdown to the first.

The second is administrative and starts tomorrow. Batteries, fragrances, aerosols and anything large-but-light gets more expensive to move around the United States, and that cost reaches shelves in three to five weeks. It is a small, boring, entirely predictable price rise, which makes it one of the easiest to sidestep — you just have to move before Sunday.

The third is invisible and is the most useful of the three. Storage fees on marketplace inventory now begin at 181 days and escalate to roughly thirteen times the base rate at a year. That means every listing has a hidden countdown attached, the seller knows exactly where they are on it, and their behaviour tells you too — coupons mean they are optimising, list-price cuts mean they are paying. Learn to see that and you stop chasing sales, because you can see the one that has to come.

Everything else this week is noise. A tournament ends in eight days, a fee schedule changes tomorrow, and a warehouse invoice comes due in August. Buy accordingly.
